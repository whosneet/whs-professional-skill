# Sector-Specific WHS Regimes — Australia & New Zealand

Several sectors operate under regulatory regimes that sit outside or alongside the
general WHS Act / HSWA. Where these regimes apply, generic WHS analysis is
insufficient — the sector-specific Act, Regulations, statutory roles, and
regulator must be considered first. This file covers the most material
sector-specific regimes in AU and NZ and the practical implications for
practitioners working across or into those sectors.

Use this file alongside `legislation.md` (which carries the general WHS statutory
provisions) and `hazards.md` (which covers operational hazard control) when the
task involves a sector with its own regulatory framework.

## 1. Scope and Regime Selection

### Why sector-specific regimes exist
General WHS legislation is structured around a duty-of-care framework that works
across industries — PCBU primary duty, hierarchy of controls, SFAIRP, hazard
identification and risk management. It is deliberately principles-based.

Sector-specific regimes exist where:

- The hazard profile is sufficiently distinctive (underground gas, aviation
  airworthiness, marine vessel stability, biosafety containment) that prescriptive
  rules are warranted alongside general duties
- Catastrophic-event history has driven legislative response (Pike River for NZ
  mining; Macondo for offshore petroleum; Moura, Box Flat, Appin for AU coal
  mining; Westray for Canadian mining)
- International conventions impose obligations that must be domestically enacted
  (SOLAS, ICAO Annex 19, IMO conventions)
- Inspector competency requires specialist technical knowledge that general WHS
  inspectorates cannot maintain (longwall geotechnics, aircraft maintenance,
  rail signalling, nuclear regulation)
- Industry funding models are baked into the inspectorate (industry-funded
  safety regulators, e.g. RSHQ in QLD coal mining)

### Regime hierarchy — overlay vs replacement
Sector-specific regimes operate in one of three modes relative to the general
WHS Act:

| Mode | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Overlay** | Sector Act/Regulation applies in addition to the general WHS Act. Both apply concurrently; PCBU has duties under both | NSW mining (WHS Act 2011 + WHS(MPS) Act 2013); AU rail (RSNL + state WHS Act); aviation airside (CASR + WHS Act) |
| **Replacement** | Sector regime substantially displaces the general WHS Act for in-scope work; the WHS Act may still apply to support activities | QLD coal and minerals mining (CMSHA / MQSHA replace WHS Act for in-mine activities); Commonwealth maritime under OPGGS Act for offshore petroleum |
| **Carve-out** | Specific activities or workforces are excluded from general WHS coverage and regulated separately | Defence Force members under DFDA for service-specific matters; aircraft in flight under CASR |

### How to determine which regime applies
Sequential test for any WHS task involving a sector-specific industry:

1. **Identify the workplace** — what is the physical location, vessel, aircraft,
   facility, mine?
2. **Identify the activity** — what work is being performed? Is it operational
   (in-scope of sector regime) or ancillary (administrative, catering,
   maintenance — may fall under general WHS Act only)?
3. **Identify the duty holders** — who is the PCBU, mine operator, RTO, AOC
   holder, vessel operator? Multiple parties typically have concurrent duties.
4. **Identify the jurisdiction** — state, Commonwealth, or international waters /
   airspace?
5. **Identify the regulator** — which inspectorate has primary jurisdiction?
   Where multiple regulators have jurisdiction, which leads investigation?

### Common multi-regime situations
| Scenario | Regimes that may apply |
|---|---|
| Port worker loading a vessel | WHS Act (port workplace) + Navigation Act 2012 (vessel) + state maritime safety regs |
| Helicopter pilot landing offshore | CASR (aircraft + crew) + OPGGS Act 2006 (offshore facility) + NOPSEMA jurisdiction at the facility |
| Rail worker on a coal mine spur line | RSNL (rail safety) + state mining Act (mine site) + WHS Act (workplace) |
| Aircraft engineer on a hangar floor | WHS Act (workplace) + CASR Part 145 (maintenance organisation) |
| Defence civilian on a base | Commonwealth WHS Act 2011 + Defence Safety Management System + Defence Act provisions |
| Cruise vessel in a NZ port | Maritime Transport Act 1994 (vessel) + HSWA 2015 (port workplace) + IMO conventions |
| Underground coal miner | State Coal Mining Safety Act + general state WHS Act (for ancillary activities) |

**Practical implications**:
- Never assume the WHS Act is the only applicable instrument when working in
  these sectors
- When multiple regimes apply, identify the more specific instrument for the
  specific hazard and apply both
- Concurrent duty under multiple regimes is the norm, not the exception — the
  3Cs framework (consultation, cooperation, coordination) under WHS Act s 46
  applies between PCBUs even where different statutory regimes govern each
- Investigation jurisdiction is often shared — a single event can trigger
  parallel WHS regulator and sector regulator investigations, with separate
  evidence collection, witness interviews, and enforcement decisions

> sector-specific regime, load `references/legislation.md`. For organisation-
> specific sector context and accreditations, load `references/company.md`.

## 2. Mining

Mining is the most material sector with separate regulatory regimes. Every
mining-active state and territory operates a distinct framework, and there is
no national mining safety law. The dual-regulation model — sector-specific
Act plus general WHS Act — applies in most jurisdictions.

### 2.1 NSW — Mines and Petroleum Sites

**Primary instruments:**
- Work Health and Safety (Mines and Petroleum Sites) Act 2013 (NSW)
- Work Health and Safety (Mines and Petroleum Sites) Regulation 2014 (NSW)
- Coal Mines Regulation 2014 (NSW) — additional coal-specific provisions
- General WHS Act 2011 (NSW) applies concurrently

**Regulator**: NSW Resources Regulator, within the Department of Regional NSW.
Operates a specialist mining inspectorate separate from SafeWork NSW.

**Statutory roles** (WHS(MPS) Act 2013 + Regulation 2014):

| Role | Function |
|---|---|
| Mine Operator | The PCBU with overall management or control of the mine; equivalent to PCBU under general WHS Act but with mine-specific duties |
| Site Senior Executive (SSE) | Single person accountable for management of WHS at the mine; cannot be more than one SSE per mine at any time |
| Statutory Function holders | Specific competency-based roles — Open Cut Examiner, Underground Mine Manager, Ventilation Officer, Electrical Engineer, Mechanical Engineer, Surveyor |
| Operator of Petroleum Site | Equivalent role for petroleum sites |

**Operational principles:**
- Dual regulation — the general WHS Act 2011 (NSW) applies in addition to
  WHS(MPS) Act, not in substitution
- Mine operator must prepare and maintain a written Safety Management System
  (SMS) covering principal mining hazards
- Notifiable incidents under WHS(MPS) Reg cl 179–180 are broader than general
  WHS Act s 35 — additional triggers include strata failure, fires underground,
  inrushes, gas outbursts, electrical incidents underground
- Inspector powers expanded — Resources Regulator inspectors can suspend
  operations on safety grounds; mine record book entries are statutory documents

**Principal mining hazards** (WHS(MPS) Reg Schedule 1):
- Strata failure (roof fall, rib failure, floor heave)
- Ground or strata gas (methane, CO, CO2)
- Inundation or inrush (water, mud, gas)
- Mine shaft conveyance
- Explosives
- Spontaneous combustion
- Air quality (dust, gases, vapours)
- Mine fires and explosions
- Electricity
- Subsidence

Each principal hazard requires a written Principal Hazard Management Plan with
risk assessment, control measures, monitoring, and emergency response.

**High-risk activities** (WHS(MPS) Reg cl 22):
- Longwall mining
- Secondary support installation
- Shaft sinking and shaft repair
- Working in unsupported ground
- Diesel engine operation underground (over defined power thresholds)

**Practical implications:**
- A general WHS consultant cannot advise on NSW mines without working knowledge
  of the WHS(MPS) framework — statutory role accountability is fundamentally
  different
- Mine record book is a legally significant document; entries are admissible
  and influence prosecution
- Resources Regulator publishes weekly safety bulletins, fortnightly significant
  incident reports — track these for situational awareness

### 2.2 QLD — Coal and Minerals Mining

**Primary instruments:**
- Coal Mining Safety and Health Act 1999 (QLD) + Coal Mining Safety and Health
  Regulation 2017 (QLD) — coal mining
- Mining and Quarrying Safety and Health Act 1999 (QLD) + Mining and Quarrying
  Safety and Health Regulation 2017 (QLD) — minerals, quarries, opal, exploration
- Petroleum and Gas (Production and Safety) Act 2004 (QLD) — petroleum and gas
- General WHS Act 2011 (QLD) **does not apply** to operational activities at
  coal mines, minerals mines, or quarries — it is substantially displaced

**Regulator**: Resources Safety and Health Queensland (RSHQ). Established as an
independent statutory body in **2020** following the Brady Review (2019), which
identified systemic regulatory weakness in the prior arrangement under DNRME.
RSHQ is industry-funded through the Mine Workers' Health Scheme levy.

**Statutory positions** (CMSHA 1999 + Regulation 2017):

| Position | Coal mines | Minerals mines / quarries |
|---|---|---|
| Site Senior Executive (SSE) | Required; single most senior accountability | Required (MQSHA) |
| Underground Mine Manager (UMM) | Required for underground coal mines; statutory competency | Required for underground mines |
| Open Cut Examiner (OCE) | Required for open cut coal mines | — |
| Ventilation Officer | Required for underground coal | Required for underground |
| Electrical Engineering Manager (EEM) | Required | Required where applicable |
| Mechanical Engineering Manager (MEM) | Required | Required where applicable |
| Underground Mine Worker (UMW) competency | Required for all underground workers | — |
| Industry Safety and Health Representative (ISHR) | Worker-elected; statutory function under CMSHA Part 7 | Equivalent under MQSHA |

**Operational principles:**
- CMSHA and MQSHA operate as substantially complete regimes — the general WHS
  Act is not the operative framework for in-mine activities
- Site Senior Executive is the single accountable person — comparable in
  structure to but distinct from the PCBU concept
- High-Potential Incident (HPI) definitions and reporting are specified in the
  Acts; failure to report is an offence
- Major incident review boards — Boards of Inquiry can be appointed for
  catastrophic events (Moura No.2 Royal Commission 1994, Grosvenor Board of
  Inquiry 2020 following the 6 May 2020 methane explosion)

**Industry-funded inspectorate** — RSHQ is funded by the coal and metalliferous
mining industries through a statutory levy, not consolidated revenue. This was
established to address the chronic under-resourcing identified by the Brady
Review. Inspector numbers, competency, and technical capacity exceed what
general WHS regulators provide.

**Brady Review (2019)** — Dr Sean Brady's review of QLD mining safety
performance (2000–2019) following multiple fatalities. Key findings:
- Fatality rate had not improved meaningfully over the review period
- Regulatory and operator focus on lagging indicators had crowded out attention
  to high-potential incidents and systemic risk
- Drift toward "normal" practices that no longer aligned with original risk
  controls
- The Review's recommendations drove the establishment of RSHQ as an
  independent industry-funded regulator and a renewed focus on HPI intelligence

**Practical implications:**
- A WHS Act prosecution cannot proceed for in-mine activity in QLD — CMSHA /
  MQSHA prosecutions are the available pathway
- HPI reporting is statutory and time-bound; under-reporting is a discrete
  offence; the HPI dataset informs both regulator focus and industry-wide
  intelligence
- Inspector engagement is technically sophisticated — practitioners should
  expect inspectors to interrogate ventilation calculations, strata management
  plans, electrical protection systems, and ground control at engineering depth

### 2.3 WA — Mines

**Primary instruments:**
- Work Health and Safety (Mines) Regulations 2022 (WA) — made under the general
  WHS Act 2020 (WA)
- Work Health and Safety Act 2020 (WA) — commenced 31 March 2022

**Historical context**: Until 31 March 2022, WA mines operated under the
Mines Safety and Inspection Act 1994 (MSIA) and the Mines Safety and
Inspection Regulations 1995. These were **repealed** when WA harmonised with
the national WHS framework. Mining-specific provisions were retained as a
dedicated set of regulations (WHS(Mines) Regulations 2022) sitting under
the general WHS Act 2020.

**Regulator**: Department of Energy, Mines, Industry Regulation and Safety
(DMIRS). The Mines Safety inspectorate (formerly Resources Safety) sits within
DMIRS but operates with specialist technical capability.

**Transition period implications:**
- Practitioners with experience under MSIA must reorient — terminology,
  duty structure, and notification frameworks have changed substantially
- Statutory positions retained: Registered Manager, Quarry Manager,
  Underground Manager, Ventilation Officer — with redefined accountabilities
  under the new framework
- Notifiable mining incidents are now defined under WHS(Mines) Reg + general
  WHS Reg Part 12 — broader than the MSIA reportable incidents list
- High-risk activities (longwall, secondary support, shaft work) retain
  specific regulatory treatment under WHS(Mines) Reg

**Practical implications:**
- Reference materials, SOPs, and risk registers prepared under MSIA require
  review against WHS(Mines) Regulations 2022 — drift between old and new
  frameworks is a known compliance gap
- The harmonisation does not import QLD's industry-funded model — DMIRS is
  funded through consolidated revenue plus mining safety levy

### 2.4 SA — Mines

**Primary instruments:**
- Mines and Works Inspection Act 1920 (SA) — still in force; the oldest mining
  safety Act in AU
- WHS Act 2012 (SA) — applies to mining workplaces in addition to the 1920 Act
- Petroleum and Geothermal Energy Act 2000 (SA)
- Radiation Protection and Control Act 2021 (SA) — applies to uranium operations

**Regulator**: Department for Energy and Mining; supported by SafeWork SA for
general WHS matters.

**Smaller mining footprint** — SA's mining activity is smaller than NSW, QLD,
or WA. The regulatory framework is correspondingly less elaborate, but the
fundamental dual-regulation model applies.

**Notable**: SA hosts unique mining workplaces including Olympic Dam
(BHP — uranium, copper, gold) and Roxby Downs — these involve concurrent
WHS, radiation protection, and uranium-specific regulatory regimes.

### 2.5 NT, TAS, VIC — Mining

**NT**: Mining Management Act 2001 (NT) + WHS (National Uniform Legislation)
Act 2011 (NT). Regulator: Department of Industry, Tourism and Trade.

**TAS**: Mines Work Health and Safety (Supplementary Requirements) Act 2012 +
WHS Act 2012 (Tas). Regulator: WorkSafe Tasmania.

**VIC**: Mineral Resources (Sustainable Development) Act 1990 (Vic) + OHS Act
2004. Regulator: Earth Resources Regulation within DJSIR. VIC's mining
footprint is smaller; the OHS Act framework provides the operational WHS basis.

### 2.6 Cross-jurisdiction mining principles

Despite jurisdictional differences, mining safety regimes share structural
features:

| Principle | Application |
|---|---|
| **Statutory positions** | Single accountable senior executive (SSE / Registered Manager); statutory technical functions (UMM, OCE, Ventilation Officer); these are not delegable in the same way as general management roles |
| **Principal Hazard Management Plans** | Written plans for defined principal hazards — strata, gas, inrush, fire, explosives, electricity — with risk assessment, controls, monitoring, emergency response |
| **High-Potential Incident reporting** | Discrete reporting framework distinct from general WHS notifiable incidents; HPI dataset is a key safety performance indicator and regulatory focus |
| **Mine record book** | Statutory document recording inspections, examinations, incidents, and management decisions; admissible in court; entries must be made by appropriate statutory function holders |
| **Tailings dam regulation** | Significantly strengthened post-Brumadinho (Brazil, 2019) and Mount Polley (Canada, 2014); Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management (GISTM) adopted by major operators; AU jurisdictions tightening regulatory oversight |
| **Ventilation and gas management** | Underground coal mines must monitor methane, CO, CO2, O2 continuously; ventilation officer is a statutory role; gas drainage and dilution are engineering disciplines, not administrative controls |
| **Industry-funded inspectorate** | QLD (RSHQ) is industry-funded; other jurisdictions partially funded through mining safety levies; this affects inspector numbers, technical capacity, and independence |

**Cross-reference**: For respirable crystalline silica (relevant to all mining
sectors, particularly tunnelling, drill-and-blast, and processing), load
`references/hazards.md` §2.

## 3. Maritime

Maritime work covers vessels at sea, in port, and offshore. The regulatory
framework is fragmented across Commonwealth, state, and international layers,
with substantial complexity at the vessel/shore-side interface.

### 3.1 AMSA — Australian Maritime Safety Authority

**Primary instruments:**
- Navigation Act 2012 (Cth) — primary maritime safety law for Australian
  regulated vessels and seafarers
- Marine Safety (Domestic Commercial Vessel) National Law Act 2012 (Cth) —
  national system for domestic commercial vessels (transitioning from
  state-based regulation)
- Marine Orders (made under the Navigation Act) — operational requirements
  for vessel construction, equipment, crewing, safety management

**Regulator**: Australian Maritime Safety Authority (AMSA) — Commonwealth
statutory authority, established 1990.

**Scope**: Regulates safety of seafarers on Australian-flagged regulated
vessels in international and domestic operations, plus all domestic commercial
vessels under the national system. Also responsible for vessel survey,
seafarer certification, marine pollution response, and search and rescue.

**Key Marine Orders**:

| Order | Subject |
|---|---|
| Marine Order 11 | Living and working conditions on vessels (Maritime Labour Convention 2006 implementation) |
| Marine Order 12 | Construction subdivision and stability, machinery and electrical installations |
| Marine Order 21 | Safety of navigation and emergency procedures |
| Marine Order 28 | Crew lists, off-signing and accident reporting |
| Marine Order 32 | Cargo handling equipment |
| Marine Order 41 | Carriage of dangerous goods |
| Marine Order 57 | Helicopter operations |

**Maritime Labour Convention 2006 (MLC)** — implemented through Marine Order 11.
Sets minimum living and working conditions for seafarers including hours of
work and rest, accommodation, medical care, repatriation rights. Port State
Control inspections verify compliance for vessels in AU ports.

### 3.2 OPGGS Act and NOPSEMA — Offshore Petroleum

**Primary instruments:**
- Offshore Petroleum and Greenhouse Gas Storage Act 2006 (Cth) (OPGGS Act)
- Offshore Petroleum and Greenhouse Gas Storage (Safety) Regulations 2009
- Offshore Petroleum and Greenhouse Gas Storage (Environment) Regulations 2009

**Regulator**: National Offshore Petroleum Safety and Environmental Management
Authority (NOPSEMA). Established **1 January 2012**, replacing NOPSA, in
response to the Montara incident (West Atlas blowout, Timor Sea, 21 August
2009) and the Macondo / Deepwater Horizon disaster (Gulf of Mexico, 20 April
2010). NOPSEMA absorbed environmental approval and management functions from
the Department of Industry to become the single offshore petroleum regulator.

**Scope**: Regulates occupational health and safety, well integrity, and
environmental management of offshore petroleum operations in
Commonwealth waters (generally beyond 3 nautical miles from coastal baseline).
State waters are regulated by state authorities (e.g., NOPTA for resource
title management).

**Safety Case regime** — operators of offshore facilities must prepare and
submit a written Safety Case to NOPSEMA demonstrating that risks to workers
and the facility have been managed to as low as reasonably practicable (ALARP).
The Safety Case must:
- Identify all major accident events
- Describe the formal safety assessment
- Describe the safety management system
- Demonstrate ALARP achievement
- Be reviewed and revised on significant change

**Well Operations Management Plan (WOMP)** — separate document covering well
design, drilling, completion, intervention, and abandonment.

**Notable post-Macondo changes:**
- Mandatory third-party verification of safety-critical equipment
- Strengthened well integrity requirements (barrier philosophy)
- Enhanced operator competency requirements
- Worker representation framework (Occupational Health and Safety Member —
  OHSM equivalent of HSR)

### 3.3 State maritime regulators — domestic commercial vessels

Historically, each state regulated domestic commercial vessels (DCVs) operating
in state waters. Since 1 July 2013, the national system administered by AMSA
has progressively replaced state systems. State maritime authorities retain
some functions during transition and for specific matters (recreational
vessels, some state-flagged operations):

| State | Authority | Residual scope |
|---|---|---|
| NSW | Transport for NSW (Roads and Maritime) | Recreational vessels, harbour pilotage |
| VIC | Transport Safety Victoria (Maritime Safety Victoria) | Recreational vessels, port safety |
| QLD | Maritime Safety Queensland (Department of Transport) | Recreational vessels, ship-sourced pollution in state waters |
| WA | Department of Transport (Maritime Division) | Recreational vessels, harbour management |
| SA | Department for Infrastructure and Transport (Marine Safety) | Recreational vessels |
| TAS | Marine and Safety Tasmania (MAST) | Recreational vessels, some commercial |
| NT | NT WorkSafe / Department of Infrastructure | Limited commercial scope |

### 3.4 Maritime NZ

**Primary instruments:**
- Maritime Transport Act 1994 (NZ)
- Maritime Rules (made under MTA — equivalent to Marine Orders in AU)

**Regulator**: Maritime New Zealand (Maritime NZ) — Crown entity established
1993 (formerly Maritime Safety Authority).

**Scope**: Regulates the safety, security, marine protection, and
environmental performance of vessels in NZ waters and NZ-flagged vessels
internationally. Operates Maritime Operator Safety System (MOSS) — vessel
operator certification framework — and SeaCert (seafarer certification).

**HSWA intersection**: Maritime Transport Act applies to vessels and seafarers
in maritime operations. HSWA 2015 applies to:
- Port and shore-side workplaces
- Loading and unloading operations at port (concurrent with maritime rules)
- Non-maritime aspects of fishing operations (e.g., onshore processing)

The Whakaari / White Island prosecution (cf. `legislation.md` §3.6) is the
landmark NZ case on overlap — WorkSafe NZ prosecuted under HSWA, not under
the Maritime Transport Act.

### 3.5 HSWA / WHS Act intersection at port

The port environment is the highest-frequency multi-regime workplace in
maritime operations. A single port can host:

- Vessel crew under Navigation Act / Maritime Transport Act
- Stevedores under WHS Act / HSWA
- Port authority workers under WHS Act / HSWA
- Customs / Border Force / Biosecurity officers under Commonwealth regimes
- Truck drivers under WHS Act + HVNL
- Pilot launch crews under maritime rules
- Shipping agents and surveyors under WHS Act / HSWA

**Vessel as workplace** — the vessel is a workplace under WHS Act / HSWA
when in port. Concurrent duties apply: vessel master / operator under maritime
law; PCBU under WHS Act / HSWA for work performed on the vessel by shore-side
workers. The 3Cs duty under WHS Act s 46 applies between these duty holders.

**Stevedoring duties** — Stevedoring Industry Levy Collection Act 1977 (Cth)
funded the Maritime Industry Finance Company; current arrangements involve
Stevedores Association and individual operator licensing under state and
Commonwealth frameworks.

### 3.6 Key maritime hazards

| Hazard | Sector context | Cross-reference |
|---|---|---|
| **Confined space** | Tanks, holds, cofferdams, void spaces — high frequency on ships. Atmosphere can develop oxygen deficiency rapidly from cargo offgassing (grain, coal, timber, fish, scrap metal). Marine Order 21 sets confined space requirements | `hazards.md` §11 |
| **Working at height** | Mast, deck cranes, hatch covers, container stacks. Falls from height are a leading cause of vessel fatality. Restraint preferred; rescue planning critical at sea | `hazards.md` §9 |
| **Lifting and rigging** | Vessel cranes, container handling, cargo gear. Inspection regimes under Marine Order 32; load testing per AS 1418 series and ISO 4309 (wire rope) | `hazards.md` §12, §16 |
| **Fatigue** | Hours of work and rest under MLC 2006 / Marine Order 11 — minimum 10 hours rest in 24 hours and 77 hours in 7 days. Heavy Vehicle National Law (HVNL) equivalent does not apply at sea; Marine Order 28 governs reporting | `hazards.md` §18 |
| **Helicopter transfers offshore** | Marine Order 57; HUET (Helicopter Underwater Escape Training) required for offshore personnel; survival suits, EBS (Emergency Breathing System) mandatory in many operations |  |
| **Engine room** | Noise (often exceeds 90 dB(A)); heat; rotating machinery; fuel and oil systems; CO2 fixed fire suppression hazard during release | `hazards.md` §14, §6 |
| **Mooring operations** | Mooring line failure — high-energy snap-back; AMSA has issued multiple safety alerts following fatalities; mooring SWMS and line condition monitoring essential |  |

**Practical implications:**
- Maritime work requires familiarity with the specific Marine Order or
  Maritime Rule covering the activity — general WHS analysis is insufficient
  for the vessel side
- Port-side / shore-side WHS work proceeds under WHS Act / HSWA but must
  recognise vessel jurisdiction and coordinate access
- Offshore petroleum work is NOPSEMA-regulated under a Safety Case regime
  fundamentally different from prescriptive WHS — Safety Case competency is a
  specialist discipline

## 4. Aviation

Aviation safety is regulated at the Commonwealth level in both AU and NZ
through specialist regulators. The general WHS Act / HSWA applies to airside
workplaces (ramp, MRO, hangar, terminal) but does not displace aviation safety
regulation.

### 4.1 CASA — Civil Aviation Safety Authority

**Primary instruments:**
- Civil Aviation Act 1988 (Cth)
- Civil Aviation Regulations 1988 (CAR) — older regulatory framework, largely
  superseded by CASR
- Civil Aviation Safety Regulations 1998 (CASR) — primary modern regulatory
  instrument
- Civil Aviation Orders (CAOs) — operational orders made under the Act
- Manual of Standards (MOS) for specific CASR Parts

**Regulator**: Civil Aviation Safety Authority (CASA) — Commonwealth statutory
authority, established 1995 following separation from the Civil Aviation
Authority (which had combined regulatory and service provision functions).

**Key CASR Parts**:

| Part | Subject |
|---|---|
| Part 21 | Certification and airworthiness requirements for aircraft and parts |
| Part 61 | Flight crew licensing |
| Part 66 | Aircraft maintenance personnel licensing |
| Part 119 | Air operator certification — general requirements |
| Part 121 | Air transport operations — large aeroplanes |
| Part 133 | Air transport operations — rotorcraft |
| Part 135 | Air transport operations — small aeroplanes |
| Part 138 | Aerial work operations |
| Part 139 | Aerodromes |
| Part 142 | Flight training |
| Part 145 | Approved maintenance organisations |
| Part 147 | Aircraft maintenance training organisations |
| Part 175 | Aeronautical information service |

**Air Operator's Certificate (AOC)** — the foundational permission for
commercial air transport. Holders must operate under an approved Operations
Manual and a Safety Management System (SMS) compliant with CASR Part 119.

### 4.2 ATSB — Australian Transport Safety Bureau

**Primary instruments:**
- Transport Safety Investigation Act 2003 (Cth)
- Transport Safety Investigation Regulations 2003

**Regulator**: Australian Transport Safety Bureau (ATSB) — Commonwealth
statutory agency. Investigates aviation, rail, and marine transport accidents
and serious incidents.

**Critical distinction — ATSB vs CASA:**
- **ATSB** conducts no-blame safety investigations under TSI Act to identify
  contributing factors and improve safety. Investigation reports are explicitly
  not for the purpose of attributing blame or determining liability
  (TSI Act s 7).
- **CASA** conducts regulatory enforcement — license suspension or cancellation,
  prosecution, civil aviation order action. Enforcement uses CASA's own
  investigative powers under the Civil Aviation Act.

**The no-blame culture** — TSI Act s 60 provides protection for restricted
information (e.g., voluntary safety reports, cockpit voice recordings) from
admission in legal proceedings, with limited exceptions. This is the
foundation of the just-culture model in aviation safety investigation. The
parallel CASA enforcement track means a single event can have an ATSB
no-blame investigation and a CASA enforcement action running concurrently —
information barriers between the two are statutory.

**International framework** — ATSB investigations conform to ICAO Annex 13
(Aircraft Accident and Incident Investigation), which applies internationally
and creates expectations for cooperation between national investigation
bodies.

### 4.3 CAA NZ — Civil Aviation Authority New Zealand

**Primary instruments:**
- Civil Aviation Act 1990 (NZ) — replaced by Civil Aviation Act 2023 (NZ),
  in force from 5 April 2025
- Civil Aviation Rules (Part 100-series)

**Regulator**: Civil Aviation Authority of New Zealand (CAA NZ) — Crown entity.

**Key Civil Aviation Rule Parts**:

| Part | Subject |
|---|---|
| Part 91 | General operating and flight rules |
| Part 115 | Adventure aviation operations |
| Part 119 | Air operator certification — general |
| Part 121 | Air operations — large aeroplanes |
| Part 125 | Air operations — medium aeroplanes |
| Part 135 | Air operations — helicopters and small aeroplanes |
| Part 145 | Aircraft maintenance organisations |
| Part 147 | Maintenance training organisations |
| Part 172 | Air traffic service organisations |

**TAIC** — Transport Accident Investigation Commission (NZ) — independent
Crown entity equivalent to AU ATSB. Investigates aviation, rail, and marine
accidents under the Transport Accident Investigation Commission Act 1990.
Same no-blame investigation principle as ATSB.

### 4.4 HSWA / WHS Act intersection in aviation

| Workplace | Applicable framework |
|---|---|
| Aircraft in flight | CASR (AU) / CARs (NZ) — exclusive |
| Cockpit / flight deck (in flight) | CASR — fatigue rules, crew rest, operational procedures |
| Cabin (in flight) | CASR — cabin crew procedures, in-flight emergencies |
| Airside ramp | WHS Act / HSWA — concurrent with CASR Part 139 aerodrome requirements |
| MRO hangar | WHS Act / HSWA — concurrent with CASR Part 145 maintenance organisation requirements |
| Ground handling | WHS Act / HSWA — concurrent with CASR for aircraft handling activities |
| Air traffic control facility | WHS Act / HSWA — facility; aviation safety functions under CASR / CARs |
| Aerodrome landside / terminal | WHS Act / HSWA — primary |

The flight deck and cabin in flight are governed by aviation safety rules
because the operational context (aerodynamics, fatigue rules adapted to
flight, emergency procedures) is specifically calibrated to in-flight work.
Airside workplaces are governed by general WHS frameworks because the work
is ground-based and the WHS hazard set (manual handling, mobile plant,
hazardous substances) is the general workplace set.

### 4.5 Key aviation hazards

| Hazard | Sector context |
|---|---|
| **Ramp pedestrian/aircraft interface** | Aircraft taxi, push-back, towing, marshalling. High-energy environment with multiple moving vehicles. Standardised hand signals (ICAO Annex 2) and high-vis clothing required. Multiple fatalities from jet blast, prop wash, and being struck by aircraft or ground support equipment |
| **Foreign object debris (FOD)** | Anything on the apron, taxiway, or runway that can damage aircraft engines or systems — bolts, tools, plastic, gravel. FOD walk-downs at shift start; tool control; FOD damage can ground aircraft and kill engines on takeoff (CASR Part 139 + ICAO Annex 14) |
| **Hot work in fuelled aircraft** | Welding, grinding, or any spark-generating work in or near a fuelled aircraft is high-consequence. Defuelling and inerting may be required; permit-to-work systems mandatory; specific MRO procedures |
| **Hypoxia for high-altitude maintenance** | Cabin pressurisation testing, work on aircraft at altitude on test flights, work in pressure chambers. Hypoxia onset is insidious — supplemental oxygen and time-of-useful-consciousness awareness required |
| **Fatigue** | Fatigue Risk Management System (FRMS) is mandated for AOC holders under CASR Part 119 / 121. Flight time limitations, duty period limits, rest period requirements; international long-haul operations particularly complex |
| **Chemical exposure** | Avionics solvents, hydraulic fluid (Skydrol — irritant), de-icing fluids, fuel vapours, composite materials (carbon fibre dust during repair) |
| **Lasers** | Inadvertent laser strikes from ground are a hazard to flight crew (CASA has prosecution powers under Criminal Code); industrial lasers in MRO have own controls |
| **Lithium batteries** | Cargo lithium battery fires — regulated under IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations and ICAO Technical Instructions; aircraft cargo fires from lithium have caused multiple hull losses |

**Practical implications:**
- Airside workplace WHS proceeds under the WHS Act / HSWA — but coordination
  with CASR-regulated operator (the airline) is required
- MRO organisations have parallel CASA approval (Part 145) and WHS Act / HSWA
  obligations — Safety Management System under Part 145 typically integrates
  WHS, airworthiness, and flight safety in one framework
- A general WHS consultant working at an airport must understand the air
  operator's SMS and the aerodrome operator's safety management plan

## 5. Rail

Rail safety is regulated under a national law administered by a national
regulator. Unlike mining, rail has achieved cross-jurisdictional harmonisation.

### 5.1 ONRSR — Office of National Rail Safety Regulator

**Primary instruments:**
- Rail Safety National Law (RSNL) — enacted in each jurisdiction; applies
  uniformly across all AU states and territories
- Rail Safety National Law National Regulations 2012 — single set of
  regulations adopted in each jurisdiction
- Approved Codes of Practice (ACOPs) issued by ONRSR

**Regulator**: Office of the National Rail Safety Regulator (ONRSR).
Established 20 January 2013 under an intergovernmental agreement; took on
state regulatory functions progressively through 2013–2017. Headquartered in
Adelaide; offices in all states and territories.

**Jurisdictional structure** — RSNL is host-jurisdiction legislation:
- SA enacted the original Rail Safety National Law (South Australia) Act 2012
- Each other jurisdiction enacted parallel Acts adopting the SA law as RSNL
- ONRSR administers in each jurisdiction under intergovernmental agreement
- Single regulator + uniform law = practical national regulation

### 5.2 Rail Transport Operator (RTO) accreditation

**Two types of accreditation** (RSNL s 62):
- **Accredited Rail Transport Operator** — operator of rolling stock or rail
  infrastructure; full accreditation
- **Registered Rail Transport Operator** — limited operations (private sidings,
  some heritage railways); reduced obligations

**Accreditation requirements** include:
- Demonstrated competency to manage rail safety
- Safety Management System compliant with RSNL Part 3 Division 6
- Adequate resources and insurance
- Notification of significant changes

**RTO duties** (RSNL s 52–61):
- Ensure SFAIRP the safety of railway operations
- Implement and maintain Safety Management System
- Consult with workers, contractors, affected parties
- Notify and report notifiable occurrences
- Conduct drug and alcohol testing (mandatory under RSNL Part 3 Division 9)
- Manage fatigue (RSNL Part 3 Division 8)
- Maintain interfaces with other RTOs and rail infrastructure managers

### 5.3 Safety Management System

The Safety Management System (SMS) is mandated for all RTOs (RSNL s 99). It
must address (per the Regulation 2012):

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Safety policy | Statement of safety commitment |
| Risk management | Hazard identification, risk assessment, controls |
| Safety performance measures | Leading and lagging indicators; targets |
| Safety performance monitoring, evaluation, improvement | Internal audit, review, continuous improvement |
| Resource availability | Personnel, equipment, infrastructure for safety |
| Operational systems | Operational rules, procedures, communications |
| Notifications | Occurrence notification framework |
| Procurement and contractor management | Safety due diligence on procurement and contractors |
| Training and competency | Training programs; competency assessment; records |
| Records | Document control; retention; accessibility |
| Internal communications | Safety information flow within the organisation |
| Health and fitness for work | Medical fitness; sleep disorders; mental health |
| Drug and alcohol | Testing program; rehabilitation; investigation |
| Fatigue | Risk management; rostering; monitoring |
| Emergency management | Plans; coordination with emergency services |
| Engineering and operational systems safety | Design verification; commissioning; change management |

### 5.4 Drug and alcohol — mandatory testing

RSNL Part 3 Division 9 mandates D&A testing for **safety-critical workers**
(defined per RTO's SMS, but typically includes drivers, signallers, network
controllers, track workers, shunters, and supervisors). Testing categories:

- **Pre-employment** — required
- **Random** — required
- **For-cause / reasonable suspicion** — required
- **Post-incident** — required (mandatory after notifiable occurrences)
- **Post-treatment / return-to-work** — required

Cut-offs and procedures must align with AS/NZS 4308 (urine) or AS/NZS 4760
(oral fluid). Industry-wide consistency on cut-off levels and procedures
through ONRSR guidance.

**Cross-reference**: For general D&A program design and Fair Work Commission
case law on testing programs, load `references/hazards.md` §5.

### 5.5 Fatigue — RSNL Fatigue Management Standard

RSNL Part 3 Division 8 requires RTOs to manage fatigue through:
- Fatigue Risk Management System (FRMS) or prescribed hours
- Rostering aligned with fatigue science
- Worker fatigue training and self-management
- Monitoring and review

The ONRSR fatigue management guidance draws on circadian science and
biomathematical modelling (FAID, SAFTE) — similar approach to mining and
aviation. Long single-driver runs, night shifts, and irregular rostering are
the highest-risk categories.

**Cross-reference**: For general fatigue management framework, load
`references/hazards.md` §18.

### 5.6 Notifiable occurrences

RSNL s 121–124 + Schedule 1 of the Regulation defines notifiable occurrences:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Category A** (most serious — notify immediately) | Train collision; derailment in passenger or freight service; level crossing collision with injury; fatality; significant fire on train |
| **Category B** (notify within 72 hours) | Signal passed at danger (SPAD); minor derailment; track worker near-miss; train protection system failure |

Notification is in addition to general WHS Act notifiable incident reporting
to the WHS regulator.

### 5.7 ATSB rail investigation

ATSB has national rail investigation jurisdiction since the post-2009
restructure (consolidating from state rail safety investigators). ATSB
investigates serious accidents and incidents under the TSI Act with the same
no-blame principle as aviation and maritime. ONRSR investigates for
regulatory enforcement purposes. As with aviation, parallel investigations
are common after serious events.

### 5.8 HSWA / WHS Act intersection

RSNL and the general WHS Act / HSWA apply concurrently:

- **Rail-specific safety** (signals, train movements, track work, rail
  infrastructure) — RSNL is the operative framework
- **General workplace safety** (manual handling in offices, slips and trips,
  electrical work in non-rail facilities, plant in maintenance workshops) —
  WHS Act / HSWA
- **Worker engagement** — RTOs typically establish a single integrated
  consultation framework that satisfies both RSNL worker engagement
  requirements and WHS Act HSR / consultation provisions
- **Officer duties** — Officers of an RTO have due diligence obligations under
  both RSNL s 55 and WHS Act s 27 / HSWA s 44; the evidentiary requirements
  are functionally similar but cited separately

**NZ rail** — Health and Safety in Employment (Rail) Regulations 1999 +
HSWA 2015. NZ Transport Agency (Waka Kotahi) administers; KiwiRail is the
predominant operator. NZ does not have a national rail safety regulator
equivalent to ONRSR.

### 5.9 Key rail hazards

| Hazard | Sector context | Cross-reference |
|---|---|---|
| **Track worker pedestrian/train interface (PTI)** | Highest-frequency rail fatality cause; worker struck by train. Controls: track possessions, lookouts, separation, exclusion zones, automatic warning systems. RSNL ACOP on Track Worker Safety | `hazards.md` §12 |
| **Level crossing** | Collision with road vehicles or pedestrians. Active controls (booms, lights), passive controls (signage), grade separation. Public-facing safety integration with road authorities | |
| **Electrical (overhead and third rail)** | Overhead 1.5 kV DC, 25 kV AC; third rail 750 V DC. Approach distances, isolation, earthing procedures. AS 7470 (overhead line systems) | `hazards.md` §10 |
| **Fatigue** | Driver fatigue in long single-driver runs; controller fatigue in 24/7 operations. RSNL Fatigue Management Standard; FRMS | `hazards.md` §18 |
| **Manual handling** | Maintenance work — couplers, brakes, track components; sustained postures; force exertion | `hazards.md` §17 |
| **Confined space** | Tank cars, locomotive fuel tanks, tunnels in some maintenance contexts | `hazards.md` §11 |
| **Noise and vibration** | Locomotives, maintenance plant, track machines; WBV from cab seats | `hazards.md` §14, §15 |
| **Working at height** | Overhead line work, double-deck rolling stock maintenance, structures | `hazards.md` §9 |

**Practical implications:**
- RTO accreditation is not a formality — the SMS is interrogated by ONRSR
  during accreditation review and on change
- Track work and possession arrangements are specialised disciplines requiring
  industry experience; generic WHS work-at-height or PTI controls are not
  sufficient
- Investigation of rail incidents commonly involves both ONRSR and the WHS
  regulator with statutory information barriers between regulatory enforcement
  and ATSB no-blame investigation

## 6. Healthcare Biosafety and Clinical WHS

Healthcare WHS combines the general WHS framework with specialised standards
for biosafety, radiation, chemotherapy, infection control, and clinical
environments. Most clinical facilities operate primarily under the WHS Act /
HSWA with sector-specific standards layered on top.

### 6.1 AS/NZS 2243.3:2022 — Microbiological safety and containment

**AS/NZS 2243.3:2022** — *Safety in laboratories Part 3: Microbiological safety
and containment* — is the foundational standard for laboratory biosafety in
AU/NZ. Defines Physical Containment (PC) levels and the engineering,
administrative, and procedural controls for each.

### 6.2 Physical Containment levels

| Level | Risk Group | Typical agents | Engineering controls | Use case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **PC1** | RG1 — unlikely to cause disease in healthy adults | Non-pathogenic E. coli, baker's yeast, low-risk environmental organisms | Basic laboratory; sink for handwashing; no special air handling | Teaching, low-risk research |
| **PC2** | RG2 — pathogens of moderate hazard, low community risk | Most clinical pathology specimens, Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella, influenza, HBV, HIV (clinical handling) | Biosafety Cabinet Class II for aerosol-generating procedures; autoclave for waste; restricted access; biohazard signage | Hospital pathology, clinical research, vaccine production |
| **PC3** | RG3 — pathogens of high hazard, low to moderate community risk | Mycobacterium tuberculosis (cultures), Bacillus anthracis, SARS-CoV-2 (some procedures), Coxiella burnetii | Dedicated facility; HVAC with HEPA filtration; negative pressure; double-door entry; BSC Class II minimum; PPE including respiratory protection; entry controls | Reference laboratories, certain research, specialist diagnostics |
| **PC4** | RG4 — pathogens of high hazard, high community risk; no effective treatment | Ebola, Marburg, Lassa, Nipah, Hendra (some procedures), Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever | Maximum containment; Class III BSC or positive-pressure suit; airlocks; effluent decontamination; very limited number of facilities globally | Australian Centre for Disease Preparedness (formerly AAHL), Geelong — only AU PC4 facility |

**The AAHL / ACDP Geelong** facility is operated by CSIRO and is one of a small
number of PC4 facilities globally. Operates under AS/NZS 2243.3 + biosecurity
legislation + OGTR requirements + Defence-related arrangements for some
agents.

### 6.3 Gene Technology Act 2000 and OGTR

**Primary instruments:**
- Gene Technology Act 2000 (Cth)
- Gene Technology Regulations 2001

**Regulator**: Office of the Gene Technology Regulator (OGTR), within the
Commonwealth Department of Health.

**Scope**: Regulates work with genetically modified organisms (GMOs). All work
with GMOs requires either a licence or certified facility (or both). Categories:

| Category | Approval required |
|---|---|
| Exempt dealings | Specified low-risk activities; no licence |
| Notifiable Low Risk Dealings (NLRD) | IBC assessment; OGTR notification |
| Dealings Not Involving Intentional Release (DNIR) | OGTR licence; PC2+ facility typically |
| Dealings Involving Intentional Release (DIR) | OGTR licence; field release; significant assessment |

**Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC)** — every organisation conducting
GMO work must have a registered IBC that assesses each project against the
Gene Technology Regulations and OGTR guidelines.

### 6.4 Radiation safety

State-based legislation regulates radiation in clinical, industrial, and
research settings:

| Jurisdiction | Act |
|---|---|
| NSW | Radiation Control Act 1990 + Radiation Control Regulation 2013 |
| VIC | Radiation Act 2005 + Radiation Regulations 2017 |
| QLD | Radiation Safety Act 1999 + Radiation Safety Regulation 2021 |
| WA | Radiation Safety Act 1975 |
| SA | Radiation Protection and Control Act 2021 |
| TAS | Radiation Protection Act 2005 |
| ACT | Radiation Protection Act 2006 |
| NT | Radiation Protection Act 2004 |
| NZ | Radiation Safety Act 2016 + Radiation Safety Regulations 2016 |

**Federal**: Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency
(ARPANSA) — regulates Commonwealth entities, sets RPS standards, advises on
medical and dental imaging. ARPANSA also operates the National Radiation
Dose Register for occupationally exposed workers.

**Clinical applications**:
- **Diagnostic imaging** — X-ray, CT, fluoroscopy, mammography; nuclear
  medicine (PET, SPECT)
- **Radiotherapy** — linear accelerators, brachytherapy, proton therapy
- **Industrial radiography** — non-destructive testing in construction,
  manufacturing, mining
- **Research** — sealed and unsealed radioactive sources

**Key controls** — time, distance, shielding, dose monitoring (TLD or OSL
badges), classified worker designations, regulated procedure rooms, source
security, transport regulations (IAEA/IATA), disposal pathways.

### 6.5 Infection control

**Primary reference**: NHMRC *Australian Guidelines for the Prevention and
Control of Infection in Healthcare* (2019 edition; periodically updated). Sets
out the framework for:

- **Standard precautions** — applied to all patients and all clinical
  encounters: hand hygiene, PPE for risk of body fluid exposure, safe handling
  of sharps, environmental cleaning, respiratory hygiene
- **Transmission-based precautions** — applied in addition to standard
  precautions when transmission risk is identified:
  - Contact precautions — gloves and gown for contact (e.g., MRSA, C. difficile)
  - Droplet precautions — surgical mask, eye protection (e.g., influenza,
    pertussis)
  - Airborne precautions — P2/N95 respirator, negative pressure room (e.g.,
    TB, measles, COVID-19 in some clinical contexts)

**Workplace duties** intersect with WHS Act / HSWA — workers exposed to
infectious agents must be provided with information, training, PPE, and
medical surveillance.

### 6.6 Sharps management

Needlestick injury (NSI) remains a significant clinical WHS exposure.

**Controls hierarchy:**
- Eliminate — substitute with non-injection administration where clinically
  appropriate
- Substitute — engineered safety devices (retracting needles, blunt-fill
  needles, needleless connectors); regulated under therapeutic goods
  framework
- Engineering — sharps containers at point of use; no recapping (single-handed
  technique only if recapping is essential)
- Administrative — training; protocols; post-exposure procedure; immediate
  reporting culture
- PPE — gloves provide partial protection only; do not prevent NSI

**Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)** — for HIV, HBV, HCV exposure. Time-critical
(HIV PEP ideally within 2 hours, must be within 72 hours). Every healthcare
PCBU must have a documented PEP pathway with 24/7 access.

### 6.7 Chemical exposures in healthcare

| Exposure | Setting | Key controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Anaesthetic gases** | Operating theatres, recovery, dental | Scavenging systems; periodic monitoring (N2O <25 ppm typical guideline); workspace ventilation; pregnancy considerations |
| **Cytotoxic drugs (chemotherapy)** | Oncology, pharmacy preparation, administration | AS 2639 (cytotoxic preparation); BSC Class II Type B2 or isolator; closed system transfer devices; spill kits; spill response protocol; specialised waste streams |
| **Formaldehyde / formalin** | Pathology, anatomy, embalming | Engineering ventilation; substitution with formalin alternatives where possible; WES (NES) 1 ppm TWA — Safe Work Australia revised 2020 |
| **Ethylene oxide (EtO)** | Sterilisation in some hospital and industrial contexts | Enclosed systems; ventilation; gas detection; aeration cycles; carcinogen — substitution with alternatives (gamma, e-beam, low-temperature steam) preferred |
| **Glutaraldehyde** | Endoscope reprocessing (legacy use); declining | Substitute with peracetic acid or other alternatives; ventilation; PPE |
| **Mercury (legacy)** | Sphygmomanometers, thermometers (legacy) | Removal program complete in most jurisdictions; spill response capability for remaining devices |

### 6.8 Aggression and violence

Healthcare — particularly emergency departments, mental health units, and
aged care — has the highest rate of occupational violence of any industry in
AU. This is treated as a **psychosocial hazard** under the WHS framework as
well as a physical hazard.

**Controls:**
- Environmental design (sightlines, alarm activation points, secure spaces,
  exit pathways)
- Triage of risk (use of risk assessment tools at presentation)
- De-escalation training (mandatory for clinical and security staff)
- Security presence (graduated; permanent in high-risk settings)
- Code Black / Code Grey response protocols
- Post-incident support (clinical debrief, psychological first aid, EAP)
- Reporting culture (high under-reporting historically)

**Cross-reference**: For psychosocial hazard regulatory framework, load
`references/legislation.md` §9.

### 6.9 HSWA / WHS Act intersection

For healthcare workplaces, the WHS Act / HSWA is the primary framework. Sector-
specific standards layer on:

| Layer | Source |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | WHS Act / HSWA — PCBU primary duty |
| **Biosafety** | AS/NZS 2243.3 + Gene Technology Act + OGTR licensing |
| **Radiation** | State Radiation Protection Acts + ARPANSA standards |
| **Infection control** | NHMRC Guidelines + state-issued infection control standards |
| **Cytotoxics** | AS 2639 + state health departmental policy |
| **Therapeutic goods** | Therapeutic Goods Act 1989 (Cth) — device safety, including engineered sharps safety devices |
| **National standards** | National Safety and Quality Health Service Standards (NSQHS) — accreditation framework for hospitals administered by Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care |

**Practical implications:**
- Healthcare WHS practitioners must operate across multiple regulatory
  frameworks; siloed compliance (WHS only, infection control only, radiation
  only) produces gaps
- The NSQHS framework (Standards 1–8) drives much hospital operational policy
  — WHS integration with NSQHS accreditation is the standard governance
  pattern
- Aged care has its own framework — Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission +
  Aged Care Quality Standards — that overlaps significantly with WHS,
  infection control, and psychosocial requirements

## 7. Defence

Defence operates under a complex regulatory framework involving the WHS Act
(Defence is a Commonwealth entity), Defence-specific statutes, internal
management systems, and specialised regulations for ordnance, security, and
operational activities.

### 7.1 Statutory framework

**Primary instruments:**
- Defence Act 1903 (Cth) — establishes the Defence Force; command authority
- Defence Force Discipline Act 1982 (Cth) (DFDA) — military justice for ADF
  members; covers service-specific offences
- Work Health and Safety Act 2011 (Cth) — applies to Defence as a Commonwealth
  entity PCBU; covers civilian and military workforce
- Defence Regulations 2016
- Defence (Inquiry) Regulations 2018

**Regulator**: Comcare administers the Commonwealth WHS Act 2011 in respect of
Commonwealth entities, including Defence. Defence is also subject to:
- Inspector-General ADF (IGADF) — internal inquiry function for serious
  incidents involving ADF members
- Defence-specific safety regulators for specialised domains (e.g., Defence
  Aviation Safety Authority — DASA — for military aviation; Defence Maritime
  Safety Authority — DMSA — for naval vessels)

### 7.2 WHS Act application to Defence

Defence is a PCBU under the Commonwealth WHS Act 2011. The Act applies to:

- All ADF members (Navy, Army, Air Force, including Reserves on duty)
- All APS civilian Defence employees
- Defence contractors when working on Defence business
- Visitors to Defence sites

The application is not absolute — section 12C (operational exclusion) and
section 12D (situational exclusions) carve out specific circumstances:

- Activities undertaken for the purposes of operations against an enemy or in
  support of UN or other international operations (s 12C)
- Specific operational training exemptions where defined by regulation
- The Defence Act and DFDA may apply to military-specific matters

In practice, peacetime training, garrison activities, base infrastructure
maintenance, and ordinary administrative work all sit under the WHS Act with
the same PCBU duty as any other Commonwealth workplace.

### 7.3 Defence Safety Management System (DSMS)

Defence operates an internal **Defence Safety Management System (DSMS)** which
operationalises the WHS Act duty for the Defence context. Key components:

- **Defence Work Health and Safety Strategy** — published periodically
- **Defence Safety Manual (DSM)** — comprehensive WHS reference for the
  Defence enterprise
- **DEFGRAMs** (Defence General Routine Administrative Messages) — formal
  directives on specific WHS matters; carry policy authority
- **Group and Service Safety Manuals** — Service-specific (Navy SAFETYMAN,
  Army Defence Aviation Safety Manual, RAAF specific) and Group-specific
  (Capability Acquisition and Sustainment Group, etc.)
- **Hazard- and activity-specific instructions** for high-risk activities
  (live-fire, explosive ordnance, parachuting, diving, helicopter operations)

The DSMS layers on top of the WHS Act framework — it does not replace it.
Defence supervisors and officers must understand both the WHS Act duty and
the relevant Defence policy.

### 7.4 Explosive Ordnance handling

Defence ordnance handling is governed by a specialised framework:

- **Defence Explosive Ordnance Publication (DEOP) 101** — Department of
  Defence Explosive Ordnance Regulations
- **Defence Explosive Ordnance Safety Branch (DEOSB)** — the regulatory
  authority within Defence for ordnance safety
- **State Munitions Officers (SMOs)** — appointed officers with statutory
  ordnance safety functions; competency-certified
- Storage, handling, transport, and disposal subject to dedicated quantity-
  distance rules, magazine standards, and personnel competency requirements

Civilian dangerous goods regulations (Australian Code for the Transport of
Dangerous Goods by Road and Rail — ADG Code; HCB regulations) apply at the
civilian / Defence interface (e.g., ordnance transport on public roads).

### 7.5 Security clearance intersection

Defence WHS work — particularly incident investigation — frequently involves
information that is security-classified. Key implications:

- Witness statements may contain classified operational information
- Photographs and incident scene records may be classified
- Investigation reports must be classified appropriately (typically PROTECTED
  or SECRET depending on operational context)
- Distribution of reports to inspectors, prosecutors, or external parties
  requires security clearance verification and Need-to-Know assessment
- Legal professional privilege management is more complex when classified
  information is in scope

**Practical implications**: Defence WHS practitioners must hold appropriate
security clearance (Baseline, NV1, NV2, PV) commensurate with the work; civilian
WHS consultants supporting Defence work typically require Baseline or NV1
as a minimum.

### 7.6 Defence industry — contractor WHS

Defence industry — Major Service Providers (MSPs), Lockheed Martin Australia,
Thales Australia, BAE Systems Australia, Raytheon Australia, NIOA, and the
many SME suppliers — is subject to:

- WHS Act 2011 (Cth) or relevant state WHS Act, depending on the workplace
  location and contracting jurisdiction
- Defence-specific contractual WHS requirements — typically aligned to
  AS/NZS 4801 or ISO 45001, with Defence-specific overlays for ordnance,
  classified work, and specific platforms (aviation, maritime, weapons)
- Defence Industry Security Program (DISP) — security accreditation; not a WHS
  framework but intersects (cleared facilities have additional access controls
  that affect emergency response and inspector engagement)
- Industry Specific Security Standards for specific platforms (e.g., aviation
  safety standards under DASA, naval safety standards under DMSA)

**Contractor WHS — Defence-specific patterns:**
- WHS Management Plans (WHSMPs) prepared to Defence template; often more
  detailed than typical commercial WHSMPs
- Site Specific Safety Plans (SSSPs) for specific Defence sites
- Pre-Mobilisation Briefings with Defence WHS representatives
- Joint incident investigation arrangements between contractor and Defence

**Practical implications:**
- Defence is a sophisticated WHS regulator-equivalent for its contractors —
  contractual WHS audit and assurance is substantial
- The Comcare jurisdiction for Defence does not exclude state WHS regulators
  from investigation where state-located workplaces are involved (e.g., a
  Defence training accident in NSW may attract both Comcare and SafeWork NSW
  attention)
- Industrial manslaughter — the Commonwealth industrial manslaughter offence
  (Criminal Code, since 1 July 2024) applies to Defence and Defence contractors
  in respect of workers killed in Commonwealth workplaces

## 8. Cross-Sector Themes

### 8.1 Regime selection — the more specific generally prevails

Where multiple regulatory regimes apply to the same hazard, the more specific
instrument generally prevails for that hazard. Examples:

- Helicopter operations offshore — OPGGS Act + CASR; CASR is the more specific
  instrument for flight operations, OPGGS Act for the offshore facility
- Track work next to an active rail line on a mining lease — RSNL is the more
  specific instrument for rail hazards; state mining Act for mining hazards;
  WHS Act for general workplace hazards
- Aircraft maintenance at a Defence base — CASR Part 145 for maintenance
  organisation duties; WHS Act 2011 (Cth) for general workplace duties; DSMS
  for Defence-specific layered requirements

The "more specific prevails" principle is not always neat — overlapping
duties commonly produce concurrent obligations that all must be discharged.
The framework for managing this is consultation, cooperation, and coordination
(3Cs) between duty holders.

### 8.2 Concurrent duties and the 3Cs

Multiple PCBUs with overlapping duties for the same workplace, workers, or
work is the norm in sector-specific environments. The WHS Act s 46 / HSWA
s 34 duty applies — duty holders must consult, cooperate, and coordinate
with each other to discharge their respective duties.

Key principles:
- Duties do **not** transfer between PCBUs — each PCBU retains their own duty
  even where contractual arrangements purport to allocate responsibility
- Each PCBU must comply with their own duty SFAIRP
- The 3Cs duty is itself a discrete legal obligation; failure to consult,
  cooperate, or coordinate is enforceable independently of any underlying
  hazard outcome

**Practical patterns in sector-specific environments:**
- Joint Safety Committees between operators sharing a facility (offshore
  petroleum, port operations, mining lease shared with rail)
- Interface agreements between rail infrastructure managers and accredited
  rolling stock operators
- Coordinated emergency response between Defence base and contractor
  workforces
- Joint inspector engagement protocols where multiple regulators may attend
  the same incident

**Cross-reference**: For the 3Cs framework in the general WHS context, load
`references/legislation.md` §4.

### 8.3 Investigation jurisdiction — ATSB vs WHS regulator

Sector-specific environments commonly produce parallel investigations after
serious incidents. The structural separation between no-blame safety
investigation (ATSB / TAIC) and regulatory enforcement (CASA / WHS regulators
/ ONRSR / NOPSEMA) is statutory and deliberately maintained.

| Investigation type | Purpose | Statutory protections | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| ATSB / TAIC safety investigation | Identify contributing factors; improve safety | Restricted information protections (TSI Act s 60); voluntary reports protected | Safety report; recommendations; no enforcement |
| WHS regulator / sector regulator | Determine compliance with statutory duties; enforce | Compelled answers; limited use immunity | Prosecution, notices, enforceable undertakings |
| Coronial inquest (death cases) | Determine manner, cause, circumstances of death | Statutory powers; broad inquiry | Findings; recommendations |
| Internal organisational investigation (ICAM) | Organisational learning; control improvement | Limited statutory protection; legal privilege management critical | Internal report; corrective actions |

**Practical implications:**
- Information sharing between investigations is statutorily controlled — do
  not assume documents provided to ATSB are available to CASA or to WHS
  regulators
- Witness interview protocols differ — workers may have different rights
  (right to silence, support person) under different investigation regimes
- Legal professional privilege management requires deliberate structuring at
  the outset of an investigation, not retrofitting once enforcement is
  threatened
- Engineering or operational analysis prepared for one investigation may be
  required by another — version control and contemporaneous record keeping is
  essential

**Cross-reference**: For general investigation methodology (ICAM, 5-Why) and
the relationship between internal investigation and regulator investigation,
load `references/investigation.md`. For inspector visit and regulator
attendance playbook, load `references/legislation.md` §10.

- [ ] Statutory roles correctly identified where relevant (SSE, UMM, OCE,
      RTO, AOC holder)
- [ ] Concurrent jurisdiction acknowledged where multiple regimes apply
- [ ] Notification framework correctly identified — sector-specific reporting
      is typically in addition to (not in substitution for) WHS Act notifiable
      incident reporting
- [ ] Investigation jurisdiction articulated — ATSB / TAIC vs regulator vs
      coroner vs internal
- [ ] 3Cs duty considered where multiple PCBUs / duty holders are in scope
- [ ] Industry-specific competency / accreditation / licensing requirements

- [ ] Cross-references to relevant general WHS framework (legislation,
      hazards, investigation) included
- [ ] Australian English spelling checked
- [ ] No safety clichés or filler phrases; direct and action-oriented

### 8.5 Practical implications for cross-sector practitioners

WHS practitioners working across sectors face structural challenges:

- **Terminology drift** — the same physical hazard is described differently
  across sectors (e.g., "confined space" in WHS Act vs "enclosed space" in
  some maritime contexts; "high-risk construction work" in WHS Reg vs
  "high-potential incident" in mining)
- **Reporting cadence differences** — sector regulators may require
  notification at different time thresholds (immediate, 24 hours, 72 hours,
  monthly) and via different channels
- **Inspector expertise** — sector inspectors are technically specialised;
  generic WHS responses to inspector queries are inadequate
- **Insurance and indemnity** — sector-specific insurance regimes (NIOA
  for mining, Maritime mutual insurance, aviation hull and liability) interact
  with workers compensation and public liability frameworks
- **Industrial relations** — sector-specific unions (CFMEU mining and energy
  division, MUA, AFAP, RTBU) have substantial WHS engagement and HSR
  involvement specific to the sector

**The principle for cross-sector practitioners**: do not assume the general
WHS framework is sufficient for sector-specific work. Identify the relevant
sector-specific Act, Regulation, standards, and regulator at the start of any
task — and engage with sector specialist colleagues where the work is
operationally complex.
