# Diversity and Inclusion as Work Health and Safety

Five domains where workforce diversity intersects directly with WHS duty:
First Nations workforce considerations, reasonable adjustments for workers
with disability, neurodivergent worker accommodations, multi-language safety
communication, and gendered violence and equity. Framing: inclusion is not
adjacent to safety. Equity gaps produce psychosocial hazards, control
failures, and consultation failures that show up in incident data and
regulator findings. The PCBU duty does not change with worker identity; the
practical work to discharge it does.

Use alongside `legislation.md` §9 (psychosocial hazards, Respect@Work
positive duty), `workplace-controls.md` §4 (WFH and FDV intersection), and
`frameworks.md` (psychosocial control frameworks).

## 1. Indigenous Workforce Considerations

First Nations workers — Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in AU,
Māori and Pasifika workers in NZ — face conditions of work most WHS systems
were not designed to address. This section covers WHS-specific implications;
RAPs and broader employment equity sit alongside but are not the focus.

### Cultural load

The disproportionate burden carried by First Nations employees as advisors,
advocates, translators between cultures, and intermediaries in moments of
organisational tension. Rarely in a position description, rarely measured,
frequently uncompensated.

| Pattern | Manifestation |
|---|---|
| Sole-of-kind in team | Asked to represent "the Aboriginal view" in meetings, design processes |
| Advisory beyond role | Consulted on RAPs, procurement, NAIDOC and Reconciliation Week, cultural protocols regardless of substantive role |
| Educator by default | Expected to brief new starters, managers, and visitors on cultural matters |
| Crisis advisor | Called in when cultural matters arise — community relations, heritage incidents, racially charged complaints |
| Symbolic representation | Photographed for marketing, asked to speak at events with no genuine role |
| Buffer for racism | Absorbs questions and "innocent curiosity" no one else has to |

Cultural load is a psychosocial hazard under the SWA Code of Practice 2022 —
high job demands, poor role clarity, inadequate reward and recognition,
traumatic events (where heritage loss is involved). It accumulates, compounds
with other demands, and is a frequent contributor to First Nations worker
turnover.

Controls that reduce cultural load:
- Build cultural advisory work into role descriptions where it is part of
  the role; remunerate, time-allocate, recognise explicitly
- Where the substantive role is not cultural advisory, protect the worker's
  capacity to decline; make declining low-friction
- Build broader organisational capability so the same workers are not asked
  repeatedly — cultural awareness is everyone's responsibility
- Engage external First Nations advisors where the matter exceeds appropriate
  internal scope
- Measure cultural load — engagement survey questions disaggregated by
  Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander identification

### Cultural heritage on Country — the WHS intersection

Many WHS-regulated activities — construction, mining, infrastructure, land
management, agriculture — occur on Country with cultural heritage values. A
heritage incident at a workplace can trigger psychosocial harm to First
Nations workers, regulatory action, community response, and operational
shutdown that becomes a WHS matter.

| Jurisdiction | Primary instrument | Practical scope |
|---|---|---|
| Federal | Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Heritage Protection Act 1984 | Emergency declarations where state law inadequate; rarely invoked |
| NSW | National Parks and Wildlife Act 1974 (NSW), Part 6 | No standalone ACH Act in NSW — Aboriginal objects and declared Aboriginal places are protected under the NPW Act; Aboriginal Heritage Impact Permits (AHIPs); due diligence code of practice; standalone ACH legislation remains a committed reform — a draft Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Bill was in stakeholder consultation through 2025 but is not yet enacted (as at mid-2026) |
| VIC | Aboriginal Heritage Act 2006 (VIC) | CHMP required for prescribed activities in cultural-heritage-sensitivity areas; Registered Aboriginal Parties consulted; Victorian Aboriginal Heritage Council oversight |
| QLD | Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Act 2003 (QLD) + Torres Strait Islander CHA 2003 | Cultural Heritage Duty of Care — every person doing an activity must take all reasonable and practicable measures to avoid harming Aboriginal cultural heritage |
| WA | Aboriginal Heritage Act 1972 (reinstated 2023 after ACH Act 2021 repeal) | s 18 consent regime; Juukan Gorge response and subsequent reversal; reform ongoing |
| SA | Aboriginal Heritage Act 1988 (SA) | Authorisation regime; Aboriginal Heritage Committee |
| TAS | Aboriginal Heritage Act 1975 (TAS) | Permits to disturb or destroy relics; Aboriginal Heritage Council |
| NT | NT Aboriginal Sacred Sites Act 1989 | Authority Certificate from AAPA before activities; Sacred Sites Register |
| ACT | Heritage Act 2004 (ACT) | Aboriginal places and objects protected |
| NZ | Heritage NZ Pouhere Taonga Act 2014 | Wāhi tapu and wāhi tūpuna; Heritage NZ Pouhere Taonga authorities; Māori Heritage Council |

Native title and ILUAs add a parallel layer — the Native Title Act 1993
(Cth) establishes the determination process; Indigenous Land Use Agreements
between native title holders and proponents are commonly the operating
framework for activity on determined or claimed Country. For WHS managers
working in heritage-sensitive industries, the heritage and native title
position is part of project setup, not an afterthought.

The 2020 destruction of Juukan Gorge rock shelters reshaped corporate,
regulatory, and community expectations. The subsequent parliamentary inquiry
(*A Way Forward*, 2021) is required reading for officers and senior
practitioners in heritage-sensitive industries.

### Sorry Business and bereavement leave

Sorry Business — cultural obligations during mourning following the death of
a community member. May include travel to Country, extended attendance at
funerals and ceremony, presence with family, and specific avoidance
protocols. Not optional in the way Western bereavement is structured — it is
cultural law.

Operational implications:
- Fair Work Act compassionate leave (2 days per occasion) is insufficient;
  most organisations with First Nations workforces extend bereavement
  provisions with paid and unpaid leave layered as needed
- Standard "immediate family" definitions exclude cultural family — uncles,
  aunts, cousins, kin by Country — who may carry the same significance as
  nuclear family in non-Indigenous policies
- Avoidance of names and images of the deceased may apply for an extended
  period — communications, training material, photo databases need
  flexibility to redact
- Return-to-work requires sensitivity; cumulative loss (Indigenous /
  non-Indigenous death rate gap is severe) compounds over time

Leading practice — explicit Sorry Business leave:
- Separately named entitlement (typically 5–10 days paid annually,
  additional unpaid as needed)
- No requirement to demonstrate "immediate family" relationship under
  Western definition
- Manager training on what Sorry Business is and is not
- Confidential disclosure pathway — to HR or a designated contact, not
  necessarily the direct manager if the relationship is strained

### Ceremony obligations and traditional welcome

Cultural obligations extend to ceremony — initiations, men's and women's
business, seasonal ceremony, Country obligations. May involve travel,
restricted communication, and absence not covered by standard leave
categories.

Smoking ceremony and Welcome to Country at workplace events:
- Welcome to Country is delivered only by a Traditional Owner of the
  specific Country; Acknowledgement of Country can be given by anyone
- Both should be planned, not improvised — engage the local Traditional
  Owner group with reasonable notice and appropriate remuneration
- Smoking ceremonies for new sites, projects, or milestones involve burning
  native plants — coordinate with fire safety, ventilation, and emergency
  response (smoke alarm response is a foreseeable issue)
- Welcomes and Acknowledgements at WHS events (toolboxes, safety forums)
  signal that safety is grounded in respect for the place where the work
  happens — authentic delivery is meaningful; perfunctory delivery deepens
  cynicism

### Indigenous employment programs — the WHS connection

RAPs (Reconciliation Australia) and Indigenous procurement targets
(Commonwealth IPP; state equivalents) sit largely outside WHS scope but
produce WHS implications:
- First Nations workers entering under employment programs need the same WHS
  supports as any worker, plus consideration of cultural load, workplace
  racism risk, and culturally informed supervision
- Indigenous procurement targets may bring First Nations contractors and
  subcontractors into scope — WHS prequalification, induction, and
  consultation must be culturally appropriate and not impose
  disproportionate documentary burden on small businesses
- Programs are easily reduced to numbers — retention, progression, and
  workplace experience are the meaningful measures; high turnover is both a
  WHS signal (psychosocial harm) and a RAP signal (the program is not
  working)

### Culturally informed safety practice

Standard WHS practice carries assumptions — individual accountability,
written English communication, formal HIRAC, hierarchical supervision,
scheduled consultation. None are wrong, but each can land differently for
First Nations workers.

| Practice | Adjustments to consider |
|---|---|
| Work design | Group work as cultural norm; sole-of-kind isolation a hazard; mentor / buddy approaches as control |
| Communication | Yarning circles as legitimate consultation format; written-first defaults exclude some workers; visual and verbal communication preferred |
| Supervision | Authoritarian styles can reproduce historical experiences of control; supportive, relational supervision more effective |
| Hazard identification | Worker-led identification (HSR, peer-led) often surfaces hazards formal processes miss |
| Investigation | ICAM and similar are extractive — repeated questioning can be retraumatising; cultural support during investigation |
| Discipline | Standard processes apply equally; cultural context — kinship obligations, community connection — may matter in proportionate response |

The principle: standard practice continues to apply; the duty is the same.
What changes is the manner of execution and recognition that one-size
practice produces inequitable outcomes.

### Racism and lateral violence as psychosocial hazards

Workplace racism — overt and covert — is a psychosocial hazard under the
SWA Code of Practice 2022. The PCBU's psychosocial duty under model WHS
Regulations rr 55A–55D (in NSW, Part 3.2 Div 11) applies. Lateral
violence (intra-community harm) is related but distinct.

The post-2023 Voice referendum period has been characterised by elevated
racism complaints across Australian workplaces — workplaces with First
Nations staff need to take this context seriously, not treat it as
exceptional.

Operational implications:
- Racism complaints are WHS matters, not just HR matters; the rr 55A–55D
  psychosocial duty applies; RDA 1975 and state equal opportunity
  legislation create parallel duties
- Manager training on recognising and responding to workplace racism is a
  psychosocial control under rr 55A–55D
- Complaints handling must be culturally safe — repeat questioning by
  multiple investigators, "proving" the conduct, comparison to non-Indigenous
  experience all deepen the harm
- EAP must include culturally appropriate counsellors; generic EAP without
  Indigenous-identified counsellors does not adequately discharge the duty
- Lateral violence requires culturally informed response, frequently
  involving external Indigenous mediators

### First Nations leadership consulted, not just informed

s 47 model WHS Act requires genuine consultation. The common failure mode is
"telling, not consulting" — communicating decisions already made.

Practical expectations:
- Engage at the framing stage, not the approval stage
- Engage with appropriate representatives — for community matters, with the
  community body, not solely with employed First Nations workers acting as
  proxies
- Allow appropriate time — consultation timelines structured around Western
  governance rhythms may not align with cultural decision-making processes
- Document decisions, consultation undertaken, and outcomes — defensible
  against later challenge that consultation was inadequate
- Pay for consultation work — Traditional Owner groups, RAP holders, and
  individual cultural advisors are routinely asked for unpaid input

## 2. Reasonable Adjustments for Workers with Disability

### The DDA — WHS intersection

The Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth) imposes obligations parallel to
but distinct from the WHS Act.

- DDA prohibits discrimination on the ground of disability in employment
  (Part 2 Div 1) — including failing to make reasonable adjustments
- The DDA contains no s 47C-style positive duty. Reasonable adjustment
  obligations arise through the definitions of discrimination — ss 5(2)
  and 6(2) DDA make a failure to make reasonable adjustments for a person
  with disability itself direct or indirect discrimination, subject only
  to unjustifiable hardship (s 11). A standalone DDA positive duty,
  mirroring the SDA s 47C duty introduced by the Anti-Discrimination and
  Human Rights Legislation Amendment (Respect at Work) Act 2022, is a
  Disability Royal Commission recommendation only — not yet law
- State equal opportunity Acts (NSW ADA 1977, VIC EOA 2010, QLD ADA 1991,
  etc.) provide parallel protections at state level
- WHS Act s 19 model primary duty requires the PCBU to ensure SFAIRP the
  health and safety of workers — including workers with disability, who may
  face elevated risk from generic controls designed for non-disabled workers

| Concept | DDA framing | WHS framing |
|---|---|---|
| Adjustment | Reasonable adjustment enables participation | Hazard control reduces risk to the worker |
| Standard | Unjustifiable hardship is the only defence | SFAIRP — only grossly disproportionate cost justifies non-implementation |
| Scope | Employment access, retention, progression, conditions | Health and safety while at work |
| Trigger | Worker has disability and asks (or PCBU is aware) | Hazard exists and could foreseeably cause harm |
| Consultation duty | Implicit — the worker is the expert on their disability | Express — s 47 model WHS Act |

For a worker with disability, both apply. A reasonable adjustment (e.g.,
height-adjustable workstation, ramp access) is also a hazard control. The
same intervention discharges both obligations with documentation referenced
to both regimes.

### The shape of reasonable adjustments

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Physical workplace | Accessible parking, ramps, accessible toilets, automatic doors, height-adjustable benches, accessible workstations, accessible meeting rooms, hearing loops |
| Assistive technology | Screen readers (JAWS, NVDA), screen magnifiers, voice recognition (Dragon), text-to-speech, ergonomic input devices, captioning |
| Rostering and hours | Modified rosters around treatment, fatigue management, flexible start/finish, reduced hours, job sharing, additional breaks |
| Communication | Auslan interpreter for meetings and training, written follow-up to verbal instructions, plain language, captioned video, accessible documents |
| Task modification | Removing non-essential tasks the worker cannot perform; reallocating within team; modifying tools or equipment |
| Supervision and support | More frequent check-ins, structured feedback, mentor or buddy, written expectations, manager training |
| Personal supports | Service animal access, personal care attendant access, flexible medication breaks |
| WFH as adjustment | Different framing from general WFH policy (cross-reference `workplace-controls.md` §4); equipment provision may differ |

### Workplace assessment for adjustments

Standard mechanism — workplace assessment by an occupational therapist (OT)
or other qualified assessor, requested by the PCBU with worker consent.
Triggered when:
- Worker discloses a disability and adjustments are being scoped
- Worker returns to work after injury or illness (cross-reference
  `compensation-rtw.md`)
- Existing arrangement needs review (role change, workplace change,
  condition change)
- A workers compensation insurer requires assessment

Assessment structure:
1. Worker interview — role, tasks, current arrangement, preferences
2. Workplace observation — environment, tasks, interactions
3. Job task analysis — physical, cognitive, sensory demands of the role
4. Recommendations — adjustments, equipment, training, environmental change
5. Costing — equipment, installation, training time
6. Implementation plan — sequence, owners, dates
7. Review at defined intervals after implementation

The OT report is the basis for the formal adjustment plan, agreed with the
worker, documented, implemented, and verified. Implementation is verified —
adjustments approved but not delivered are a frequent audit finding.

### Conversation tools and funded resources

Australian Disability Network (renamed from the Australian Network on
Disability, with the new name taking effect 15 January 2024) and its
Inclusion@Work program provide structured
conversation tools for managers initiating adjustment discussions:
- Adjustment conversation guide — questions that elicit information without
  requiring disclosure beyond worker choice
- Permissible questions under DDA and Privacy Act 1988
- Position description review — separating inherent requirements from
  preferred attributes
- **JobAccess** (jobaccess.gov.au) — the Commonwealth disability
  employment information and advice gateway; its **Employment Assistance
  Fund (EAF)** is the funding vehicle for workplace modifications,
  equipment, Auslan interpreting, and disability awareness training for
  eligible workers

NZ equivalents — Workbridge, CCS Disability Action, Government employment
services. HSWA 2015 operates alongside Human Rights Act 1993 in NZ; broadly
parallel structure.

### Unjustifiable hardship — the only defence

DDA s 11(1) sets out the factors:
- (a) the nature of the benefit or detriment likely to accrue to, or to be
  suffered by, any persons concerned
- (b) the effect of the disability of any person concerned
- (c) the financial circumstances of, and the estimated amount of
  expenditure required to be made by, the person claiming unjustifiable
  hardship
- (d) the availability of financial and other assistance to that person
- (e) any relevant action plans given to the AHRC under s 64

Practical reality:
- Threshold is high; modest cost or modest disruption is not unjustifiable
- Financial test scales — $5,000 adjustment unjustifiable for a sole trader
  may be trivial for a large PCBU
- Tax deductibility, JobAccess Employment Assistance Fund (EAF) funding,
  equipment redeployment reduce net cost; gross figure overstates impact
- Decisions to decline must be documented with reasoning — including
  consultation, alternatives considered, the specific factors that ground
  the unjustifiable hardship finding

Audit position: a documented refusal with detailed reasoning is defensible;
an undocumented refusal or one based on broad assertion ("we can't do that
here") is not.

### Consultation duty extends to workers with disability

s 47 consultation must be meaningful, which means:
- Workers with disability consulted on hazard identification and controls
  affecting them — not informed after the fact
- Where disability affects communication, consultation must be in an
  accessible format (Auslan, captioning, plain language, written follow-up)
- HSRs representing workers with disability should have awareness of
  disability-specific WHS considerations
- Worker advocates and union representatives are valid representatives — the
  PCBU cannot insist on bypassing representation in adjustment conversations

### Disclosed vs undisclosed disability

A meaningful proportion of workers with disability do not disclose — fearing
discrimination, career impact, assumption of incompetence. The ABS Survey of
Disability, Ageing and Carers (SDAC) 2022 — the latest release — found
5.5 million Australians (21.4%) have disability, a prevalence consistently
well above employer-reported rates. Implications:
- Workplaces designed for the disclosed population serve a fraction of the
  actual population
- Universal design — accessible buildings, accessible documents, captioned
  video, plain language — supports disclosed and undisclosed workers
  equally without requiring disclosure
- Disclosure environments are safer where leaders model that disability is
  normal, confidential pathways are clear, and adjustments are delivered
  consistently without performance penalty

## 3. Neurodivergent Worker Accommodations

### Framing — neurodivergence is not impairment

Neurodivergence covers autism, ADHD, dyslexia, dyspraxia, dyscalculia,
Tourette's, and others. Some are formally diagnosed disabilities under the
DDA; others are not. Common framing:
- Neurodivergent traits are differences in cognitive processing, not
  deficits to be remediated
- The workplace environment determines whether traits are advantageous or
  hazardous — a quiet, structured environment may be where an autistic
  worker excels; an open-plan, noisy, ambiguous environment may produce
  significant harm
- The control hierarchy applies — environment design (elimination,
  substitution, isolation) sits higher than individual adjustment

This framing is increasingly mainstream in employer practice and aligns
with Safety II / HOP principles — design the system for human variation,
do not assume a standard worker.

### Common conditions in workplace context

| Condition | Workplace-relevant characteristics |
|---|---|
| Autism spectrum | Sensory sensitivity (lighting, noise, scent); preference for predictability; literal communication interpretation; strong focus on areas of interest; social interaction differences; high attention to detail |
| ADHD | Variable attention; high creativity and divergent thinking; impulsivity; rejection sensitivity; time blindness; thrives with novelty |
| Dyslexia | Variation in reading speed and processing; visual / spatial strengths often counterbalance; strong oral comprehension; benefits from text-to-speech, structured documents |
| Dyspraxia (DCD) | Motor coordination differences; spatial planning; handwriting; sequencing of physical tasks; benefits from demonstration over written instruction |
| Tourette's | Involuntary movements or vocalisations; often comorbid with ADHD and OCD; stress amplifies; appropriate response is non-reaction, not management |
| Dyscalculia | Numerical processing differences; calculator reliance not a deficit; benefits from visual representation |

Workers may have multiple conditions, may have formal diagnoses or
self-identify. The workplace response does not require diagnosis.

### Sensory environment — the most common adjustment

The most consistent friction point, particularly for autistic and ADHD
workers.

| Factor | Adjustment options |
|---|---|
| Lighting | Avoid fluorescent flicker; full-spectrum or warm LED; local task lighting; choice of desk near or away from windows; dimmable meeting rooms |
| Noise | Quiet zones; noise-cancelling headphones (workplace equipment, not personal); located away from high-traffic areas; acoustic baffling in open-plan |
| Scent | Fragrance-free policies in shared spaces; cleaning product specification (low-VOC, unscented); meeting room ventilation; avoid scented air fresheners |
| Visual clutter | Quiet, low-stimulus workspace option; meeting rooms with neutral walls; reduced screen clutter; option to turn off video in calls |
| Temperature | Acceptable range with worker control over local environment (desk fan, personal heater, layering) |
| Touch / proximity | Predictable, sufficient personal space; advance notice of physical contact; culturally varied norms respected |

Open-plan offices are structurally hostile to many neurodivergent workers.
Hot-desking compounds the issue — environmental predictability and personal
control are eliminated. Where open-plan and hot-desking are organisational
defaults, deliberate alternatives — assigned quiet zones, bookable focus
rooms, WFH as an adjustment — are the workable mitigation.

### Communication preferences

| Mode | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Written vs verbal | Provide written as well as verbal; follow-up emails after meetings; written agendas before meetings |
| Asynchronous vs synchronous | Allow asynchronous response where feasible; reduce reliance on real-time chat with immediate-response expectation |
| Clear vs ambiguous | Specify deadlines and priorities; avoid "as soon as possible"; concrete deliverables; avoid sarcasm and figurative language in important communications |
| Direct vs indirect | Many autistic workers prefer direct feedback; indirect (hint, implication) may not register; explicit is respectful, not blunt |
| Eye contact and body language | Do not enforce neurotypical norms; the worker is communicating, the form differs |
| Pre-reads | Pre-distribute meeting material; reduces verbal-processing load in the meeting |

### Rostering — predictability vs flexibility

Two patterns appear, and they sometimes conflict:
- Some neurodivergent workers (often autistic) thrive on predictability —
  same shift, same start time, same tasks; disruption is a significant
  stressor
- Other neurodivergent workers (often ADHD) struggle with rigid structure —
  benefit from variability, autonomy, project-based work, and novelty

Implication: there is no universal neurodivergent adjustment. The adjustment
is to the worker, in conversation with the worker.

### Meetings and presentations

Common friction point — verbal, real-time, social, often ambiguous.
Adjustments:
- Pre-distribute material; allow processing time
- Provide written summary after the meeting
- Allow video off where it eases participation
- Allow contribution in chat as well as verbally
- Avoid surprise expectations to speak — invite contribution with notice
- For presentations by the worker, allow notes, rehearsal, and format choice
  (live verbal, recorded, written, demo)
- Avoid mandatory networking events; provide alternative connection pathways

### Disclosure — the worker chooses

A worker is not required to disclose neurodivergence (or any disability).
The DDA protects against discrimination regardless of disclosure. Implications
for the PCBU:
- The workforce includes neurodivergent workers whether identified or not
- Universal design (sensory consideration, communication clarity,
  predictability where feasible) supports all workers regardless of
  disclosure
- Disclosure environments are safer where leaders model neurodivergent
  affirmation (visible neurodivergent leadership, ERGs, communications that
  treat neurodivergence as normal variation)
- Where a worker chooses to disclose, the response is matter-of-fact:
  acknowledge, ask what would help, deliver, review

### Manager training

Manager capability is the highest leverage point. Training should cover:
- What neurodivergence is and is not — clearing common misconceptions
- Adjusting management practices (communication, feedback, meeting design,
  performance management)
- Recognising signs of distress (burnout, masking exhaustion, sensory
  overload)
- Responding to disclosure — what to say, what to do, what not to ask
- Adjustment conversation tools — Australian Disability Network
  Inclusion@Work resources

AU providers — Untapped Group, Specialisterne, Aspect (Autism Spectrum
Australia), DXC Dandelion, individual neurodivergent-led consultancies. NZ
providers — Altogether Autism, ADHD NZ, Workbridge.

### Emerging legal context

DDA case law on neurodivergence is developing. Key positions as at mid-2026:
- Diagnosed autism, ADHD, dyslexia, dyspraxia are accepted disabilities
  under the DDA s 4 definition (broad — "the malfunction, malformation or
  disfigurement of a part of the person's body" and "a disorder, illness or
  disease that affects a person's thought processes, perception of reality,
  emotions or judgment")
- Reasonable adjustment failures are actionable; unjustifiable hardship
  defence rarely available for low-cost adjustments
- WHS-related psychological injury claims following sustained failure to
  accommodate neurodivergent needs are an emerging area
- Neurodivergent women in particular face compounded exclusion patterns;
  WHS and discrimination duties apply together

## 4. Multi-Language Safety Communication

### The CALD workforce reality

27.6% of Australians were born overseas at the 2021 Census (ABS); later
ABS estimated resident population figures put the overseas-born share above
30%. Approximately 22.3% speak a language other than English at home (2021
Census). Concentrations are sector-
specific — construction, cleaning, hospitality, food processing, aged care,
transport, and parts of healthcare have CALD workforce proportions
substantially above the national average.

WHS implications:
- Safety communication designed for native English speakers excludes a
  significant portion of the workforce
- "Pass the toolbox in English" satisfies a procedural box, not the
  consultation duty
- Incidents disproportionately affect CALD workers in some sectors —
  documented in industry data and inspector reports

Relevant duties:
- s 19 model PCBU primary duty — workers must be able to understand the
  information, training, instruction, supervision provided
- s 47 consultation duty — must be meaningful, which requires comprehension
- Codes of Practice on information, training, instruction explicitly note
  language accessibility

### Literacy assumptions that fail

| Assumption | Reality |
|---|---|
| Workers can read English at adult workplace level | CALD workforce often reads English at lower proficiency than oral fluency suggests |
| Pictograms are universally understood | Symbol interpretation is culturally variable; AS 1216 / ISO 7010 reduce but do not eliminate variation |
| Workers will ask if they don't understand | Cultural and power dynamics make asking difficult; silent compliance frequently substitutes for comprehension |
| Verbal explanation is adequate | Verbal under stress (noise, accent, technical vocabulary) is often misunderstood; comprehension rarely checked |
| One translated document covers the workforce | "Translated" rarely means "translated well by a qualified person, validated by back-translation, and culturally adapted" |

Diagnostic question: can the worker, in their working language, articulate
the hazard, the control, what to do if the control fails, and who to tell?
If not, the training has not landed.

### Translation governance

**Professional translation** — by an accredited translator:
- **NAATI accreditation** (National Accreditation Authority for Translators
  and Interpreters) — the Australian standard; Recognised Practising and
  Certified Translator levels
- Industry-specific experience — safety, construction, healthcare,
  manufacturing terminology
- Knowledge of target audience literacy — translation for university-
  educated migrants differs from translation for low-literacy workers in
  the same language

**Back-translation for verification** — translated text translated back to
English by a second independent translator; the back-translation compared to
source to identify drift, ambiguity, or error. Required for high-stakes
safety material.

**Cultural adaptation** — beyond literal translation; idioms, examples,
imagery adapted to be meaningful to the target audience.

**Ad-hoc translation traps**:
- Google Translate / machine translation for safety-critical content —
  unsuitable as primary translation
- Workforce members translating informally (see multilingual peers below)
- Family members translating — fails confidentiality, accuracy,
  appropriateness
- "Close enough" — close enough is not the SFAIRP standard

### Pictographic standards

| Standard | Scope |
|---|---|
| ISO 7010 *Graphical symbols — Safety colours and safety signs* | International standard for safety signs; prohibition, mandatory, warning, safe condition, fire safety |
| AS 1216 *Class labels for dangerous goods* | Australian standard for dangerous goods labelling; aligned with UN system |
| GHS pictograms (Globally Harmonized System) | Nine hazard pictograms for chemicals; adopted in AU under the model WHS Regulations Chapter 7 (labelling at reg 335); Australia is on GHS 7, mandatory since 1 January 2023 |
| AS 1319 *Safety signs for the occupational environment* | Australian standard for workplace safety signs; complementary to ISO 7010 |
| AS 2293 *Emergency escape lighting and exit signs* | Egress signage; widely understood across language groups |

Pictographic content is necessary but not sufficient. Most safety
communication requires both pictographic and text elements; the text drives
translation requirements.

### Multilingual peers — formal vs informal interpretation

In many workplaces, multilingual workers act as informal interpreters for
colleagues — translating toolboxes, induction, instructions, peer
conversation. Widespread and largely uncompensated.

| Status | Practical reality |
|---|---|
| Formal interpretation | NAATI-accredited interpreter engaged for specific events (induction, formal training, incident investigation); paid, time-allocated, recognised |
| Informal peer interpretation | Co-worker translating on the fly; not paid, not time-allocated, impacts the peer's own work; quality variable |
| Bilingual supervisor or trainer | Supervisor delivers training to bilingual or non-English-fluent crew; effective where genuinely bilingual; problematic where second language is conversational only |
| Bilingual induction crew | Designated bilingual workers conduct induction for new starters in their language group; semi-formal arrangement common in large construction and meat processing |

Duty of care for the peer interpreter:
- Peer interpretation is part of their work — time recognised, recorded,
  reasonable in scope
- Quality assurance — checking translation matches meaning; the bilingual
  peer may not know technical safety terms
- Trauma exposure — peer interpreters in incident response or investigation
  may be repeatedly exposed to distressing content; EAP and structured
  debrief required
- Power dynamics — the peer may be junior or have different community
  standing; this can silence the interpreter

Leading practice — register of bilingual workers with languages and
proficiency levels, willingness to interpret in specific contexts, and
remuneration for substantive interpretation work.

### Induction for non-English-fluent workers

The highest-stakes safety communication. Requires deliberate design:
- **Translation** of induction content into the languages used in the
  workforce — written and verbal
- **Demonstration** — visual demonstration of correct work practice;
  observed practice by the worker; corrective feedback
- **Supervised practice** — observed performance of safety-critical tasks
  before independent work; duration matched to task complexity and prior
  experience
- **Comprehension check** — not "do you understand?" (the answer is always
  yes) but "show me how" or "tell me what you would do if"
- **Bilingual induction crew** where available
- **Buddy system** for early weeks — paired with a competent bilingual or
  same-language worker

White card and HRWL training are statutorily required and currently
delivered in English in most jurisdictions — a known gap. Workplace-specific
induction has more latitude and is where the gap can be closed.

### HSR election in multilingual workforces

The HSR system depends on workers being able to nominate, vote, raise
issues, and participate in consultation. In multilingual workforces:
- HSR election information and ballot materials available in workforce
  languages
- HSR training currently in English — bilingual HSRs particularly valuable;
  PCBU support for training access (extra time, support person, post-
  training translated material) eases barrier
- HSR-PCBU consultations should accommodate the HSR's language —
  interpreter for formal meetings, written follow-up
- Issue resolution in the language the worker raised the issue in —
  translating worker concerns to satisfy PCBU process is not the worker's
  burden

### Consultation duty fulfilled in language

s 47 is not discharged by communicating in English to a workforce that
includes workers who do not understand English fluently:
- Toolboxes, training, consultation conducted only in English do not
  satisfy the duty for non-fluent workers
- "Workers can ask if they don't understand" is the absence of consultation
- The duty is to consult with workers, not with their interpreters —
  translation enables direct consultation
- Records of consultation should reflect the languages used

Regulator expectation has moved firmly toward language accessibility in
high-CALD sectors. SafeWork NSW, WorkSafe Victoria, and WHSQ have all
published multilingual safety resources; inspectors in CALD-heavy sectors
routinely ask how non-English-fluent workers were inducted and consulted.

## 5. Gendered Violence and Equity Considerations

The Respect@Work positive duty (SDA s 47C, in force from 13 December 2022 —
the day after the Anti-Discrimination and Human Rights Legislation
Amendment (Respect at Work) Act 2022 received assent; AHRC enforcement
powers from 12 December 2023) and its WHS intersection are covered in
`legislation.md` §9. This section
addresses everyday practice, equity dimensions, and the practical issues
that sit beside but outside the s 47C framework.

### Beyond the positive duty — the daily practice

The positive duty is the statutory frame. Daily practice includes:
- Hazard identification and assessment — identifying where gendered
  violence is foreseeable (mixed-gender crews, customer-facing roles,
  isolated work, alcohol-involved settings, high power-differential
  relationships)
- Specific controls — design controls (sight-lines, lighting, lone-work
  pairing), administrative controls (codes of conduct, complaint pathways),
  consequence management (visible response when standards breached)
- Disclosure pathways — multiple, accessible, trauma-informed; one pathway
  is not sufficient if the manager is part of the issue
- Investigation capability — trauma-informed, independent where required,
  confidentiality protected, witness support
- Reporting and analytics — incident classification specifically identifying
  gendered violence, sexual harassment, sex-based harassment; trend
  visibility; dis-aggregation by department, role, demographic

The WHS function holds the hazard management framework. HR and Legal
typically hold investigation and consequence management. The boundary is a
common failure point — gaps appear at the interface, particularly for
matters that span WHS, HR, and legal frameworks (e.g., sexual harassment by
a contractor of an employee).

### The statutory net around sexual harassment — FW Act Part 3-5A and the SDA

Sexual harassment at work is now regulated through three interlocking
regimes, and a competent response engages all of them:

- **FW Act Part 3-5A** (in force from 6 March 2023, inserted by the Secure
  Jobs, Better Pay amendments) prohibits sexual harassment in connection
  with work — covering workers, prospective workers, and persons
  conducting a business or undertaking. An aggrieved person can apply to
  the FWC for a **stop-sexual-harassment order** (preventative, while the
  working relationship continues — this sits in **Part 6-4B**, the
  stop-bullying jurisdiction extended to sexual harassment from
  11 September 2021, distinct from the Part 3-5A prohibition itself) and/or
  ask the FWC to **deal with the dispute**
  (a new jurisdiction: conciliation; if unresolved, arbitration by consent
  or court proceedings for compensation). Employers are **vicariously
  liable** for contraventions by employees or agents in connection with
  employment unless they prove they took all reasonable steps to prevent
  the conduct.
- **SDA s 28B** prohibits sexual harassment in employment and other work
  contexts (coverage aligned to WHS-style "worker" and PCBU concepts by
  the 2021 amendments). **SDA s 28M**, inserted by the Anti-Discrimination
  and Human Rights Legislation Amendment (Respect at Work) Act 2022,
  separately prohibits subjecting a person to a **hostile workplace
  environment** on the ground of sex — capturing conduct not
  targeted at an individual (pornographic material, sexual banter,
  demeaning "jokes") without a complainant needing to show they were
  personally harassed.
- **WHS psychosocial duty** — sexual harassment is a recognised
  psychosocial hazard under model WHS Regulations rr 55A–55D (NSW
  Part 3.2 Div 11); the PCBU must eliminate or minimise it SFAIRP through
  work design and systems of work, not merely respond to complaints.

The interlock: SDA s 47C demands proactive prevention (the positive duty,
AHRC-enforceable from 12 December 2023); the WHS psychosocial duty demands
the same through hazard management; FW Act Part 3-5A and SDA s 28B supply
the individual remedy pathways when prevention fails. One incident can
engage all of them simultaneously — and "all reasonable steps" under the
FW Act, "reasonable and proportionate measures" under s 47C, and SFAIRP
controls under WHS converge on the same evidence: risk assessment, a
prevention plan, training, leadership conduct, and a complaint system that
works. For the full statutory chain, load `legislation.md` §9.

### Third-party harassment — the commonly missed exposure

Workplace sexual harassment policies frequently address worker-on-worker
conduct in detail and customer / client / patient / member-of-the-public
conduct cursorily. The Respect@Work positive duty applies equally to third-
party harassment.

| Sector | Common patterns |
|---|---|
| Healthcare | Patient-on-staff harassment; cognitive impairment, D&A affected, deliberate; affects nursing, aged care, mental health, paramedicine |
| Retail | Customer-on-staff; intoxicated customers; visible harassment of young workers; verbal and physical |
| Hospitality | Customer-on-staff; intoxication-amplified; late-night and licensed venue exposure |
| Education | Parent and student-on-staff; social media amplification; particularly affecting female teachers |
| Real estate | Open homes, after-hours inspections; client and prospective client conduct |
| Community services | Client and client-family harassment; in-home settings |
| Public-facing government | Citizen-on-officer; Centrelink, council, regulator front counters |
| Transport | Passenger-on-staff; bus, taxi, rideshare drivers; train station staff |

Controls for third-party harassment differ from worker-on-worker:
- Site design — visibility, separation, lighting, panic alarms
- Customer-facing training — de-escalation, recognising risk patterns,
  authorised early intervention
- Refusal-to-serve policies — workers empowered to refuse service or end
  interactions; backed by management
- Banning and incident response — third parties recorded, banned where
  appropriate, reported to police where conduct is criminal
- Worker support after incidents — debrief, EAP, time off, workers
  compensation claim management for psychological injury

Common failure: customer-facing harassment treated as part of the job. It is
not. The PCBU duty applies regardless of the source of the harm.

### Gender pay equity reporting — the WGEA layer

WGE Act 2012 (Cth) requires non-public sector employers with 100+ employees
to report annually to the Workplace Gender Equality Agency. The 2023
amendment (Closing the Gender Pay Gap) strengthened the regime:
- WGEA publishes employer-specific gender pay gaps annually (from February
  2024)
- Reporting includes paid FDV leave provisions, sexual harassment incidents
  and outcomes (aggregated), workforce composition by gender, and
  remuneration analysis

WHS connection:
- Sexual harassment incident reporting to WGEA aligns with WHS hazard
  reporting — same incident captured for two regulatory purposes
- Persistent gender pay gaps are correlated with workplace cultures that
  produce psychosocial harm — not deterministic but indicative
- Public disclosure drives accountability not present in WHS-only
  frameworks

### Psychological safety differentials by gender

Workplace surveys and research consistently show differential psychological
safety experiences by gender:
- Women report lower psychological safety in male-dominated workplaces
- Indigenous women, women from CALD backgrounds, women with disability,
  LGBTIQ+ women face compounding effects
- The gap is sector-specific — construction, mining, defence, parts of
  emergency services, parts of financial services, IT engineering have
  documented differentials

WHS implication:
- Engagement and culture surveys disaggregated by gender (and intersecting
  characteristics); aggregate figures hide the issue
- Hazard identification consultations conducted in formats that surface
  underrepresented perspectives — not assuming the loudest voice represents
  the workforce
- Manager training on inclusive leadership — psychological safety is built
  or destroyed by managers

### Flexible work as gender equity issue

Flexible work — WFH, part-time, job sharing, compressed weeks —
disproportionately matters for women, who continue to carry disproportionate
share of caring responsibilities. WHS interaction:
- Refusal of reasonable flexible work requests can be a psychosocial hazard
  (high job demands, poor support, work-life conflict)
- Fair Work Act provides a statutory right to request flexible work
  arrangements (s 65) — refusal must be on reasonable business grounds
- Flexible work refusals concentrated in roles held by women, or in
  workplaces with cultures hostile to flexibility, indicate a systemic issue
- Right to Disconnect (cross-reference `workplace-controls.md` §4)
  intersects — after-hours availability expectations disproportionately
  affect carers

### Hybrid equity — the two-class workforce risk

The hybrid transition has created a two-class pattern in many organisations:

| Class | Workforce | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Office workers (WFH-capable) | Knowledge workers, administration, management, professional services | Flexible hours, hybrid, reduced commuting, WFH equipment provided |
| Frontline (not WFH-capable) | Customer-facing, healthcare, manufacturing, FM, hospitality, transport, education delivery | Fixed location, fixed hours, in-person, often shift-based |

This pattern is gendered, racially correlated, and class-correlated in ways
that produce visible inequity. The frontline workforce — higher proportion
of women in caring sectors, CALD workers in hospitality and manufacturing,
lower-paid workers across — bears the in-person burden. WHS dimensions:
- Perception of differential treatment is itself a hazard (poor reward and
  recognition, low job control for the frontline group)
- Frontline workers often have higher physical and psychosocial demands AND
  lower flexibility — compounded exposure
- The hybrid policy can address this — explicit acknowledgement, deliberate
  compensating measures (shift premiums, frontline-specific flexibility
  within fixed location, more generous leave for frontline workers, parking
  and amenity differential recognising commute)
- Communications that frame flexibility as a universal benefit when the
  frontline cannot access it deepen the inequity perception

### Family and domestic violence — the workplace dimension

FDV in the WFH context is covered in `workplace-controls.md` §4. Beyond
WFH, FDV manifests in the workplace through:
- Workers experiencing FDV may be at risk during work hours from contact by
  the abuser at work
- Workers may use work as a refuge — extended hours, reluctance to leave
- Workers may demonstrate behavioural changes visible to managers and
  colleagues
- Abusers may be co-workers — workplace contact, control through work
  scheduling, surveillance

Workplace safety planning for affected workers:
- Confidential disclosure and intake — a trained, named contact in HR or
  EAP
- Threat assessment — is the abuser likely to come to the workplace,
  monitor electronically
- Workplace adjustments — desk location, parking, escort to vehicle,
  variable schedule, changed contact details
- Communication discipline — workplace systems should not reveal worker
  location to external callers; HR confidentiality strict
- Police and AVO coordination where applicable
- EAP with FDV specialist counsellors
- Fair Work Act 10 days paid FDV leave (ss 106A–106E) — eligibility
  checked, access supported, confidentiality protected

Current resources: 1800RESPECT (the national domestic, family and sexual
violence counselling service) publishes workplace-focused resources on
responding to disclosure and supporting affected workers; Champions of
Change Coalition publishes employer guidance on FDV-informed workplace
policy and leadership practice.

### Supplier diversity intersection

Indigenous procurement, women-owned business, social enterprise, and
disability enterprise programs intersect with WHS through:
- WHS prequalification — designed for incumbent contractor profiles, may
  impose disproportionate documentary burden on diverse suppliers
- Capability uplift — simplified prequalification for low-risk scopes,
  mentoring through prequalification process
- WHS performance of diverse suppliers — typically equivalent or better
  than peer contractors when supported through prequalification; failure
  rate concentrated in unsupported entry

A WHS function aligned to supplier diversity engages with procurement on
prequalification design, provides capability uplift, and reports on WHS
performance of diverse suppliers as a deliberate slice of contractor
performance reporting.

### Equity-as-safety — the connection some organisations miss

Treating equity as a HR or Diversity portfolio matter separate from safety
produces predictable failures:
- Psychosocial hazards specific to underrepresented groups (sexual
  harassment, racism, cultural load, ableism) are managed under
  discrimination frameworks but not WHS frameworks; controls are weaker
- Investigation conducted with HR rigor rather than WHS rigor; root cause
  analysis often shallow
- Boards see workforce engagement and incident data side by side without
  the connection drawn; equity gaps appear as engagement issues rather than
  safety risks
- The WHS function lacks visibility of issues that would inform hazard
  identification — discrimination complaints, harassment patterns, pay
  equity findings — that should feed psychosocial risk assessment

The connection some organisations have made:
- All forms of workplace harm — physical, psychological, gendered, racial,
  ableist — managed under a unified harm prevention framework
- WHS incident classification includes gendered violence, sexual harassment,
  racism, ableism as distinct categories within psychosocial reporting
- Psychosocial risk assessment uses engagement, complaint, and
  discrimination data alongside incident data
- Board reporting integrates the picture — safety, conduct, engagement,
  equity in one frame
- The WHS function has a seat at the diversity and inclusion strategy
  table, and vice versa

Not duplicate management — recognition that the psychosocial duty under
model WHS Regulations rr 55A–55D covers harm historically managed under
separate frameworks.
Regulator expectation is moving toward integration; leading organisations
are already there.

## 6. Output Checklist for Diversity and Inclusion Tasks

Before finalising any output addressing First Nations workforce, disability
adjustments, neurodivergent accommodations, multi-language communication, or
gendered violence and equity, confirm:

### First Nations workforce
- [ ] Cultural load addressed where the task involves advisory, representational, or educator burden
- [ ] Heritage framework identified for the relevant jurisdiction (NSW NPW Act 1974 Part 6 AHIPs, VIC CHMP, QLD Duty of Care)
- [ ] Native title and ILUA position considered for activities on Country
- [ ] Sorry Business and ceremony leave provisions explicit, not assumed within standard bereavement
- [ ] Welcome to Country / Acknowledgement of Country protocols correct for event type
- [ ] Cultural advisory work compensated, time-allocated, recognised
- [ ] Racism and lateral violence treated as psychosocial hazards under rr 55A–55D
- [ ] Consultation with First Nations representatives genuine, not retrofitted

### Disability adjustments
- [ ] DDA reasonable adjustment framework and WHS hazard control framework both applied
- [ ] Workplace assessment by OT or qualified equivalent for substantive adjustments
- [ ] Adjustment plan documented, agreed with worker, implemented, verified
- [ ] JobAccess Employment Assistance Fund (EAF) funding considered for eligible adjustments
- [ ] Unjustifiable hardship reasoning documented where adjustment declined
- [ ] s 47 consultation duty discharged in accessible format
- [ ] HSR awareness of disability inclusion considerations

### Neurodivergent accommodations
- [ ] Environmental design considered (sensory, communication, predictability) before individual adjustment
- [ ] Communication preferences accommodated (written, asynchronous, clear)
- [ ] Meeting and presentation alternatives offered
- [ ] Disclosure not required — universal design applies
- [ ] Manager training in neurodivergent affirmative practice in place
- [ ] DDA framework applied to formal accommodation requests

### Multi-language communication
- [ ] CALD workforce composition known for the workplace or sector
- [ ] Translation by NAATI-accredited translator for high-stakes material
- [ ] Back-translation verification for safety-critical content
- [ ] Pictographic standards applied (ISO 7010, GHS, AS 1216, AS 1319)
- [ ] Induction includes translation, demonstration, supervised practice, comprehension check
- [ ] Bilingual peer interpreters compensated, time-allocated, supported
- [ ] HSR election and consultation conducted in workforce languages
- [ ] Records of consultation reflect languages used

### Gendered violence and equity
- [ ] Respect@Work positive duty (SDA s 47C) framework applied — cross-reference `legislation.md` §9
- [ ] FW Act Part 3-5A and SDA ss 28B / 28M remedy pathways identified alongside the WHS psychosocial duty
- [ ] Third-party harassment exposures identified for client / customer / patient-facing roles
- [ ] WGEA reporting alignment for in-scope employers
- [ ] Psychological safety data dis-aggregated by gender and intersecting characteristics
- [ ] Flexible work refusals reviewed for systemic patterns
- [ ] Hybrid arrangement equity considered — frontline workforce explicitly addressed
- [ ] FDV workplace safety planning in place; Fair Work Act ss 106A–106E FDV leave accessible
- [ ] WHS classification captures gendered violence, sexual harassment, racism, ableism as distinct psychosocial categories
- [ ] Board reporting integrates safety, conduct, engagement, and equity data
