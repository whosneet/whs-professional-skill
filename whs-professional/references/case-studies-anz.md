# Case Studies Reference — Australian and New Zealand Landmark Cases

Australian and New Zealand accidents, inquiries and prosecutions that shaped
the law and practice practitioners work under here. Load this file when a
board paper, officer briefing, training session or safety alert needs a local
precedent: long-latency disease and parent company liability, offshore well
integrity, community exposure from a site fire, restart decisions after a
warning event, Crown and public-sector liability, driver incapacitation, or
what courts have actually done with industrial manslaughter. Cite the case
alongside the inquiry, coroner or court whose findings establish the systemic
reading, not the case alone.

For Longford, Pike River, Dreamworld and Whakaari, and for the international
cases (Texas City, Macondo, Grenfell and others), see `case-studies.md`. For
fictional everyday incidents used as ICAM calibration material, see
`case-studies-everyday.md`; for fictional critical-risk worked cases, see
`case-studies-critical-risk.md`. Statute text, duties, fault elements and
penalty frameworks live in `legislation.md`.

## 1. Scope and How to Use

### What this file covers
Six Australian and New Zealand cases, each followed by an inquiry, coronial
finding or judgment that still shapes current law, and a running record of
the first industrial manslaughter prosecutions (§8). Longford, Pike River,
Dreamworld and Whakaari are covered in case-studies.md §2, §5, §6 and §7 and
are not repeated here; several lessons below point back to them.

Each case follows the sibling file's structure: site context, sequence of
events, contributing factors (attributed to the inquiry, coroner or court
that found them), regulatory outcome, and AU/NZ practice lessons. §8 differs
because it tracks a series of matters rather than one event.

For when and how to use a case in a board paper, training session or safety
alert, follow case-studies.md §1; for citation conventions, common errors
and the Dekker test, follow case-studies.md §13. Both apply here unchanged.

### Choosing a case
| The question in front of you | Case |
|---|---|
| Long-latency disease, state of knowledge, exposure beyond the site boundary, parent company liability, record retention | §2 Wittenoom |
| Barrier verification, management of change on a resequenced task, operator–contractor sign-off, the offshore petroleum regime | §3 Montara |
| Foreseeable external hazards such as bushfire, duty to the community and responders, emergency planning with agencies, non-operational areas | §4 Hazelwood |
| Restart after a warning event, ground control, independent review, near-miss notification, prosecution time limits | §5 Beaconsfield |
| Public-sector and Crown liability, engineered design of structures, under-resourcing, closing an asset on a report of movement | §6 Cave Creek |
| Operator-presence controls, sudden incapacity and fitness-for-work medicals, second-person intervention, emergency response, tracking inquiry recommendations | §7 Waterfall |
| What prosecutors and courts have done with industrial manslaughter, for a board or officer briefing | §8 |

### Reading the cases
- Read each case the way its inquiry or court did: as a question about the
  system of work, not about the person at the sharp end (SKILL.md §6;
  case-studies.md §13, the Dekker test). §9 draws the recurring patterns
  together.
- "As at" markers flag facts that move: appeals, live proceedings, penalty
  regimes and current instruments. Re-check each against the court record or
  the regulator before it reaches a board paper.
- Penalty maxima are stated in penalty units; current unit values are in
  `assets/penalty_units.json`.

## 2. Wittenoom Blue Asbestos (1943–1966 and aftermath)

### Site context
Wittenoom Gorge sits in the Hamersley Range in the Pilbara, Western
Australia, on Banjima country. Crocidolite (blue asbestos) was worked
there on a prospector scale from 1937. In 1943, with State and federal
government support, **Australian Blue Asbestos Ltd (ABA)** (later
Midalco Pty Ltd), a subsidiary of the Colonial Sugar Refining Company
(**CSR Ltd**), took over the workings and ran them until December 1966,
managed from CSR's Sydney head office. Production peaked in 1962 at
17,491 tonnes, more than half of it exported.

About 6,900 people worked for ABA (6,485 men, 410 women); median tenure
was 114 days and 74% stayed under a year. More than 20,000 people are
estimated to have lived in the town; the residents cohort includes about
2,500 who were under 15 (Musk et al, *International Journal of
Epidemiology*, 2020; Berry et al, *British Journal of Cancer*, 2012).

### Sequence of events
| When | Event |
|---|---|
| 1943 | ABA takes over; the workings move from Wittenoom Gorge to nearby Colonial Gorge (three mines operated over the life of the field) |
| 1948 | WA Health Department medical officer Dr Eric Saint warns that the operation will produce an unprecedented crop of asbestosis |
| 1948–1958 | Periodic konimeter surveys repeatedly record dust at six to eight times the level regarded as acceptable at the time |
| 1959–1962 | First officially recognised asbestosis diagnosis in WA (1959); first Wittenoom mesothelioma identified (1960) and reported (1962) |
| 1966 | Asbestos fibres counted in the work areas for the first and only time; mine and mill close in December for economic reasons, despite government assistance |
| 1978 | State begins phasing down the town on health grounds |
| 1988–1989 | First trial verdicts against Midalco and CSR; CSR settles 200 claims for $18,266,000 |
| 2007–2008 | Town abolished (2007); area classified a contaminated site, unsuitable for any human occupation or land use (2008) |
| 2022 | *Wittenoom Closure Act 2022* (WA) provides for taking the remaining private lots under Part 9 of the *Land Administration Act 1997* (WA) (ss 6–9), at fixed compensation per lot (s 12) |
| 2026 | On 25 February the Banjima Native Title Aboriginal Corporation commences Federal Court proceedings against the State seeking orders to remediate the mines, tailings dumps and floodplain, and reparations (as at February 2026) |

### Contributing factors (trial findings; Wittenoom cohort studies)
No inquiry of the Pike River kind was ever held. The factors come from
the 1988 trial judgment of Rowland J and the cohort research of Musk,
de Klerk, Reid and Berry.

| Level | Factor |
|---|---|
| Hazard | Crocidolite dose-response established for mesothelioma, lung cancer and asbestosis; the mesothelioma rate kept rising with time since first exposure and levelled off only after 40–50 years; only one case arose within 15 years of first exposure |
| Exposure control | Heavy dust in the mill, worst in the old mill; dust reduction efforts found insufficient by the trial judge; cloth masks were available to mill workers but never required and rarely worn |
| Monitoring | Particle counts only; fibres were never counted until 1966, so individual exposure had to be reconstructed decades later by grading jobs |
| Knowledge | The 1948 warning pre-dated most of the exposure; diagnosed asbestosis (1959) and mesothelioma (1960) did not stop production |
| Information to workers | Workers were not warned; a high-turnover workforce left before any disease appeared |
| Exposure boundary | Tailings were spread on roads, the airfield, the racetrack and around public buildings, used as aggregate and given to employees for gardens; Aboriginal workers loaded and rode the trucks carting fibre to Point Samson, where State-employed wharf workers handled it again |
| Corporate structure | CSR exercised hands-on management of ABA, while ABA (later Midalco, a company with no significant assets) was the employer on paper |
| Regulator | State agencies held the 1948 warning and a decade of adverse dust readings, and the State assisted the operation to continue |
| Production economics | The mine closed when it stopped paying, not when the hazard was understood |

### Regulatory outcome
Accountability came through civil courts, not the regulators. Most early
claims failed or died with the plaintiffs; the position turned in 1988.

| Case | Held |
|---|---|
| *Barrow and Heys v CSR Ltd and Midalco Pty Ltd* (WASC, Rowland J, 4 August 1988, unreported) | After a 131-day trial, both companies negligent towards mill workers exposed in 1948–51 and for three months in 1955; CSR owed the workers a direct duty because it, not ABA, controlled the operation |
| *Midalco Pty Ltd v Rabenalt* [1989] VR 461 | Victorian jury awarded $426,000 compensatory and $250,000 exemplary damages to a man who worked at Wittenoom in 1960–61, partly as a bagger in the mill; reported as the first exemplary award in an Australian negligence claim; appeal dismissed |
| *Watson v State of Western Australia* [1990] WAR 248 | State liable as employer of a Point Samson wharf worker who handled Wittenoom fibre in 1958–59 |
| *CSR Ltd v Wren* (1997) 44 NSWLR 463 | Not a Wittenoom claim; parent liable alongside its subsidiary because of the degree of actual management control |
| *CSR Ltd v Young* (1998) Aust Torts Reports 81-468 | Duty extended to a child resident exposed to tailings in the town; the claim began in the NSW Dust Diseases Tribunal, the specialist court for common-law dust disease claims (*Dust Diseases Tribunal Act 1989* (NSW)), not the no-fault scheme in compensation-rtw.md §3 |
| *James Hardie & Co Pty Ltd v Putt* (1998) 43 NSWLR 554 | The contrast: parent not liable to an employee of its New Zealand subsidiary because control of that workplace was not shown |

**Disease toll and the site**. To December 2014 the cohort studies record
373 mesotheliomas among former workers and 158 among residents who never
worked for ABA, plus 497 and 103 lung cancers (Musk et al, 2020); the WA
Government puts asbestos-related deaths among workers and residents at
more than 2,000 (as at March 2019). The Wittenoom Asbestos Management
Area (46,840 hectares) is unsuitable for any human occupation and closed
behind permanent roadblocks (as at October 2025), with about three
million tonnes of tailings left in place (as at March 2019).

**Companion case — James Hardie**: parent liability tested by a restructure.

| When | Event |
|---|---|
| 15–16 February 2001 | James Hardie Industries Ltd separates its former asbestos subsidiaries, Amaca and Amaba, into the Medical Research and Compensation Foundation with stated assets of $293 million, and tells the ASX the Foundation can meet all legitimate future claims |
| October 2001 – March 2003 | The group re-domiciles under a Dutch parent; partly paid shares giving a call of about $1.9 billion on that parent are cancelled |
| 21 September 2004 | NSW Special Commission of Inquiry (D F Jackson QC): future claims about $1.5 billion in net present value; funds projected to run out in the first half of 2007; the model assumed 11.7% annual returns for 50 years; the public statements "seriously misleading"; no legal obligation on the parent to fund the liabilities |
| 2005–2006 | Shortfall addressed by agreement, not by a finding of liability: the Asbestos Injuries Compensation Fund (formed 2006) administers the Amended and Restated Final Funding Agreement under the *James Hardie Former Subsidiaries (Winding up and Administration) Act 2005* (NSW) and pays claims against Amaca, Amaba and ABN 60 (as at October 2026) |
| 2012 | *ASIC v Hellicar* [2012] HCA 17: seven non-executive directors breached their duty of care and diligence (*Corporations Act 2001* (Cth) s 180(1)) by approving the draft ASX announcement |

### AU/NZ practice lessons
- **Latency defeats every lagging indicator**. No diagnosis or claim
  surfaced for more than a decade; when asbestosis (1959) and
  mesothelioma (1960) did, most of the dose had been delivered and
  production went on. Judge control by measured exposure, never by the
  absence of disease, and treat the exposure standard as a legal ceiling,
  not a safe level: eliminate exposure SFAIRP, otherwise minimise it
  SFAIRP below the standard (model WHS Regulations reg 420 for asbestos;
  regs 35, 36 and 49 for RCS and other hazardous chemicals). Workplace
  Exposure Standards become Workplace Exposure Limits on 1 December 2026
  (legislation.md §14).
- **Design the dose out; RPE protects only the wearer**. No worker
  behaviour explains Wittenoom. Even a well-run RPE program would have
  protected only the wearer, not the town, the haul route or the port;
  the controls that mattered were wet processing, enclosure and
  extraction in the mill, and keeping tailings out of circulation
  (frameworks.md §1; hazards.md §2).
- **State of knowledge is objective and dated**. "Ought reasonably to
  know" is a limb of reasonably practicable (model WHS Act s 18(c); HSWA
  s 22(c); OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 20(2)(c); frameworks.md §2), and officers
  need a process that receives a warning like 1948's and acts on it
  (model s 27(5)(a), (b) and (d); HSWA s 44(4)). Keep a dated log of
  regulator alerts, SDS revisions, exposure results, medical advice and
  your response. Ask the board: when did we first know, and what did we
  do that month?
- **Corporate structure does not move the duty**. The primary duty covers
  workers whose activities are "influenced or directed" by the PCBU
  (model WHS Act s 19(1)(b); HSWA s 36(1)(b); legislation.md §4), so a
  parent running a subsidiary's operation owes it directly and its
  officers owe due diligence (model s 27; HSWA s 44; legislation.md §6;
  case-studies-anz.md §8). In Victoria the parent is reached through OHS
  Act 2004 ss 21(3), 23 or 26, and officer liability is derivative
  (s 144). Hardie's second trap: a restructure can breach no funding
  obligation yet leave claimants unfunded, and its announcement can
  breach directors' duties (*Hellicar*). Carry latent disease exposure
  as a live liability through any divestment (frameworks.md §13).
- **The exposure boundary is where the dust goes**. Assess waste,
  transport, take-home contamination and neighbours, and control them at
  source: change rooms and on-site laundering, decontamination before
  leaving the dirty area, covered loads, no reuse of waste as fill. The
  duty to other persons (model WHS Act s 19(2); HSWA s 36(2)) and
  contaminated land law follow the material (environment.md §8); ground
  carrying naturally occurring asbestos, as in parts of the Pilbara, falls
  under Chapter 8 of the model WHS Regulations (hazards.md §3).
- **Records must outlive the business**. Exposure monitoring records
  (model WHS Regulations reg 50, 30 years) and health monitoring records
  (reg 378 for hazardous chemicals including RCS, 30 years; reg 418 for
  lead; reg 444 for asbestos, 40 years) prove claims decades later
  (specialist-topics.md §1). Presumptive claims (compensation-rtw.md §12),
  the NSW dust diseases scheme (compensation-rtw.md §3) and common law all
  turn on where and when exposure happened. Write record custody into
  every closure, sale and system migration plan.
- **Engineered stone is the same pattern on a shorter fuse**: potent
  dust, dry processing, monitoring that trailed disease and a transient
  workforce (hazards.md §1–§2). Before the first diagnosis, ask of any
  novel material: what is the measured exposure and its trend; what did
  we know, and when; who controls the operation; where does it go beyond
  the site; and who will hold the records in 40 years?
- **NZ difference**. The ACC bar (Accident Compensation Act 2001 s 317)
  removes the compensatory damages claims that forced accountability
  here, though exemplary damages survive (compensation-rtw.md §4);
  *Putt* shows the limits of reaching an Australian parent. In NZ,
  regulator enforcement and your own exposure data carry the load.

## 3. Montara Wellhead Platform Blowout (21 August 2009)

### Site context
The Montara oilfield lies in Commonwealth waters of the Timor Sea (the Ashmore
and Cartier Islands offshore area), about 250 km off the Western Australian
coast, in 76–90 m of water. Thailand's PTT Exploration and Production bought
the titleholder, Coogee Resources, in February 2009 and renamed it PTTEP
Australasia (Ashmore Cartier) Pty Ltd (PTTEPAA), part-way through a five-well
batch drilling campaign over the Montara wellhead platform (WHP) by Atlas
Drilling's *West Atlas* jack-up rig.

Regulation was split. The Commonwealth Minister was the Designated Authority
for this external-territory area (Offshore Petroleum and Greenhouse Gas
Storage Act 2006 (Cth) (OPGGS Act) s 70(8)–(9) as then in force) and had
delegated well approvals and environment plans to the Northern Territory
Department of Resources (NT DoR) under the Petroleum (Submerged Lands)
(Management of Well Operations) Regulations 2004 and (Management of
Environment) Regulations 1999 (Cth). The National Offshore Petroleum Safety
Authority (NOPSA) regulated OHS under OPGGS Act Sch 3 and the environment
department held the EPBC Act 1999 (Cth) approval; both left well integrity to
the NT DoR.

### Sequence of events
| When | Event |
|---|---|
| 7 March 2009 | 9⅝" casing shoe cemented in the H1 well, inside the reservoir. The float valves failed and 16.5 barrels of returned fluid were pumped back down, over-displacing the cement (a wet shoe). The shoe was not pressure tested |
| 6–13 March 2009 | PTTEPAA sought, and the NT DoR approved, suspension of H1 with two pressure-containing anti-corrosion caps (PCCCs) in place of the planned cement plug. The 9⅝" cap went on; the 13⅜" cap was deferred so H1 could be used from time to time to park the blowout preventer (BOP) |
| 21 April 2009 | *West Atlas* left the field. H1 stood suspended on the untested shoe and one untested cap; the 13⅜" cap was never fitted |
| 20 August 2009 | Rig back over H1 at 4.30 am; the 13⅜" cap found missing. The 9⅝" cap was removed at about 11.30 am to clean corroded threads and not refitted. The derrick, carrying the BOP, was skidded to the GI well at about 5 pm and to H4 at midnight |
| 21–22 August 2009 | 5.30 am: 40–60 barrels of fluid escaped from H1, then the flow subsided. 7.23 am: the well kicked, blowing oil and gas onto the underside of the rig floor. All 69 people were evacuated without injury. On 22 August a NOPSA prohibition notice barred people from the rig and from vessels alongside it |
| 14 September–3 November 2009 | *West Triton*, sailed from Singapore, began a relief well on 14 September. The fifth intercept attempt succeeded on 1 November, about 2.6 km into the seabed, and fire broke out on the WHP during kill operations. Heavy mud killed the well and the fire was out on 3 November, 74 days after the blowout |

The Inquiry put the flow at possibly 400 to 1,500 barrels of oil a day (about
29,600 barrels, or 4.7 million litres, at 400 a day) and faulted the lack of a
better estimate. Oil was sighted at various times across up to 90,000 km² and
entered Indonesian and Timor-Leste waters. The Australian Maritime Safety
Authority led the response as Combat Agency under the National Plan to Combat
Pollution of the Sea by Oil and Other Noxious and Hazardous Substances; the
Inquiry found its dispersant use appropriate. It called Montara the worst oil
spill in the history of Australia's offshore petroleum industry.

### Contributing factors (Montara Commission of Inquiry)
The **Montara Commission of Inquiry** (David Borthwick AO PSM), announced on
5 November 2009 and established under OPGGS Act Part 9.10A with nearly all
royal commission powers, reported on 17 June 2010: 100 findings and 105
recommendations, measured against sensible oilfield practice. The failings
were systemic: when H1 was suspended not one of its barriers met PTTEPAA's own
Well Construction Standards, and no Montara well was under proper control.

| Level | Factor (Commission of Inquiry) |
|---|---|
| Primary barrier | Cemented casing shoe compromised in the cement job and never pressure tested, although the company's standards required it; the warning signs were in the daily drilling report and were not evaluated |
| Secondary and assumed barriers | Two caps programmed, one installed, neither tested in place, and the manufacturer said they were not designed as blowout barriers; the inhibited seawater believed to overbalance the reservoir was neither monitored nor sufficiently overbalanced |
| Management of change | Substituting caps for a cement plug rested on a couple of lines in an 11 March change control form and a recorded US$50,000 rig-time saving; removing the last cap in August was treated as a mere change of sequence and not notified to the regulator |
| Standards and competence | Well Operations Management Plan (WOMP) and Well Construction Standards generic, ambiguous and silent on batch drilling; rig personnel had a manifestly inadequate understanding of what the standards required |
| Information flow | Defective exchange of information between rig and shore, day and night shift, and offline and online work; the missing 13⅜" cap went unnoticed until the rig returned in August |
| Operator–contractor interface | PTTEPAA made the well-integrity decisions; Atlas, responsible for rig safety, was either unaware of them or went along; no formal joint sign-off |
| Production pressure and governance | The prevailing approach was to get the job done without delay (the 13⅜" cap deferred for BOP parking, no internal audit or review at critical milestones); a newly acquired titleholder reporting straight to its overseas parent had no mechanisms to assess project risk, competence or contractor interaction |
| Regulator | NT DoR gave preliminary approval for the cap substitution in 30 minutes, approved in July 2009 a program that would have left H1 on one barrier for 36 hours or more, had effectively one person on the task and no effective monitoring or audit: "the regulatory dog did not bark". The Victorian and Western Australian regulators said they were unlikely to have approved that program |

Macondo eight months later (case-studies.md §4) repeated the pattern: a failed
cemented shoe with no valid test (none at Montara, a misread negative pressure
test at Macondo), the remaining barrier removed during suspension, a BOP that
could not help, Halliburton cementing on both, and a regulator unable to
challenge the operator. The difference was ignition: Montara burned only after
72 days and a full evacuation; Macondo killed 11.

### Regulatory outcome
The report was released with a draft response on 24 November 2010; the final
response (23 May 2011) accepted 92 recommendations (two in principle), noted
10 and rejected three.

- **Licence**. Instead of the show-cause notice the Inquiry recommended, the
  Minister on 4 February 2011 relied on an independent review of the Montara
  Action Plan and bound the Thai parent to a Deed of Agreement (22 February
  2011), 18 months of monitoring and extra title conditions.
- **Prosecution**. PTTEPAA pleaded guilty in the NT Magistrates Court, Darwin,
  to three OHS offences under OPGGS Act Sch 3 cl 9 (failing to verify well
  barriers) and one under s 569 (good oilfield practice), and on 31 August
  2012 was fined A$510,000 against a combined maximum then reported as about
  A$1.7 million: NOPSEMA's first successful OPGGS Act prosecution.
- **Regulator**. NOPSA took over WOMPs in April 2011 and, under the OPGGS
  Amendment (National Regulator) Act 2011, became NOPSEMA on 1 January 2012,
  adding environment plans; NOPTA took titles (sector-regimes.md §3). Two 2013
  OPGGS Amendment Acts (Compliance Measures; Compliance Measures No. 2) lifted
  OHS penalties to WHS Act 2011 (Cth) levels or above and added civil
  penalties, infringement notices, polluter pays and financial assurance.
- **Current regime** (as at October 2026). Every well activity, suspension
  included, needs a WOMP accepted by NOPSEMA under Part 5 of the OPGGS
  (Resource Management and Administration) Regulations 2025 (Cth), in force
  31 March 2026 (ss 76–77), with acceptance criteria and assurance for
  non-operational periods (s 82(5)–(6)). A significant change to how
  well-integrity risk is managed needs a revision lodged first (s 83(2));
  changing barrier elements needs 21 days' notice (s 97(1)(a)(v)). The
  operator must take all reasonably practicable steps (OPGGS Act Sch 3
  cl 9(1)); the titleholder's well duty (cl 13A) and WOMP acceptance (s 81(d))
  are ALARP tests. Sch 3 breaches carry up to 3,500 penalty units if reckless,
  1,750 if negligent (cls 16B–16C), and up to five times that for a company
  (Crimes Act 1914 (Cth) s 4B(3); assets/penalty_units.json). Onshore duties
  stay SFAIRP (frameworks.md §2).
- **NZ** (as at October 2026). Health and Safety at Work (Petroleum
  Exploration and Extraction) Regulations 2016, Part 6: SFAIRP safe suspension
  (reg 61), well examination scheme (reg 64), notice to WorkSafe before well
  operations (reg 66).
- **Civil liability**. *Sanda v PTTEP Australasia (Ashmore Cartier) Pty Ltd
  (No 7)* [2021] FCA 237 (Yates J, 19 March 2021) held PTTEPAA owed Indonesian
  seaweed farmers a duty of care, breached it in suspending H1, and that its
  oil killed their crops; the lead applicant got about Rp 252 million before
  interest. The class action (more than 15,000 farmers, 81 villages) settled
  for A$192.5 million, approved February and March 2023; payments ran from
  December 2023, the last due April 2025 (as at October 2026).

### AU/NZ practice lessons
- **A barrier counts only once it is tested in place**. The Inquiry's floor:
  two or more barriers at all times, each tested soon after installation and
  re-verified before the rig leaves, before re-entry and before any barrier is
  removed; one barrier only with the regulator's prior written approval in a
  true emergency (Recommendations 5, 6, 24–28). Ask the same of any critical
  control (frameworks.md §7): who verified it, and against what criterion?
- **A change of sequence is a change**. Run management of change on anything
  that removes, substitutes, defers or reorders a barrier: check design
  intent, have someone who does not own the schedule approve it, and tell the
  regulator first where it departs from the accepted plan (2025 Regulations
  ss 83(2), 97; frameworks.md §3, clause 8.1.3; sector-regimes.md §8).
- **Approval is not assurance**. As at Pike River (case-studies.md §5), the
  regulator was a contributing factor, not a backstop: audit your own
  compliance with an accepted plan at each critical milestone.
- **No injuries is not evidence of control**. Nobody was hurt, yet three of
  four convictions were OHS offences: failing to take all reasonably
  practicable steps needs no harm (OPGGS Act Sch 3 cl 9; compare model WHS Act
  ss 32–33, legislation.md §6). Classify such events on potential (pSIF,
  analytics.md §1) and report them to officers as such.
- **Shared decisions need shared sign-off**. The Inquiry wanted joint
  operator–contractor sign-off on safety, well-integrity and environmental
  decisions. Put barrier-status decisions in the bridging document for both to
  sign; that meets the consult, cooperate and coordinate duty (model WHS Act
  s 46; HSWA s 34; legislation.md §4; offshore, OPGGS Act Sch 3 cls 9–11).
- **Plan source control before drilling**. The relief-well rig came from
  Singapore and the kill took ten weeks. Identify relief-well rigs, and tell
  the regulator, before each drilling phase (Recommendation 80).
- **Acquisitions inherit live risk**. The operator changed hands weeks before
  the failed cement job. Due diligence must reach operating standards and live
  barrier status, not only claims history (frameworks.md §13).
- **The fine is not the exposure**. The 2012 fine was A$510,000; the 2023
  class-action settlement was A$192.5 million. Board papers should size
  third-party and cross-border liability, not just the statutory maximum.

## 4. Hazelwood Mine Fire (February–March 2014)

### Site context
The Hazelwood open-cut brown coal mine (almost 1,200 hectares, perimeter over
18 km) sat beside Morwell in Victoria's Latrobe Valley and fed the Hazelwood
Power Station, then about a quarter of Victoria's baseload supply. Hazelwood
Power Corporation Pty Ltd (HPC), part of the GDF Suez (later ENGIE) group,
operated the mine and employed its workforce for a partnership of four
companies. The coal is porous, lies in thick seams near the surface and is
very hard to extinguish; Hazelwood had major fires in 1977, 2006 and 2008. The
northern batters (mine walls) nearest Morwell were worked out, their coal
exposed under unslashed vegetation and without adequate fire-water pipework.

### Sequence of events
Sunday 9 February 2014 was a day of extreme fire danger. Two bushfires
burned close to the mine: the Hernes Oak fire, which broke containment
lines early that afternoon, and a fire at Driffield. Just before 2 pm mine
personnel saw embers spotting into the mine; by 2.30 pm several spot fires
were alight. One at the top of the northern batters burned down all five
levels, about 100 m, in under an hour. By Monday morning the northern
batters fire was about 2 km long.

Smoke and ash sat over Morwell for weeks, exposing residents, mine workers and
firefighters to fine particles and carbon monoxide (CO). Hourly mine-fire
PM2.5 was later estimated at up to 3,700 µg/m³; the then 25 µg/m³ daily NEPM
standard was exceeded in Morwell on 27 days. The EPA's mobile monitoring
station reached southern Morwell on 19 February, day 11. An expert reference
group convened on 16 February rebuilt suppression around compressed air foam
and thermal imaging. The fire was under control by 10 March and declared safe
on 25 March: 45 days, over 7,000 fire-service and mine personnel, and a cost
the Board put above $100 million.

### Contributing factors (Board of Inquiry)
The **Hazelwood Mine Fire Inquiry** (Hon Bernard Teague AO, chair; Prof
Emeritus John Catford; Sonia Petering) reported on 29 August 2014 with 18
recommendations (12 to the State, 6 to GDF Suez) and 57 affirmed
commitments. It rejected the perfect-storm account: every factor in the
ignition and spread was foreseeable. The table adds the jury's 2019 findings.

| Level | Factor (Board of Inquiry 2014; jury verdict 2019, unless stated) |
|---|---|
| Risk assessment | No adequate assessment of fire from an external source: bushfire ember attack on the worked-out areas |
| Engineering | No adequate reticulated fire-water pipework on the northern batters; extra pipework had to be laid during the fire |
| Housekeeping | Vegetation on the northern batter faces not slashed |
| Readiness | Northern batters not wetted down on 9 February despite the extreme fire danger forecast; too few staff with fire-fighting expertise rostered for the weekend of 8–9 February |
| Warning information | CFA predictive fire mapping reached the mine indirectly the evening before; mine personnel were not familiar with the tool |
| Power supply | Fire-water system and command centre depended wholly on mains power; the Board required back-up supply (rec 14), though the jury acquitted on the two alternative-power charges |
| Incident command | The Board required a pre-established command centre and an accredited incident controller at the mine on total fire ban days (rec 13), and incident management teams that integrate the operator with the emergency services (rec 2) |
| Regulation | Mine work plans (MR(SD) Act 1990 (Vic) s 40) did not have to address risk; WorkSafe's mine oversight centred on major mining hazards, defined by multiple-fatality potential. A foreseeable risk to the town fell between the mining and OHS regulators |
| Public health response | The State could not monitor air quality rapidly; the Board required data to inform decisions within 24 hours (rec 5). The communications strategy was written a week into the fire and adopted four days later |

The reopened Inquiry (Teague, Catford, Anita Roper; four reports, 2015–16, on
Anglesea mine, deaths, health improvement and mine rehabilitation) found the
fire likely contributed to some of the increase in Latrobe Valley deaths in
2014. It directed 32 recommendations and five affirmations to the State, which
committed $51.2 million in the 2016–17 Budget. Monash University's Hazelwood
Health Study (2014 recommendation 10: run it at least 20 years) found each
10 µg/m³ of mine-fire PM2.5 associated with a 9 per cent higher hazard of
respiratory hospitalisation over five years (Xu et al., 2022).

### AU/NZ practice lessons
- **The duty to non-employees reaches beyond the fence**. Five of HPC's ten
  convictions were under s 23(1), persons other than employees; the smoke
  reached responders and the Morwell community. The same duty sits in model
  WHS Act s 19(2) (harmonised jurisdictions and WA; the CMSHA/MQSHA at Qld
  coal and mineral mines, sector-regimes.md §2) and HSWA s 36(2) (NZ). List
  off-site populations and responders as exposed groups in every major
  hazard scenario. Since 1 July 2020 a negligent s 23 breach that kills a
  member of the public can be workplace manslaughter in Victoria (s 39G;
  legislation.md §16; case-studies-anz.md §8).
- **Foreseeable external hazards are yours to assess**. HPC did not light the
  fire and was still convicted. Bushfire, flood and a neighbour's release
  belong in the risk register: the SFAIRP question is what the event does once
  it reaches your site. The partners' s 41 offences were "outcome based, not
  risk based" (Keogh J): the pollution sufficed. Since 1 July 2021 Victoria's
  risk-based general environmental duty (Environment Protection Act 2017 (Vic)
  s 25) has run alongside WHS (environment.md §2).
- **Success hides drift**. Keogh J found "a degree of complacency and
  subconscious acceptance of risk born from success" in reducing mine
  fires. Dekker's drift into failure (frameworks.md §12) is the frame:
  fewer fires read as proof the controls were adequate while protection
  of the worked-out areas stayed thin. Test the control, not the record.
- **Readiness follows the forecast, not the roster**. The trigger rows
  above suit any weather-triggered hazard, but they are administrative;
  the fuel and engineering rows do more of the work (frameworks.md §1).
- **Watch the responders as well as the town**. At a long-running fire,
  measure CO and particulates at the work front from day one, set exposure
  triggers and relief rotations, keep people out of the plume before relying
  on respirators, and log who was exposed and for how long (CO limits and the
  1 December 2026 WEL change: legislation.md §14). Community health advice is
  not the operator's call: in Victoria it runs through the health department
  and emergency agencies under the State Smoke Framework (environment.md §12).
  Agree what data you supply, and when (strategy-function.md §5).
- **Build the emergency plan with the agencies and the council**. A Victorian
  prescribed mine must prepare it with the emergency services and, where major
  mining hazards could reach the surrounding area, the council, and test it at
  least yearly (OHS Regulations 2017 (Vic) rr 433–435). Elsewhere cite the
  mine regime first (sector-regimes.md §2): model WHS Regulations Ch 10 in SA,
  WHS (Mines and Petroleum Sites) Regulation 2022 (NSW), WHS (Mines)
  Regulations 2022 (WA), Qld CMSHA/MQSHA, and in NZ the HSW (Mining Operations
  and Quarrying Operations) Regulations 2016 (case-studies.md §5). A major
  hazard facility consults the emergency services and, on off-site
  consequences, the local authority (model WHS Regulations r 557). Model r 43
  and NZ GRWM Regs r 14 cover other workplaces (workplace-controls.md §2).
- **Non-operational is not non-hazardous**. Worked-out, mothballed and
  care-and-maintenance areas lose pipework, staff and attention while the
  hazard stays; name them in the risk assessment. Covering exposed coal is
  a fire control, so progressive rehabilitation belongs in the risk
  register. Price unfunded rehabilitation in any deal (frameworks.md §13).
- **Regulatory seams are your risk**. Where a mining, OHS and environment
  regulator share a site, map each major hazard to the instrument and
  regulator that covers it. A hazard nobody inspects is not a hazard
  nobody owns (case-studies.md §5; sector-regimes.md §2).

## 5. Beaconsfield Gold Mine Rockfall (25 April 2006)

### Site context
Beaconsfield Gold Mine, 40 km north of Launceston, Tasmania, worked the
narrow, steeply dipping Tasmania Reef via a shaft to the 375 m level and a
footwall decline below. Its joint venture manager, Allstate Explorations NL,
was in administration from June 2001; mine seismicity began in 2002 at the
760 m level. The friable quartz-ankerite reef broke into small blocks under
strain, so failure depth around ore drives grew with stoping. Standard support
was 1.8 m and 2.4 m friction bolts at 1.5 m spacing with mesh and straps.

Workplace Standards Tasmania (WST) had a two-person mines inspectorate. The
Workplace Health and Safety Act 1995 (Tas) set general duties, with no mining
regulations or approved mining code (s 22), but the Workplace Health and
Safety Regulations 1998 (Tas) required risk assessment (r 17), a written
record of significant risk (r 18(4)) and the hierarchy of controls (r 19(2)).

### Sequence of events
Two October 2005 rockfalls were the rehearsal. On 9 October, seismicity after
a production blast shook down about 350 t of already damaged ground on the 925
level. On 26 October a local magnitude (ML) 2.1 event, the mine's largest yet,
brought down about 350 t on the 915 level, with a smaller fall on 925.
Management stopped stoping in the block, briefed WST (which could not send an
inspector), engaged consultants and set a five-step pre-restart plan. In
December it added 2.4 m resin threadbars and straps on 925 and cone bolts on
915. By late January 2006, before the plan's forward stress modelling was
done, it decided to resume; from 10 February it mined what the Coroner found
was in effect the old modified Avoca sequence, not the advised "checkerboard".

At 9.23 pm on Anzac Day 2006 an ML 2.3 event, generated by slip on an unmined
section of the Tasmania Shear in the hanging wall, perhaps as close as 10–20 m
to the crew, shook down the 925 level ore drive. Larry Knight, 44, was
operating a telehandler while Todd Russell and Brant Webb worked from its
basket, building a mesh wall at a stope brow. A fall of about 120 t killed
Knight; a second of about 800 t entombed Russell and Webb.

Fourteen of the 17 underground got out. A remote-controlled bogger cleared the
falls; Knight's body was found in its stockpile on 27 April (the Coroner
rejected the theory that the recovery killed him). On 30 April the underground
manager walked to the rear of the fall, called out and was answered. Blasting
stopped; a raise borer cut a 1 m tunnel, finished by hand and low-impact
charges, and both men were freed on 9 May.

### Contributing factors (Coroner)
Special investigator Greg Melick SC, with Professor Michael Quinlan (UNSW) on
OHS management, reported after about 14 months that the event's magnitude and
location were unforeseeable. **Coroner Rod Chandler** (26 February 2009) drew
on Quinlan and went further into how the risk was managed.

| Level | Factor (Coroner) |
|---|---|
| Hazard knowledge | By late 2004 consultants' reports had identified every element of the shakedown risk in ore drives — a fragmented rock mass under seismic loading — and the need for purpose-designed support |
| Precursor data | 25 reported falls of ground from February 2004 to April 2006 — 64% over 50 t, 40% with failure depth over 2 m; the reports alone were enough to put the support design in question |
| Reporting integrity | The register leaked: a 535 t "fretting" fall on 915 appeared in a monthly production report but never in a rockfall report; the 9 October report said no-one was exposed, wrong if a bogger operator's near miss was that fall; the 980 level fall (below) was left out |
| Ground support | A system built on a "compressive arch" of short friction bolts was unsuited to friable rock in the block; after October 2005 that was obvious, yet no comprehensive independent reassessment was done |
| Independent review | The support designer audited his own design; management believed a second consultant had peer-reviewed it — he had not; a third advised that failure depth exceeded bolt length and cable bolts were needed, and nothing records how that disagreement was resolved |
| Restart and change | Stoping resumed before the mine's own restart plan was complete; no document records who decided, when, or on what analysis; no consultant was asked whether mining should resume in the block at all, a question that may have led to footwall drives or alternatives to the bund wall; the advised sequence was then reversed in April 2006 without a documented risk evaluation |
| Escalation | 21–23 April: a supervisor withdrew an operator from the 925 level (water through the backs, mesh distorted) and the underground manager never heard; a 30 t fall on the 980 level landed beside two miners, and the next shift re-entered without the underground manager being told or an inspection considered, contrary to the Ground Control Management Plan (GCMP) |
| Worker input | Rock-noise reporting flattened once real-time seismic monitoring went live in August 2005, some crews evidently treating the system as a substitute for reporting; Quinlan found crews' concerns were not sought in the post-October risk assessment |
| Accountability | WST had notice of three concurrent Responsible Officers under s 10; the underground manager was one, and took part in mine design and method decisions without being the final decision-maker |
| Regulator | Inspectorate staffing grossly inadequate; WST took no part in the October investigation or the restart decision — an abrogation of its statutory duty, though not shown to be causal |

The Coroner accepted that the event's timing and source were not reasonably
predictable, but asked whether the mine was managed for the real likelihood of
an event of up to ML 2.5 on any day. He could not find that anyone directly
contributed to the death, but found its likelihood would have been
significantly reduced by a thorough, rigorous risk assessment before restart,
which in all likelihood would have exposed the inadequate support and changed
the support or mining method. A late-April 3,000 t catch-up was not shown to
have compromised safety.

### Regulatory outcome
WST stopped mining by notice under s 38 of the 1995 Act, then under s 39
required an independently reviewed case for underground safety before restart.
Production resumed in 2007 under that Case for Safety, built on eliminating
exposure: workers no longer entered ore drives in the seismically active zone
during extraction. Stopes were drilled from footwall drives stood off at least
6 m, reinforced for an ML 2.5 event at 10 m with a factor of safety of 1.5,
and mucked by tele-remote loaders. The mine closed in 2012.

No prosecution followed (as at October 2026). Melick did not recommend one and
the Director of Public Prosecutions laid no charges. A 1995 Act charge was in
any case time-barred in practice: s 55 allowed 12 months from an inspector
becoming aware of the act or omission, less than the investigation took;
Melick and the Coroner urged two years. The WHS Act 2012 (Tas) s 232(1) now
allows the later of 2 years from the regulator's notice or 1 year after a
coronial report or inquest disclosing an offence; fresh evidence can extend
Category 1 cases (s 232(2)), and industrial manslaughter has no limit
(s 232(3)); see legislation.md §6.

The Coroner also recommended mining-specific regulation, notice of all
unplanned falls in supported, travelled ground, and one responsible officer
per mine. The Mines Work Health and Safety (Supplementary Requirements) Act
2012 (Tas), in force from 1 January 2013 (current as at October 2026),
requires a site senior officer (s 14), a health and safety management system
matched to the mine's risks (s 20), worker consultation on it (s 21) and mine
design accounting for geotechnical data and ground support (s 32). Officers
owe due diligence (WHS Act 2012 (Tas) s 27).

### AU/NZ practice lessons
- **Design for the credible event, not the forecast**. Unpredictable timing is
  the design premise, not a defence. As rock mechanics expert Professor Peter
  Kaiser put it, in burst-prone mines "the unexpected must be expected and
  multiple lines of defence other than ground support must be implemented".
  Write the design event into the critical risk standard and test every
  control (frameworks.md §7).
- **A warning event opens a restart gate**. Hold evidence of each item:

| Gate | Minimum evidence |
|---|---|
| Decision record | One named accountable decision-maker, date, analysis relied on, state of knowledge at the time, residual risk accepted |
| Recovery plan closed | Every step of the organisation's own plan complete, or waived in writing with reasons |
| Independent reassessment | Reviewer who did not design the control, given the full failure history and a written scope; advisers convened together, with each difference and the chosen option recorded |
| Elimination asked first | "Should people be in this zone at all?" put explicitly to the advisers before any upgrade to protection |
| Workers consulted | Exposed crews' concerns sought and recorded (model WHS Act ss 47–49; Vic OHS Act 2004 s 35; HSWA s 58; Tasmanian mines also Mines WHS (Supplementary Requirements) Act 2012 s 21) |
| Later change | Any departure from the assessed sequence or method goes through management of change with engineering sign-off |

- **Instrumentation must not displace people's reports**. Set Trigger Action
  Response Plan (TARP) red flags: an unplanned fall in a travelled area,
  deforming support, water through the backs, drill holes closing. Let
  supervisors withdraw crews; before re-entry, require inspection by the
  manager the ground control plan names and a recorded decision. Reconcile the
  rockfall register with production and survey records.
- **Notify near-miss falls**. The 30 t fall beside two miners was reportable
  under s 47 of the 1995 Act. A fall exposing anyone to serious risk is now
  generally notifiable, injury or not: model WHS Act ss 37–38
  (legislation.md §5), OHS Act 2004 (Vic) ss 37–38, HSWA ss 24 and 56. Mining
  regimes add triggers, such as Queensland high potential incidents (MQSHA
  1999 (Qld) s 195; CMSHA 1999 (Qld) s 198; sector-regimes.md §2). Set the
  internal bar at the Coroner's: every unplanned fall in supported, travelled
  ground.
- **Quiet is not clearance**. The crew found the night unusually quiet. Fixed
  re-entry periods (12 to 24 hours here) manage post-blast seismic decay, not
  fault slip, which can come at any time. Against slip, use support designed
  for the credible event (the Case for Safety used ML 2.5 at 10 m, factor of
  safety 1.5) and, stronger still, keep people out of the drive.
- **Production messaging sets the reporting climate**. No causal finding was
  made, yet catch-up targets and talk of closure are the conditions in which
  efficiency beats thoroughness (Hollnagel; frameworks.md §12). The mine's
  chief geologist later wrote that exclusion rules hold only if they are
  credible in both the crib room and the boardroom.
- **Do not lean on the regulator**. An absent inspectorate is not assurance;
  Pike River (2010) repeated the lesson (case-studies.md §5).
- **Entrapment response**. The survivors were found by a manager who walked in
  and called out: keep searching during recovery. Pre-plan remote recovery
  plant, low-vibration access, borehole supply and communications, and rescuer
  exposure limits (workplace-controls.md §2; investigation-advanced.md §3).

## 6. Cave Creek Platform Collapse (28 April 1995, NZ)

### Site context
The platform overlooked the resurgence where Cave Creek / Kotihotiho
re-emerges from the limestone karst, in Paparoa National Park near Punakaiki
on the West Coast of New Zealand's South Island. The Department of
Conservation (DOC) built it in a track upgrade: a timber deck about 3 m
square, cantilevered about 1.4 m beyond its front piles over a drop of about
30 m. Nobody treated it as high-risk. A conservation worker with a motor-trade
background drew the plans in 1992. Four DOC staff assembled the prefabricated
platform, with no plans on site, during a two-day volunteer work program in
April 1993 that about 18 staff attended. The concrete steps meant to act as
the counterweight were poured in April 1994 without the steel ties the plans
intended. DOC's only registered engineer first heard of it after it fell.

### Sequence of events
On 27 April 1995 a DOC visitor centre manager guiding a polytechnic group
thought she felt the front of the platform move. She told the group's tutor
and persuaded the new Punakaiki Field Centre Manager to come and look the next
day; the platform stayed open. On the track on 28 April she asked him to
"check out the flexing". At about 11:25 am he and 17 outdoor recreation
students from Tai Poutini Polytechnic walked onto the platform together. The
nails fixing the front bearer to its piles gave way, the deck levered up off
the rear bearer and the platform tipped into the chasm. Thirteen students and
the DOC manager died; four students survived with injuries. A "maximum 5" sign
ordered for the platform had been in the field centre workshop since January.

### Contributing factors (Commission of Inquiry)
The **Commission of Inquiry into the Collapse of a Viewing Platform at Cave
Creek** (Judge G S Noble, District Court) sat for 31 hearing days and reported
on 10 November 1995. It found one dominant cause (the platform was "not
constructed in accordance with sound building practice") and six secondary
causes, the pre-eminent being systemic failure.

| Level | Factor (Commission of Inquiry) |
|---|---|
| Design | No structural or geotechnical engineering input; plans did not detail the pile layout, the bearer-to-pile connection or the tie-down to the counterweight |
| Construction | No plans on site; bearers nailed to the piles; bolts delivered to site were never used; steel tie-down never installed; no qualified supervision |
| Structural capacity | Independent engineer's evidence: as built, the platform could not safely carry more than two or three people; at failure the critical front joint carried about 20 times its safe load |
| Procurement | Materials for a cantilevered structure were requisitioned and approved with no check against certified engineering plans |
| Statutory compliance | No building consent under the Building Act 1991; a retrospective application was considered but never lodged; no hazard identification at Punakaiki under the Health and Safety in Employment Act 1992 (HSE Act) |
| Inspection | No formal inspection during or after construction; the critical fixings were hidden beneath the decking, so a visual check would not have found them; the report of movement the day before did not close the platform |
| Project management | No department-wide system running from concept to post-completion inspection; whether a sound process was followed was "a matter of chance" |
| Resourcing | Department under-funded from its creation; constant reprioritisation; a culture of doing more with less |

Judge Noble found the department negligent: it failed to provide, maintain and
implement an adequate project management system and breached its Building
Act 1991 duties. "In all of those ways it acted unlawfully." It also failed to
comply with the HSE Act. Against that systemic failure, no individual or group
was singly or jointly responsible and no named staff member fell below the
standard of care; in an under-resourced department a tragedy was "almost bound
to happen". A State Services Commission review (December 1995) was narrower:
systems existed and would have worked if used as intended. Both describe the
gap between work-as-imagined and work-as-done (frameworks.md §4).

### Regulatory outcome
Nobody was prosecuted. The HSE Act (s 3) and the Building Act 1991 (s 5) bound
the Crown but barred prosecuting it, so DOC could not be charged. Its staff
had no such immunity: police investigated and in 1996 announced that no
charges would be laid. ACC barred compensatory damages (today Accident
Compensation Act 2001 (NZ) s 317; s 319 keeps exemplary damages); after
families threatened exemplary damages claims, the Crown paid them and
survivors nearly $3 million. DOC accepted responsibility when the inquiry
opened; the West Coast conservator resigned in December 1995 and the Minister
of Conservation gave up the portfolio in May 1996. The Commission recommended
ending Crown immunity in both Acts, a Building Act 1991 s 80 offence of using
an unconsented or uncertified public structure, a DOC project management
system, and funding for enough qualified staff. The Crown Organisations
(Criminal Liability) Act 2002 (NZ), assented 17 October 2002, answered the
first (s 6 as enacted: Building Act s 80 and HSE Act ss 49–50 offences).

| Question | 1995 | As at October 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Is the Crown bound? | Yes, under both Acts | Yes: HSWA s 5(1); a Crown organisation is treated as a separate legal person and can be a PCBU in its own right (s 5(2)) |
| Can a department be prosecuted? | No (its staff could be) | Yes, in its own name: HSWA s 6(2); 2002 Act s 6(1)(a)–(b) (Building Act 2004 and HSWA offences) |
| Can it be fined? | Not applicable | For HSWA offences, yes. The 2002 Act as enacted barred fines for every offence it covered (s 8(4)); HSWA s 232 narrowed the bar from 4 April 2016. It still covers Building Act 2004 offences |
| Who owes the officer duty? | No positive officer duty | The chief executive (HSWA ss 18(b), 44); a Minister of the Crown is not an officer (s 18(c)). Current law until the HSWA Amendment Act 2026 commences on 1 April 2027: check its officer due diligence changes (legislation.md §3.10) |
| Is opening an unconsented public structure an offence? | Not specifically; the Commission sought one | Yes: Building Act 2004 s 363 binds whoever owns, occupies or controls premises open to the public (s 362W) |

DOC reviewed all its structures, closed some pending upgrade or demolition,
adopted engineering design standards and appointed a national health and
safety manager. The NZ handbook for these assets is SNZ HB 8630:2004 *Tracks
and outdoor visitor structures* (current as at October 2026). The legislative
fix was narrow: Crown immunity went, the HSE Act regime stayed. Pike River
(19 November 2010) killed 29, and the Royal Commission (October 2012) again
found systemic failure, this time reaching the regulator; that produced
WorkSafe NZ and HSWA (case-studies.md §5; legislation.md §3.7).

### AU/NZ practice lessons
- **Systemic failure is a finding about organisational design, not an
  acquittal**. The department was still found negligent and unlawful. Each
  person who touched the platform did something that looked reasonable from
  where they stood; the gates that should have stopped them did not exist.
  When a board asks "who was responsible?", ask "which gate was missing?"
  (frameworks.md §12, Reason; contrast case-studies-anz.md §8).
- **Public agencies carry the same duties as any PCBU**. Model WHS Act s 10
  binds the Crown; those making or taking part in decisions affecting the
  whole or a substantial part of its undertaking are officers (s 247),
  Ministers are not. The same test applies to public authorities (s 252), but
  not to elected local government members acting as such (s 4). In Victoria
  the OHS Act 2004 binds the Crown as a body corporate (s 6) but has no
  positive officer duty: an officer is liable if a contravention is
  attributable to their failure to take reasonable care (s 144).
- **The duty reaches visitors**. In 1995 the polytechnic's HSE Act duty ran to
  its employee, not the students. Today a PCBU must ensure, SFAIRP, that its
  work does not put other persons at risk (HSWA s 36(2); model WHS Act
  s 19(2); Vic OHS Act s 23), and whoever manages or controls the structure as
  a workplace owes that to anyone there (HSWA s 37; model WHS Act s 20; Vic
  s 26). A school or operator bringing a group onto another PCBU's asset must
  consult, cooperate and coordinate (HSWA s 34; model WHS Act s 46).
- **Classify structures by consequence, not by size or cost**. Set written
  triggers for engineered design and independent review: any cantilever, any
  structure over a fall, any structure the public can crowd onto. Block the
  purchase order until certified drawings exist (HSWA ss 39, 43; model WHS Act
  ss 22, 26; Vic OHS Act s 28 for designers, ss 23 and 26 for the build, as
  s 31 covers plant only).
- **Build the chain into the asset system**. An engineer witness gave the
  Commission seven links that produce a safe structure. Each is a gate:

| Link (engineering evidence to the Commission) | Gate |
|---|---|
| Decision to build | Consequence classification at concept stage |
| Design loading | The full crowd the deck can hold, to the imposed floor and barrier actions of AS/NZS 1170.1:2002 (current as at October 2026); the Commission tested this platform against the 5.0 kPa that NZS 4203:1984 set for grandstands, movable seating and cantilevers |
| Designer competence | Named chartered or registered structural engineer; geotechnical input where foundations sit near a cliff or slope |
| Design review | Independent check before materials are ordered |
| Builder competence | Competent builder; certified drawings on site |
| Construction monitoring | Hold points before connections are covered; designer sign-off |
| Performance in service | Asset register; scheduled inspection; reports of movement acted on |

- **A load-limit sign does not fix an under-strength structure**. The
  engineering evidence opposed posted limits except on specific back-country
  structures; this platform would probably have failed under five people
  eventually. Build it to carry all who can stand on it.
- **Reported movement closes the structure**. Here a report of movement
  triggered a visit, not a closure, and the visit put 18 people on the deck.
  Write the rule down: any report of movement, deflection or cracking in a
  structure over a fall closes it until a competent person inspects, and
  frontline staff can close it alone. Officers must see that this process
  exists (HSWA s 44(4)(d); model WHS Act s 27(5)(d)).
- **Assurance has to reach the hidden detail**. A builder who visited a week
  earlier listed visible defects and assumed the fixings were sound; the
  as-built drawings repeated the error. Audit the design basis and hold-point
  records (inspections-audits-permits.md §3).
- **Under-resourcing is escalated, not absorbed**. The project was not short
  of money and could have been deferred; willing staff built it beyond their
  competence. Deferring or closing an asset is a legitimate control. Put the
  shortfall on the record: resourcing is an officer due diligence element
  (HSWA s 44(4)(c); model WHS Act s 27(5)(c); in Victoria, s 144).

## 7. Waterfall Rail Accident (31 January 2003)

### Site context
The State Rail Authority of NSW (SRA) ran Sydney's CityRail services; its
passenger functions passed to Rail Corporation New South Wales (RailCorp) on
1 January 2004, mid-inquiry. G7 was a four-car Outer Suburban Tangara running
Sydney Central to Port Kembla with a driver and a guard.

The engineered defence against driver incapacitation was a **deadman
system**: the driver held either a spring-loaded master controller or a foot
pedal, and a fully released or fully depressed pedal triggered an emergency
brake application. G7 had no vigilance device, and trackside train stops act
only at a signal at stop. Its data logger was fitted but not operating, so
the driver's last actions had to be reconstructed from other evidence.

### Sequence of events
Shortly after G7 left Waterfall station the driver suffered a sudden cardiac
arrest. He was using the foot pedal; the static weight of his legs held it
in the set position, so no brake application followed. The train ran away
down a falling gradient under green signals. Passengers were alarmed by the
speed; the guard, who had an emergency brake in his compartment, did not
apply it. At about 7:14 am G7 entered a 60 km/h curve at approximately
117 km/h and rolled over about 1.9 km south of the station. The driver and
six passengers died; the guard and the other 41 passengers were injured.

The response then failed in ways that could have added deaths had there been
a fire, or had attempts to re-energise the overhead succeeded:
- The Rail Management Centre (RMC) knew by 7:18 am that overhead power was
  lost and the driver silent, but triggered a major incident only at 7:32 am.
- A passenger's 000 call (7:24 am) was treated as suspicious, responders lost
  time to wrong locations and unknown access gates, and the injured guard
  was not authorised to use the Metronet radio in his compartment.
- Staff tried several times to reset the tripped circuit breakers; power was
  not isolated until 8:06 am.
- Passengers were trapped for about 30 minutes. A 1990 passenger containment
  policy had disabled or left off the Tangaras' internal emergency releases,
  with no risk analysis of trapping, and responders did not know the external
  release existed. No site control was set up until the rescue was over.

### Contributing factors (Special Commission of Inquiry)
The **Special Commission of Inquiry into the Waterfall Rail Accident** (Hon
Peter McInerney QC, *Special Commissions of Inquiry Act 1983* (NSW)) reported
on causes on 15 January 2004 and on safety management in January 2005. It
framed the accident in Reason's terms: four defences, all penetrated, against
a hazard that proper risk analysis would have predicted.

| Level | Factor (Special Commission) |
|---|---|
| Defence 1 — medical fitness | Periodic medicals had no predictive element for sudden incapacitation; GP examiners did not understand the driving task or the deadman, had no medical history, and there was no follow-up or occupational physician review. Autopsy found a 90 per cent blockage of a coronary artery |
| Defence 2 — deadman | The foot pedal was not fail-safe. Expert testing found an incapacitated driver over 110 kg could hold it set by the dead weight of his legs; the driver weighed 118 kg at autopsy. Around 40 per cent of Tangara drivers had a moderate to high ability, by body mass alone, to hold it set when incapacitated, and half of drivers at 105 kg or more could do so without effort. A driver trainer raised the defect with the project manager on 30 November 1988, who dismissed it without testing the pedal on a motoring train; consultant reports in 1992, 1994 and 1999 repeated the warning |
| Defence 3 — guard | Did not brake: not adequately trained for emergencies, never told of the pedal defect, conditioned to trains exceeding speed boards, fatigued by his roster, and reluctant to act in the driver's domain — the **authority gradient** between driver and guard |
| Defence 4 — signalling | Train stops only work at a signal at stop. Risk effort was concentrated on signals passed at danger (SPADs); a rollover under green signals was left uncontrolled |
| Design and change | No functional specification, design review or risk assessment of the driver safety system at procurement, and none in 1993 when Tangaras were modified for outer-suburban running, which raised the incapacitation risk |
| Risk management | Reactive: hazards that had not yet caused an accident were never analysed and near misses were not read as precursors. The pedal was reviewed for ankle strain (OH&S) but never for whether it would stop the train (system safety) |
| Safety culture | On-time running dominated. Drivers were reprimanded for reporting defects; incidents met a "blame and train" response; some drivers wedged the pedal with flag sticks, and SRA supervision failed to detect or overlooked this and routine speed-board breaches |
| Emergency preparedness | Exercises were rare: Blue Rattler (23 May 1997), whose debrief found no one knew where the external door releases were, then none until a 1999 Olympics exercise that neither tested passenger rescue nor coordinated the emergency services. Findings were not acted on, the containment policy was never reconsidered, and on the day the emergency services were not working to a coordinated response plan |
| Learning | Of the 95 recommendations (April 2001) from the same Commissioner's inquiry into the Glenbrook collision (2 December 1999, seven deaths), those on data loggers, communications, crew training and an independent investigator sat largely unimplemented (fleet data-logger installation was completed only on 16 June 2003), and the SRA did not learn from comparable events on other railways (Footscray, Victoria, June 2001, had exposed the same type of pedal and weak medical controls) |
| Regulator | The rail safety regulator (the Director-General of the Department of Transport, under the *Rail Safety Act 1993* (NSW)) accredited the SRA on a documented safety management system it had too few field officers to verify. The SRA never disclosed the pedal defect in its accreditation applications (in 1994 and 1996 a contravention of s 14(1)(a)) or its annual safety reports |
| Overall | The SRA's safety management system was grossly inadequate and the accreditation regime that passed it a failure; the SRA appeared to have failed to comply with the *Occupational Health and Safety Act 2000* (NSW). In a 2004 safety climate survey of 469 RailCorp staff, no group rated rail operations safe |

### Regulatory outcome
- **Investigator independent of regulator**. The *Transport Legislation
  Amendment (Safety and Reliability) Act 2003* (NSW) created the Independent
  Transport Safety and Reliability Regulator (ITSRR) from 1 January 2004,
  with the Office of Transport Safety Investigations (OTSI) as a division.
  The Commissioner rejected that model, as Glenbrook had: the regulator's own
  conduct is in scope of any serious investigation. He wanted the ATSB to
  investigate all NSW rail accidents, and backed national regulation if it
  gave NSW no lesser safety outcome (recommendation 120). Both came with the
  2013 national reforms (see sector-regimes.md §5).
- **Engineering defences**. Recommendation 31: every train to have at least
  two independent engineering defences against driver incapacitation. The
  Commissioner found an appropriate vigilance device on G7 would probably
  have prevented the accident; a Tangara retrofit began only in February
  2003. Recommendation 32 called for level 2 automatic train protection (ATP).
- **Health assessment**. Recommendation 57: periodic medicals for
  safety-critical staff with a predictive cardiac risk element and follow-up,
  examiners who understand the duties, occupational physician review, and
  standards reviewed at least every five years. Today Rail Safety National
  Law (RSNL) s 114 requires a health and fitness management program, and
  National Regulations reg 27 ties it to the National Transport Commission's
  National Standard for Health Assessment of Rail Safety Workers
  (pre-placement, periodic and triggered assessments by Authorised Health
  Professionals; 2024 edition in force from 11 November 2024, current as at
  October 2026).
- **Public tracking**. Recommendations 125 and 126 (of 127) required
  quarterly progress reports tabled in Parliament. ONRSR's last published
  report (No. 40) lists 177 items including sub-elements: 163 closed and
  verified, 6 closed subject to an approved program, 1 not applicable,
  5 rejected, 2 open (ATP; compatible train radio) as at March 2020.

### AU/NZ practice lessons
| Waterfall failure | Test to run in your own operation |
|---|---|
| Deadman held on by dead weight | For every operator-presence control (deadman, hold-to-run, seat switch): can an unconscious operator keep it made? Test it in the operating state, and inspect for wedges, tape and cable ties |
| One engineering defence | Where sudden incapacity of one person can kill others (trains, buses, cranes, winders, heavy mobile plant), is there a second, independent engineered defence? |
| Retrofit alarm mimicked the guard's bell | Risk-assess the new control itself before it goes live: RailCorp's Tangara vigilance alarm first sounded like the guard's all-clear bell, inviting a departure while passengers were still boarding |
| Medical with no predictive element | Where collapse could directly harm others, does the assessment target sudden incapacity, and does the examiner know the task? Use recommendation 57 as the checklist. Never let a risk-calculator score act as a hard cut-off: RailCorp's post-accident trigger for a stress ECG was a cardiac risk score of 22, the driver had scored 20, and the Commissioner endorsed examiner discretion instead. Pair with hazards.md §18 |
| Guard did not intervene | Does the second person have explicit authority to stop the job, the information to recognise trouble, and a rehearsed drill? Rail resource management (RRM) non-technical skills training (piloted with V/Line; ONRSR-hosted guideline, 2007) is not a Waterfall reform but addresses the same gap |
| Exercise findings ignored | Are exercise debrief actions tracked to verified closure like incident actions? See workplace-controls.md §2 |
| Prior inquiry unimplemented | Who independently verifies closure of investigation and audit actions, and does the board see the ageing? |

- **A documented system is not a control**. The SRA held accreditation on
  paper. RSNL s 99 mandates a safety management system and ONRSR tests it;
  making it real is the operator's duty and verifying it the officers' (RSNL
  s 55; model WHS Act s 27; HSWA s 44; see legislation.md §6).
- **Personal (OH&S) safety is not system safety**. The split Hopkins drew at
  Longford (case-studies.md §2) recurs: diligent OH&S work on the pedal's
  ergonomics sat beside an uncontrolled catastrophic risk. Rare catastrophic
  hazards need proactive analysis, independent verification of controls, and
  officers who confirm on evidence that the risk is eliminated or minimised
  SFAIRP (see frameworks.md §2, frameworks.md §7).
- **Authority gradients are designed in or designed out**. The inquiry
  traced the guard's inaction chiefly to role perceptions, training, fatigue
  and missing information; a second person is a defence only if the system
  makes them one.
- **Blame suppresses the signals**. The Commissioner called for a just
  culture in place of both blame and an incoherent no-blame policy (Reason;
  see frameworks.md §12). Flag sticks in the pedal were work-as-done
  evidence about the control that management never went looking for.
- **Outside the licensed transport regimes, the judgement is yours**. No
  medical standard is prescribed for most safety-critical roles; the primary
  duty applies (model WHS Act s 19; OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 21; HSWA s 36).
  Consult on any program (model WHS Act s 47; OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 35; HSWA
  s 58), treat results as sensitive health information, and set the standard
  at the role's inherent requirements (*Disability Discrimination Act 1992*
  (Cth) s 21A; see diversity-inclusion.md §2). NZ rail operators answer
  through the Railways Act 2005 (NZ) safety case.

## 8. The First Industrial Manslaughter Prosecutions

### Context and current position
Every Australian jurisdiction now has a discrete industrial manslaughter
offence (workplace manslaughter in Victoria). The provisions, fault elements
and maxima are in legislation.md §6 and are not repeated here. This section
records what prosecutors and courts have actually done with them, for board
papers and officer briefings. Position as at October 2026:

- **Convictions** are reported only in Queensland (WHS Act 2011 (Qld) s 34C
  and Coal Mining Safety and Health Act 1999 (Qld) (CMSHA) s 48C) and
  Victoria (OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 39G).
- **Charges that have not produced a manslaughter conviction**: the NT
  (Kalidonis, withdrawn; Williams, outcome not found), Victoria (Nordic
  Elevators, withdrawn on pleas to lesser offences) and SA (Nylastex,
  before the court).
- **No charge has been publicly reported** in NSW, WA, Tasmania, the ACT or
  the Commonwealth jurisdiction. The ACT has had an offence since 2004 with
  no prosecution reported.
- **NZ** has no discrete offence (legislation.md §3).

The table lists the six convictions found in public sources (four QLD, two
VIC) and the charged matters without one. Check the OWHSP court reports and
WorkSafe Victoria's prosecution results before quoting a count.

### Contributing factors (sentencing remarks and charges; allegations marked)
| Pattern | Where it shows | What to take from it |
|---|---|---|
| Person on foot inside the operating zone of mobile plant | Brisbane Auto Recycling, Owen, LH Holding, Narellan Pools — four of the six convictions | Forklift and crane interaction is the modal fact pattern, not an exotic one |
| Known hazard, absent or inadequate control | All six convictions: forklifts, cranes, work at height, strata | Five had no system for a textbook risk. Mastermyne (under appeal) turned on whether its strata control was adequate. A missing control and an inadequate one have both been put to a jury as negligence |
| Warnings on record | Narellan (doggers known to walk in front of the crane; operator's driving history); Kalidonis (two earlier unauthorised chain recoveries); alleged in Williams and Nylastex | An unanswered warning converts "should have known" into "knew". A repeated workaround is a warning |
| Owner at the controls | Owen and Hanna were driving the forklift; the Brisbane Auto Recycling directors supervised the yard and accepted by plea that they knew of the risk and consciously disregarded it | Where the officer runs the floor, the officer's conduct is the company's conduct |
| Paper without practice | Narellan had engaged WHS compliance consultants; the OWHSP court report still describes "flagrant and glaring oversights" | A manual and a consultant are not a control in the yard |
| Conduct after the incident | A Brisbane Auto Recycling director told paramedics the worker had fallen from the truck and named the wrong forklift driver to investigators; the other director gave the worker's daughter a false account. The company held no WorkCover policy | False accounts and statutory non-compliance are set out in the sentencing remarks; post-incident conduct is relevant to sentence |
| Size of defendant | Mostly owner-operated businesses; Mastermyne, a subsidiary of an ASX-listed group, is the first larger corporate convicted | Scale and listed-company governance did not prevent a jury conviction (under appeal as at May 2026) |

In every conviction liability rested on the duty holder's system of work,
not on the conduct of the person who died; in Owen and LH Holding the owner
was at the controls, so act and system were one failure. Below the
manslaughter line, the Kalidonis court rejected the argument that the worker
was responsible: he had not been adequately trained in towing.

### Regulatory outcome
**Negligence convicts; recklessness has not been needed.**

- **QLD** (s 34C; CMSHA s 48C): the prosecution must prove beyond
  reasonable doubt negligence about causing the death (legislation.md §6).
  Two juries have found it (Owen; Mastermyne, under appeal). Substituted
  s 34C (Act No. 44 of 2024) now covers the death of any individual to whom
  the PCBU has a health and safety duty, visitors and bystanders included
  (current text as at August 2026).
- **VIC** (s 39G): negligence means a great falling short of the standard
  of care a reasonable person would take, with a high risk of death, serious
  injury or serious illness; the conduct must contribute significantly.
- **Recklessness** has appeared only by plea or allegation. By pleading to
  Category 1 the Brisbane Auto Recycling directors accepted they knew of the
  risk and consciously disregarded it, yet drew lighter sentences than Owen:
  a risk-exposure offence has a far lower maximum and no causation element.
  The offence charged drove the difference, not the fault label.
- **The fall-back**: Kalidonis (NT) and Nordic Elevators (VIC) both had the
  manslaughter charge withdrawn. Kalidonis was convicted of Category 2 after
  a contested hearing; the Nordic companies pleaded to lesser offences.
- **Untested elsewhere**: no conviction yet under SA s 30A (gross
  negligence or recklessness), WA s 30A (knowledge and disregard) or the NT,
  NSW, Tas, ACT or Cth offences. WA's first prison term for a workplace death
  (MT Sheds, 2021) was for gross negligence under the former Occupational
  Safety and Health Act 1984 (WA), not industrial manslaughter.

**Sentencing signals (as at October 2026):**
- Fines have run from $1.5M to $7M. The corporate maximum is 100,000 penalty
  units, but the unit differs by Act. The WHS Act 2011 (Qld) unit is fixed
  at $100 (Penalties and Sentences Act 1992 (Qld) s 5(1)(d)), so the s 34C
  maximum is $10M: Brisbane Auto Recycling's fine was 30 per cent of it,
  Narellan's 15. CMSHA s 48C and OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 39G use the general
  indexed unit (`assets/penalty_units.json`).
- In LH Holding the Court of Appeal held $1.3M (about 7 per cent of the
  maximum) manifestly inadequate and said it would have fined $4.5M but for
  the plea. A company's financial circumstances do not immunise it, even
  where the fine is ruinous and unlikely to be paid.
- Only Owen was ordered into custody. Individuals have been reached as the
  PCBU itself (Owen, a sole trader), by Category 1 against directors
  (Brisbane Auto Recycling), by attribution under OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 144
  (Hanna; legislation.md §16) and, in SA, by charging directors under s 30A.
- Timing varies. Brisbane Auto Recycling was sentenced on pleas within 13
  months of the incident. Contested or complex matters took two to three
  years to charge (Kalidonis, Williams, Mastermyne, Nylastex) and close to
  five to sentence (Mastermyne, Risham). The offence typically carries no
  limitation period (legislation.md §6).

### AU/NZ practice lessons
- **Start with plant and people on foot**. This week, walk every area where
  forklifts, cranes or trucks share ground with pedestrians: separation,
  exclusion zones, a current traffic management plan, and a sighted
  high-risk work licence for every operator, directors included
  (case-studies-critical-risk.md §5).
- **Treat a warning as a clock**. A concern about a fatal-risk control (a
  bypassed interlock, a removed scaffold) needs an owner, a same-day interim
  control and a recorded close-out. A workaround is a warning too: the
  Kalidonis operator had tried the same chain recovery twice. Trigger a
  critical-control check and a learning review of why it was the easy path.
- **Verify the control, not the document**. Narellan had consultants and
  paperwork. Ask for field verification of fatal-risk controls, including
  whether each still suits the conditions (Mastermyne turned on strata
  control), and re-verify at every management of change.
- **Brief the board on the right fault element**. Of the offences in
  legislation.md §6, only WA s 30A needs knowledge and disregard. Negligence
  is enough in QLD and the Cth, a great falling short of the standard of care
  in VIC, gross negligence in NSW and SA (or recklessness in SA). Confirm the
  NT, ACT and Tasmanian element in the current Act before advising.
- **Officers: keep the due diligence record now**. Charges are built from
  what the officer knew and did before the event (legislation.md §6). Where
  the director also runs the floor (Owen, Hanna), the operational record is
  the due diligence record: licences sighted, traffic plans reviewed.
- **After a death, the first statements are evidence**. Give investigators
  accurate facts and no version before it is verified, preserve records, set
  up privilege, and consider separate lawyers for officers and the company.
  No enforceable undertaking is available for the offence
  (legislation.md §6; investigation.md §14; investigation-advanced.md §3).
- **Do not plan on insolvency or insurance as an exit**. Capacity to pay is
  weighed but does not override general deterrence (LH Holding). Most WHS
  Acts ban insuring a WHS monetary penalty (specialist-topics.md §5), but
  the bans are Act-specific. After the Mastermyne verdict the parent said
  its exposure may be mitigated by insurance; the Mining and Energy Union
  wants the law amended (as at May 2026).
- **Do not overstate the record**. MT Sheds, Kalidonis and Nordic Elevators
  are not industrial manslaughter convictions, Mastermyne is under appeal,
  and Dreamworld (case-studies.md §6) pre-dated the Queensland offence. The
  Williams trial was vacated on the regulator's late disclosure, with a
  stay sought. Re-check every matter's status before it reaches a board.

| Case | How accountability arrived | What changed after |
|---|---|---|
| Wittenoom (§2) | Civil courts from 1988 (*Barrow and Heys*; exemplary damages in *Rabenalt*); no inquiry | Town abolished (2007) and contaminated site classification (2008); *Wittenoom Closure Act 2022* (WA) |
| Montara (§3) | Commission of Inquiry (2010); A$510,000 fine on guilty pleas (2012); A$192.5 million class-action settlement (2023) | NOPSEMA from 1 January 2012; higher OPGGS penalties and new compliance tools (2013); WOMPs now under the 2025 Regulations |

### Reading across the cases
The inquiries and courts located the failure in the system of work. Cave
Creek found systemic failure and no individual singly or jointly responsible
(§6). Waterfall traced the guard's inaction chiefly to role perceptions,
training, fatigue and missing information (§7). The Beaconsfield Coroner
could not find that anyone directly contributed to the death, but found that
a rigorous risk assessment before restart would have significantly reduced
its likelihood (§5). Every industrial manslaughter conviction rested on the
duty holder's system of work, not on the conduct of the person who died
(§8). Use the cases to ask which gate was missing, not who failed.
