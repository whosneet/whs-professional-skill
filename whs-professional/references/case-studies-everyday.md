# Case Studies Reference — Everyday Incidents (Worked Examples)

Day-to-day incidents that WHS coordinators and managers actually deal with —
the cases that make up the bulk of incident registers and investigation
workload. Use as worked-example pedagogy for new investigators, as toolbox
content, and as ICAM calibration material. These are not catastrophes; they
are the steady stream of HiPo near misses, LTIs, and complaints that
constitute most of the work.

For major catastrophes (Longford, Texas City, Macondo, Pike River, Dreamworld,
Whakaari, Grenfell) used in board papers and strategic training, see
`references/case-studies.md`. For the deeper ICAM executive summary format
that one of these cases drives towards, see
`examples/04-icam-executive-summary-forklift-near-miss.md`. For
organisation-specific severity classification and investigation triggers, see
`references/company.md`.

All cases below are fictional but constructed from common AU/NZ incident
patterns. Names, locations, and reference numbers are illustrative.

### Risk-band legend and matrix note

Each case rates risk on a generic illustrative 5×5 matrix using lettered
bands:

| Band | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **A** | Extreme / Critical |
| **B** | High |
| **C** | Moderate |
| **D** | Low |

The Consequence (1–5) × Likelihood mapping used in the worked examples
below follows the canonical 5×5 matrix in `references/company.md` §3
(rating output Low / Moderate / High / Critical). For reference, on that
matrix Consequence 5 (Severe) × Possible = **Critical (Band A)**;
5 × Unlikely = High (Band B); 4 × Possible = High (Band B);
4 × Unlikely = High (Band B); 3 × Likely = High (Band B);
1 × Possible = Low (Band D).

**These ratings are illustrative.** Before relying on any rating in an
output, re-derive it on your organisation's actual risk matrix — the
band, the consequence descriptors, and the likelihood anchors may differ
from the generic matrix used here. See `references/company.md` §3 for the
worked organisational matrix, likelihood frequency anchors, and
consequence descriptors.

## 1. How These Cases Differ

The catastrophe set in `case-studies.md` exists to give board-level and
training audiences evidentiary depth on systemic failure. The cases below
exist for a different audience: WHS coordinators investigating last week's
incidents, supervisors running toolboxes on a Wednesday, and new
investigators trying to calibrate the depth of analysis expected for a
medium-severity event.

The everyday case is not a different *kind* of incident from a
catastrophe — the systemic factors are recognisably the same — but it sits
at a different end of the consequence distribution. The forklift near miss
that didn't make contact, the manual handling claim that lands as a sprain
not a permanent injury, the spill that didn't ignite, the slip that
sprained an ankle rather than breaking a hip. The mechanisms are the same;
the dice rolled the other way.

Two practical consequences:

**Investigation depth scales to potential, not actual.** An LTI 5-day
sprained-ankle case may have a potential severity of 4 (fall from a
ladder, broken hip, hospitalisation) — and the ICAM should treat it that
way. Conversely, a HiPo near miss with no injury is investigated against
its potential severity, not its zero-injury outcome. The cases below
demonstrate this discipline.

**Systemic factors at this severity range tend to be local.** Catastrophes
implicate national regulators, multi-decade engineering decisions, and
corporate strategic posture. Everyday incidents implicate the roster, the
SWMS, the supervisor who's been running this shift for three years, and
the equipment maintained on a 12-week cycle. The corrective actions are
correspondingly local and largely under management control at the site or
business unit level — which is why these cases are useful for coordinator
and supervisor capability building.

## 2. Case 1 — Forklift / Pedestrian HiPo

### Scenario
A 2.5-tonne counterbalance forklift travelling in reverse came within
1 metre of a pedestrian worker conducting a stocktake in an active
warehouse aisle. No contact, no injury, classified HiPo on potential
fatality outcome. Distribution centre, Sydney, mid-afternoon shift.

### What happened
Operator A was delivering a pallet to rack location 14-32, reversing
down Aisle 14 at approximately 6 km/h. Pedestrian Worker B was
performing a manual bin-location stocktake in the same aisle, on the
operator's right-rear blind side. Operator A heard a colleague in the
adjacent aisle shout, stopped, and saw the pedestrian for the first
time when the forklift was approximately 1 metre away. Both workers
reported within the hour.

The stocktake activity had been authorised verbally by the Shift
Supervisor at the start of the shift. The Site Traffic Management Plan
required physical exclusion zones (barriers + signage) for pedestrian
activity in active forklift aisles; for at least 18 months prior,
"small spot-checks" had been treated as outside the scope of the TMP
and authorised verbally without exclusion zones. The drift was widely
known and not challenged.

### Sequence of events

| # | Time | Event |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 13:30 | Stocktake activity verbally authorised by Shift Supervisor; no exclusion zone applied |
| 2 | 14:05 | Pedestrian B enters Aisle 14 to commence bin-location stocktake |
| 3 | 14:18 | Operator A receives system instruction to deliver pallet to location 14-32 |
| 4 | 14:20 | Operator A enters Aisle 14 in reverse to align with rack face |
| 5 | 14:22 | Forklift comes within 1 m of Pedestrian B; operator alerted by colleague shout; emergency stop |
| 6 | 14:30 | Incident reported; Aisle 14 closed |
| 7 | 16:00 | Incident raised in system; Preliminary Internal Incident Notification (PIIN) issued within 24 hours — an organisation-internal step, distinct from the statutory notifiable-incident notification to the regulator under WHS Act s 38 |

### PEEPO summary
- **People** — Operator A: current HRWL (LF class), 6 years on this
  site, no prior incidents. Pedestrian B: current stocktake
  competency, 2 years experience. Both alert; no fatigue indicators on
  pre-shift assessment.
- **Environment** — Aisle width 3.2 m (within site design spec);
  lighting adequate; ambient noise high due to adjacent racking
  activity, likely masking reverse alarm.
- **Equipment** — Forklift current on inspection; reverse alarm
  operational (~90 dB at 1 m, constant tone); no reverse camera or
  proximity sensor fitted.
- **Procedures** — Site TMP requires physical exclusion zone for
  stocktake in active aisles; verbal authorisation route taken did
  not trigger the requirement.
- **Organisation** — "Spot-check" verbal authorisation route had
  become routine over 18 months; Shift Supervisor and HSR both aware;
  not raised in safety walks or HSR meetings.

### ICAM analysis

| Absent or Failed Defences | Individual / Team Actions | Task / Environmental Conditions | Organisational Factors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical exclusion zone not applied | Operator did not perform 360° check before reversing | Aisle noise masked reverse alarm | Verbal "spot-check" authorisation has become routine workaround; TMP treated as optional for small activity |
| Reverse vision aid (camera, sensor) not fitted | Pedestrian entered aisle without confirming forklift activity status | Pedestrian on operator's blind side | No site-level review of TMP application in 18 months; HiPo precursors not identified in safety walks |
| Spotter not required by procedure | | Stocktake task requires sustained presence in aisle (>5 minutes) | Forklift procurement specification has not required reverse vision aids despite known blind-spot risk |

### Risk assessment
- **Actual**: Consequence 1 (no injury) × Likelihood Possible — Risk D (Low)
- **Potential**: Consequence 5 (single fatality) × Likelihood Possible — Risk A (Critical)

Potential rationale: 2.5-tonne counterbalance forklift in reverse at
~6 km/h with a pedestrian on the blind side creates a credible
worst-case outcome of fatal crushing. The 1 m clearance was achieved
by third-party intervention (colleague shout), not by the design of
the safety system.

### Corrective actions (mapped to factors)

| Action | Factor addressed | Control level |
|---|---|---|
| Apply physical exclusion zones (barriers + signage) to all stocktake activity in active aisles | Absent control: physical exclusion zone | Engineering / Administrative |
| Retrofit reverse cameras and proximity-warning systems to Sydney DC forklift fleet | Engineering control gap | Engineering |
| Rescind verbal "spot-check" authorisation; require written TMP-aligned authorisation for any aisle pedestrian activity | Organisational drift in TMP application | Administrative |
| Toolbox to all forklift operators and pedestrian workers on blind-spot risk and 360° check discipline | Individual action factors | Administrative |
| Quarterly TMP compliance audit added to assurance program; results to BU GM Zero Harm | Assurance gap | Administrative |
| Network-wide review of TMP application across DC portfolio | Pattern unlikely isolated to this site | Administrative |

*Control-level note: the barriers are an engineering control and the
signage administrative (matching `examples/04`). Classifying the
barriers as isolation would be equally defensible — substitution,
isolation and engineering share the same statutory tier under WHS
Reg 36(3), so nothing turns on the label.*

### Lessons
- HiPo classification on potential, not actual — the investigation
  resourcing follows the potential rating
- Procedural drift over 18 months without challenge is itself a
  finding — the disposition to challenge "the way we've always done
  it" is a measurable cultural attribute (see `frameworks.md` on
  Edmondson and psychological safety)
- Engineering controls (reverse cameras, proximity sensors) sit
  upstream of the human compliance demand — the existing reverse
  alarm is a low-tier engineering control that the noisy environment
  defeats

### Suggested training use
Worked example for new ICAM investigators. The case demonstrates the
HiPo discipline (investigate to potential, not actual), the
distinction between proximate cause (operator didn't see pedestrian)
and systemic cause (TMP drift), and the hierarchy-of-controls test on
corrective actions. A useful one-case toolbox for operations
supervisors covers all three points in 15 minutes.

> See `examples/04-icam-executive-summary-forklift-near-miss.md` for
> the full executive-summary format.

## 3. Case 2 — Manual Handling MSI

### Scenario
Aged-care worker on the morning shift, residential aged-care facility,
regional Victoria. Worker presents to GP with progressive lower back
pain; imaging shows L4-L5 disc protrusion. Workers compensation claim
lodged; claim accepted on a cumulative-injury basis. Worker placed on
suitable duties; long-term return to full duties uncertain.

### What happened
Worker C had been employed as a Personal Care Worker for 4 years at
the facility. The role involved assisting residents with transfers
(bed to chair, chair to commode, chair to walker) across the morning
care round — typically 12 to 15 transfers per shift on a 6-hour
shift. The facility had two electric stand-aid hoists and one ceiling
hoist over a single room; for most transfers, manual or pivot
transfers were used.

Over the preceding 18 months, Worker C had reported intermittent
lower back discomfort to the shift coordinator on at least three
occasions, including once via an internal hazard report. The
discomfort had not been formally assessed and had not generated
restricted duties or referral. In the week before claim lodgement,
Worker C had performed a manual transfer of a high-care resident
without assistance because the buddy worker was off the floor
attending to another resident; back pain that night prompted GP
attendance the following day, and imaging four days after the acute
episode showed the protrusion.

The claim was lodged eight days after the acute episode. The insurer
accepted the claim on cumulative-injury grounds — i.e., the injury
was characterised as the product of repeated exposure rather than a
single mechanism event. Suitable employment was offered as part-time
medication-rounds and administration duties; the worker has not
returned to direct care.

### Sequence of events

| # | Date | Event |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ~48 months prior | Worker C commences role; manual handling training provided as part of induction |
| 2 | ~18 months prior | Facility transitions from 3 buddy-shifts per round to 2 due to roster reduction |
| 3 | 14 months prior | Worker C reports intermittent back discomfort to shift coordinator (verbal) |
| 4 | 10 months prior | Hazard report logged: "back ache after morning round, third time this month" — no formal follow-up |
| 5 | 4 months prior | Worker C reports back discomfort again; advised to take Panadol and rotate posture; no formal duties change |
| 6 | 8 days prior | High-care resident transfer performed unassisted (buddy off floor); acute lumbar pain that evening |
| 7 | 7 days prior | GP attendance; imaging ordered |
| 8 | 4 days prior | Imaging shows L4-L5 protrusion |
| 9 | Day 0 | Workers comp claim lodged |
| 10 | Day 14 | Claim accepted; suitable duties commenced |
| 11 | ~4 months post-injury | Investigation review point: worker remains on part-time suitable duties; return to direct care unresolved |

### PEEPO summary
- **People** — Worker C: 4 years tenure, manual handling training
  current, no prior workers comp claims. Buddy workers rotate across
  shifts; pairing was reliable until roster reduction 18 months
  prior.
- **Environment** — Resident rooms vary in layout; some restrict
  hoist access. Floor surfaces generally compliant. Lighting
  adequate. Aged-care facility built 2008, retrofitted for some
  ceiling hoist installation in 2019.
- **Equipment** — Two stand-aid hoists for a 32-bed facility;
  ceiling hoist in a single room; slide sheets present but
  availability inconsistent across rooms; mechanical hoists report
  long set-up time per transfer (~3 minutes).
- **Procedures** — Site no-lift policy in place; resident risk
  assessments and care plans specify transfer methods; buddy
  requirement for high-care transfers in care plan.
- **Organisation** — Roster reduction 18 months prior reduced staff
  per shift; care plans not reviewed against new roster capacity;
  hazard reports closed without action.

### ICAM analysis

| Absent or Failed Defences | Individual / Team Actions | Task / Environmental Conditions | Organisational Factors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buddy worker not present at point of high-risk transfer | Worker proceeded with manual transfer rather than wait for buddy | Hoist set-up time creates time pressure to use manual alternative | Roster reduction 18 months prior not assessed against transfer load |
| Mechanical hoist availability insufficient for 32-bed facility | Worker did not request relief / refuse the transfer (cultural barrier) | Resident room layouts inconsistently accommodate hoist access | Hazard reports of back discomfort closed without formal investigation or care plan review |
| Sit-stand alternatives not available for all transfer types | | Time pressure across the morning round (15 transfers in ~3 hours) | No-lift policy not aligned with realistic transfer time budget on the reduced roster |
| Early symptoms not triaged into restricted duties or referral | | Cumulative shift load — 12 to 15 transfers per shift × 18 months | Manual handling training treated as the control, without engineering / scheduling support |

### Risk assessment
- **Actual**: Consequence 3 (LTI, ongoing claim, suitable duties) ×
  Likelihood Likely (given 18-month exposure pattern) — Risk B (High)
- **Potential**: Consequence 4 (permanent functional impairment, role
  loss) × Likelihood Possible — Risk B (High)
- **Classification note**: the potential Consequence 4 rating meets a
  potential-based HiPo definition (see `company.md` §5) — the case is
  a HiPo as well as an LTI; the two labels are not mutually exclusive

Potential rationale: Repeated cumulative lumbar disc injury at the
L4-L5 level commonly progresses to chronic pain and functional
limitation. The worker's return-to-direct-care prognosis is
uncertain at 4 months post-injury. The longer-tail outcome is
permanent role loss, not full recovery.

### Corrective actions (mapped to factors)

| Action | Factor addressed | Control level |
|---|---|---|
| Roster review against transfer load; restore buddy availability for all care plans specifying buddy transfer | Roster mismatch | Administrative |
| Procure additional mechanical hoists and slide-sheet kits sufficient for one per wing | Engineering control gap | Engineering |
| Trigger care-plan review for all residents on next 90-day cycle, with transfer method recosted against current roster | Care plan / roster mismatch | Administrative |
| Hazard report triage process — back-pain reports automatically generate an OT or physio referral and a duties review | Early symptom triage gap | Administrative |
| Build refusal-to-perform-transfer authority into care worker procedure (worker may decline a transfer in the absence of required buddy or equipment) | Cultural barrier to refusal | Administrative |
| Ceiling-hoist rollout plan over 24 months, prioritising high-care resident rooms | Engineering control upgrade | Engineering |

### Lessons
- "Manual handling training" alone is a known weak control for
  sustained cumulative-injury exposure — the evidence base treats
  training as a supporting administrative control, not as a stand-
  alone treatment (see `hazards.md` §17)
- Roster reductions that affect transfer load require a formal
  WHS impact assessment, not just a workforce-planning exercise —
  the same logic Hopkins applied to Longford's engineer relocation
  (see `case-studies.md` §2) applies to aged-care roster decisions
- Early-symptom reports are intelligence; closing them without
  formal triage converts intelligence into an undocumented
  exposure record
- Suitable employment design starts well before the claim — the
  facility's failure to offer restricted duties on the first three
  reports is what allowed the cumulative exposure to continue

### Suggested training use
Useful in two contexts. First, for RTW coordinator and HR
capability — the case illustrates the cumulative-injury claim
pathway and the role of pre-claim early-intervention. Second, for
aged-care and disability-services supervisor training — the case
demonstrates that no-lift policies require engineering and rostering
support to function, and that worker refusal authority is a
control, not insubordination.

> Cross-reference: workers compensation claim lifecycle and suitable
> duties in `references/compensation-rtw.md`; manual handling
> hierarchy of controls in `references/hazards.md` §17.

## 4. Case 3 — Psychosocial Complaint

### Scenario
A worker in a B2B sales team raised a formal complaint citing
"unreasonable workload, lack of support, and bullying behaviour from
my manager" after returning from two weeks' sick leave for
work-related anxiety and depression. Investigation conducted under
the organisation's grievance procedure; psychosocial risk assessment
triggered under the psychosocial provisions of the model WHS
Regulations (rr 55A–55D; in NSW, Part 3.2 Division 11).

### What happened
Worker D had been in the sales team for 3 years. Quarterly revenue
targets had been increased twice in the preceding 14 months
following a corporate restructure. The team manager had introduced
weekly individual review meetings and a public sales-performance
dashboard. Worker D had missed quarterly target in three of the last
four quarters.

Worker D's GP had certified two weeks' sick leave citing
"work-related anxiety and depressive symptoms." On return, Worker D
lodged a written complaint with HR alleging:
- Unreasonable workload following target uplift
- Lack of support — requests for training and account
  reassignment unanswered
- Bullying behaviour by manager — frequent public comments about
  Worker D's performance in team meetings and on the dashboard
- A specific incident where manager raised voice at Worker D in
  open-plan office, witnessed by two colleagues

The complaint did not allege discrimination on a protected
attribute, but the manager and Worker D were of different genders
and the workplace was male-majority. The Respect@Work positive duty
under SDA s 47C was considered in scoping the investigation.

A workers compensation claim for psychological injury was lodged
shortly before the investigation commenced; the claim went into the
insurer's extended liability decision window pending investigation
outcome.

### Sequence of events

| # | Date | Event |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ~14 months prior | Restructure; first quarterly target uplift; sales process changed |
| 2 | ~12 months prior | Manager appointed to team; introduces weekly individual reviews and public dashboard |
| 3 | ~8 months prior | Second target uplift; team headcount unchanged |
| 4 | ~5 months prior | Worker D submits a request for training on a new product category; no response on file |
| 5 | ~4 months prior | Worker D requests reassignment of two declining accounts; declined verbally |
| 6 | ~2 months prior | Public dashboard updated to traffic-light format; Worker D consistently red |
| 7 | 6 weeks prior | Open-office voice-raising incident; two witnesses |
| 8 | 4 weeks prior | Worker D commences 2 weeks' sick leave with medical certificate |
| 9 | 2 weeks prior | Worker D returns; lodges written complaint |
| 10 | 1 week prior | Workers comp psychological injury claim lodged |
| 11 | Day 0 | Investigation commenced; psychosocial risk assessment scoped |

### PEEPO summary
- **People** — Worker D: 3 years tenure, prior performance ratings
  meeting or exceeding expectations until the target uplift cycle. Manager:
  12 months tenure, no prior complaints. Two named witnesses to the
  voice-raising incident; two team-member observers of broader
  pattern.
- **Environment** — Open-plan office, dashboard prominently
  displayed near the team area; team meetings held in glass-walled
  meeting room visible to broader office.
- **Equipment** — Sales CRM, dashboard system, individual review
  documentation. Documentation of training requests and account
  changes is partial.
- **Procedures** — Performance management procedure in place;
  Code of Conduct addresses respectful behaviour; psychosocial risk
  policy adopted 12 months ago; grievance procedure used.
- **Organisation** — Two target uplifts without headcount or
  capability uplift; manager introduced dashboard and weekly reviews
  without HR or WHS consultation; no psychosocial risk assessment
  conducted at the time of restructure or target uplift.

### ICAM analysis

| Absent or Failed Defences | Individual / Team Actions | Task / Environmental Conditions | Organisational Factors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychosocial risk assessment not conducted at restructure or target uplift | Manager's review style was confrontational and public; not adjusted on observation | High demand (target) with low control (declining accounts not reassigned) | Restructure and target uplifts implemented without WHS impact assessment |
| Public dashboard design exposes individual performance to peers and broader office | Worker D did not formally escalate workload concerns prior to medical leave | Lack of role clarity following process change | Manager appointed without people-management training; no support framework for new managers |
| Training and account-reassignment requests not actioned and not formally responded to | | Job demands exceed capacity for sustained period | No leading-indicator monitoring of psychosocial hazards (turnover, sick leave, EAP utilisation) — not surfaced |
| Open-office voice-raising not addressed by witnesses or manager's manager | | Sustained underperformance with visible peer exposure | EAP utilisation rate increased in the team over 8 months but not reported as a signal |

### Risk assessment
- **Actual**: Consequence 3 (psychological injury, claim liability
  pending; ~2 weeks lost time, classified as occupational illness
  (OI) rather than LTI for statistical purposes — see `company.md`
  §5) × Likelihood Possible — Risk B (High)
- **Potential**: Consequence 4 (sustained psychological injury,
  role loss) × Likelihood Likely if conditions continue — Risk A
  (Critical)
- **Classification note**: potential Consequence 4 meets a
  potential-based HiPo definition (see `company.md` §5), so the case
  is flagged HiPo alongside its occupational-illness handling

Potential rationale: The same risk factor profile is present for
the rest of the team. EAP utilisation has increased over 8 months,
and team turnover is above the organisation's benchmark. The risk
is to the team, not just the complainant.

### Corrective actions (mapped to factors)

| Action | Factor addressed | Control level |
|---|---|---|
| Conduct formal psychosocial risk assessment across the sales team using SWA Code of Practice and survey-based instrument | Hazard identification gap | Administrative |
| Reset the dashboard format — remove public individual traffic-light reporting; retain individual-private metrics | Job demand / exposure design | Engineering (system) |
| Roster a workload review against the current target and headcount; adjust target, headcount, or both | Demand / capacity mismatch | Administrative |
| Manager development plan including HR-led coaching on review style and feedback delivery; second-line management oversight | Capability gap | Administrative |
| Mediation pathway offered to Worker D and manager (after substantive workload and dashboard changes are in place) | Relationship factor | Administrative |
| Add psychosocial leading indicators (EAP utilisation trend, sick leave by team, turnover, survey scores) to BU monthly safety pack | Indicator surveillance gap | Administrative |
| Document the Respect@Work positive duty consideration on file — the SDA s 47C duty applies even where the complaint is not formally about discrimination | Documented positive-duty discharge | Administrative |

### Lessons
- Psychosocial hazards under model WHS Regulations rr 55A–55D (in
  NSW, Part 3.2 Division 11) are managed
  with the same hierarchy of controls as physical hazards — the
  hazard identification, risk assessment, and SFAIRP discipline are
  not optional because the harm is psychological
- "Reasonable management action conducted in a reasonable manner"
  is not a defence to a psychosocial hazard finding — it is a
  workers compensation claim defence, with a different test
- Public performance exposure (dashboards, traffic lights, named
  call-outs) is a known psychosocial risk design — the engineering
  control is to redesign the system
- Manager appointment without people-management training is the
  most common upstream factor in psychosocial complaints — the
  organisational learning question is whether the
  manager-development pipeline is fit for purpose
- The Respect@Work positive duty applies even where the complaint
  is not framed as discrimination; a defensible record of
  consideration is required regardless of the complaint's framing

### Suggested training use
Best used as a manager-level case study, ideally for second-line
managers (managers of managers). The case demonstrates the
intersection of performance management, psychosocial hazard, and
workers compensation that most people-managers will encounter at
some point. For investigators, it illustrates the difficulty of
investigating psychosocial complaints fairly — the evidence is
soft, the witnesses are uncertain, and the complainant is on
medical leave during the process.

> Cross-reference: psychosocial hazard regulatory basis in
> `references/legislation.md` §9; psychosocial control framework
> in `references/frameworks.md`; psychological injury claim
> handling in `references/compensation-rtw.md`; Respect@Work
> positive duty in `references/legislation.md` §9.

## 5. Case 4 — Electrical Near-Miss

### Scenario
A cleaning contractor working alone in the main electrical room of
a mid-rise commercial building made unintentional contact with the
front face of an active 415 V main switchboard while cleaning the
wall above the board. No
electrical contact occurred — the board front panel remained
closed — but the contact was hard enough to dislodge a panel screw.
Reported by the contractor's supervisor on the following shift.
HiPo classification on the basis that loose panel hardware combined
with cleaning fluid exposure could have produced flashover.

### What happened
Worker E, a contracted cleaner with 18 months at the building,
was completing a high-dust clean in the main electrical room of a
mid-rise commercial building. The room contained the building's
main switchboard (415 V, 800 A) and ancillary distribution boards.
The cleaning work order specified "high dust clean — walls and
ceiling — quarterly," issued by the FM contractor's CAFM system.

The work order did not specify approach distance to the switchboard,
did not require isolation, and did not flag the switchboard as a
restricted-access asset. Worker E used a microfibre mop on an
extension pole to clean the wall directly above the switchboard.
The pole struck the front of the switchboard panel hard enough to
dislodge a panel-retaining screw, which fell to the floor.

Worker E reported the dropped screw to the cleaning supervisor at
shift end. The cleaning supervisor reported it to the FM site
manager on the following morning. The FM site manager attended,
confirmed the switchboard remained closed (no exposed live parts),
and raised the incident in the FM system as a HiPo.

Investigation found Worker E held no electrical competency,
had not received site-specific induction covering the electrical
room, and had not been provided the building's low-voltage
electrical safe-work procedures or restricted-access procedures
(at 415 V the switchboard is low-voltage plant; the governing
documents are the site's LV electrical safe-work procedures, not a
high-voltage regime). The contract specification required all
electrical-room cleaning to be performed under permit, supervised
by a licensed electrical worker with a safety observer in place;
that requirement had not flowed into the CAFM work order.

### Sequence of events

| # | Time | Event |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ~2 years prior | Building owner specifies in head contract that electrical-room cleaning requires permit + electrical supervision |
| 2 | ~18 months prior | Cleaning subcontractor onboarded; Worker E commences cleaning role |
| 3 | ~12 months prior | Quarterly electrical-room dust clean added to CAFM work order schedule; flow-through from head contract specification fails — work order does not specify permit or supervision |
| 4 | Day -1, 22:30 | Worker E receives quarterly work order; commences clean unsupervised |
| 5 | Day -1, 23:10 | Extension pole strikes switchboard panel; screw dislodged |
| 6 | Day -1, 23:25 | Worker E reports to cleaning supervisor at shift end |
| 7 | Day 0, 07:00 | Cleaning supervisor reports to FM site manager |
| 8 | Day 0, 08:15 | FM site manager attends; switchboard inspected; HiPo raised |
| 9 | Day 0, 10:00 | Switchboard locked out pending detailed inspection by licensed electrician |

### PEEPO summary
- **People** — Worker E: 18 months at the building, current
  general construction induction, cleaning competency only, no
  electrical competency or permit training. Cleaning supervisor:
  3 years; not present during the task.
- **Environment** — Electrical room, locked, accessed by master
  key held by cleaning contractor; lighting from corridor (low);
  switchboard at standard height (~1.2 m to top of panel); no
  approach-distance markings on floor.
- **Equipment** — Microfibre mop with 1.8 m extension pole;
  cleaning fluid (proprietary detergent, water-based) in 500 mL
  trigger spray; main switchboard 800 A 415 V; panel retained by
  screws.
- **Procedures** — Head contract specifies permit + electrical
  supervision for electrical-room cleaning; CAFM work order issued
  to subcontractor did not include this requirement; no SWMS
  prepared for the task; no permit issued.
- **Organisation** — Procurement and operations interface failed
  to translate head-contract specification into CAFM work order
  configuration; subcontractor management did not detect the gap
  in periodic audit cycles.

### ICAM analysis

| Absent or Failed Defences | Individual / Team Actions | Task / Environmental Conditions | Organisational Factors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Permit-to-work for electrical-room access not issued | Worker proceeded with task without questioning approach distance to switchboard | Lighting in electrical room marginal; cleaner unable to assess approach distance accurately | Head contract specification (permit + supervision) did not flow through to CAFM work order configuration |
| Electrical supervision not present | Cleaning supervisor not on site during task; supervision arrangement absent in shift design | Cleaning fluid in proximity to switchboard panel (flashover risk if panel had been open) | Subcontractor competency management did not identify electrical-room access as a gap in induction |
| Restricted-access marking on switchboard absent (no approach-distance line, no warning of contact prohibition) | | Extension pole 1.8 m increased reach above visual zone | Quarterly assurance audit of CAFM work-order configuration not performed |
| Site-specific induction for electrical room not provided | | Switchboard panel retention by screws (vs latches with interlock) is a passive defence at risk of loosening over time | Contractor management cycle did not include a periodic review of high-risk work orders against current contract specifications |

### Risk assessment
- **Actual**: Consequence 1 (no injury, no exposed live parts) ×
  Likelihood Possible — Risk D (Low)
- **Potential**: Consequence 5 (single fatality — an arc-flash
  event at an 800 A LV switchboard is credibly fatal to a lone
  worker at ~1 m) × Likelihood Unlikely — Risk B (High)

Potential rationale: A pole strike sufficient to dislodge a panel
screw is sufficient to compromise the panel's containment of an
arc-flash event. The cleaner was working alone at ~1 m from the
panel front. The flashover scenario, while requiring multiple
conditions to coincide, is foreseeable and well-documented in
electrical incident literature.

### Corrective actions (mapped to factors)

| Action | Factor addressed | Control level |
|---|---|---|
| Reconfigure CAFM work order template to require permit + electrical supervision flag for all electrical-room tasks | Procurement-to-operations specification failure | Engineering (system) |
| Quarterly assurance audit of CAFM high-risk work orders against current contract specifications | Assurance gap | Administrative |
| Apply restricted-access marking to switchboard front: approach-distance line + signage prohibiting contact | Restricted-access marking gap | Engineering / Administrative |
| Reissue subcontractor induction with site-specific module covering electrical room and restricted-access assets | Site induction gap | Administrative |
| Permit-to-work issued for all future electrical-room cleaning, with a licensed electrical worker supervising and a safety observer in place | Permit-to-work gap | Administrative |
| Network-wide review of CAFM work-order configurations against head contract specifications for high-risk asset access | Pattern likely beyond this site | Administrative |
| Investigate retrofit of interlocked panels to switchboards in scope where current panel retention is screw-based | Passive defence design upgrade | Engineering |

### Lessons
- Permit-to-work systems fail when the specification doesn't reach
  the worker — the work order is the operational artefact, and gaps
  in the procurement-to-CAFM-to-work-order chain leave workers
  performing high-risk tasks with none of the specified controls
  in place
- Subcontractor management depth must include the work-order
  configuration as well as the contract specification — the gap is
  almost never visible in the contract itself
- Restricted-access assets need *physical and visual* markings, not
  just procedural restrictions — workers cannot comply with rules
  they are not made aware of at the point of work
- "Routine" cleaning tasks in proximity to high-energy assets
  warrant treatment as high-risk work — the energy is not visible
  but the consequences are catastrophic

### Suggested training use
Useful for FM, building operations, and contractor management
audiences. The case demonstrates the disconnect between contract
specification and operational work-order — a pattern that recurs
across multi-PCBU portfolio contracts. For investigators, it
illustrates the rigour of investigating a no-injury incident at
potential severity 5 — the analysis is the same as for a fatality
even though the outcome was no harm.

## 6. Case 5 — Slip-Trip-Fall

### Scenario
A kitchen hand in a 120-cover restaurant slipped on a wet section
of tiled floor near the deep fryer station and rolled an ankle.
Initial GP attendance, soft-tissue sprain, 5-day LTI, returned to
modified duties on day 6. Common-looking incident on the register —
the systemic factors are the part most often missed.

### What happened
Worker F was on the Saturday evening dinner shift in the main
kitchen of a mid-sized hospitality venue. At approximately 19:45,
during the peak service period, Worker F walked from the salad
station past the deep fryer station to collect plates from the pass.
On the section of floor between the fryer station and the pass,
Worker F's right foot slipped on accumulated oil-and-water residue;
Worker F's right ankle rolled and Worker F went down on one knee.

Worker F reported the incident to the head chef immediately;
service continued. Worker F finished the shift with discomfort but
no immediate impairment. Sunday morning ankle swelling prompted GP
attendance; sprain confirmed; 5 days off work; modified duties
(no extended standing) for a further 2 weeks.

Investigation found the deep fryer station drainage grate had been
partially blocked since the previous Tuesday's deep-clean; condensate
and oil from the fryer were pooling on the floor and being tracked
across the section to the pass. The cleaning schedule provided for
hourly spot-mopping by the kitchen porter; on Saturday evening, the
porter was assisting with dishwasher recovery from a backup and had
not spot-mopped since 18:30. Kitchen flooring was a non-slip tile
specified at the original fit-out (2019); the non-slip coating had
worn substantially in high-traffic sections, last assessed in 2023.

Worker F was wearing branded kitchen footwear with slip-resistant
soles, supplied as part of the uniform — marked SRC under EN ISO
20345 (slip resistance tested to the EN ISO 13287 method; note the
SRA/SRB/SRC marking scheme was superseded in the 2022 revision of
the standard, which replaced the three SRA/SRB/SRC marks with a
single mandatory "SR" slip test). In AU/NZ workplaces, footwear is
more commonly specified to AS/NZS 2210 — check the actual standard
marked on the supplied footwear rather than assuming the EN ISO
series.
Sole tread on the right shoe was approximately 70% worn; the
uniform policy provided for free replacement on request, but
Worker F had not requested replacement.

### Sequence of events

| # | Time / Date | Event |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 days prior (Tuesday) | Kitchen deep-clean; drainage grate disturbed and partially blocked on reinstatement |
| 2 | 3 days prior (Wednesday) | Condensate pooling first noted by porter; mentioned to head chef verbally; no action |
| 3 | Saturday, 17:30 | Worker F commences dinner shift |
| 4 | Saturday, 18:30 | Last spot-mop of the fryer section before incident |
| 5 | Saturday, 19:30 | Dishwasher backup; porter pulled to assist; no scheduled spot-mop |
| 6 | Saturday, 19:45 | Worker F slips; ankle rolled |
| 7 | Saturday, 22:30 | Service ends; head chef raises incident in venue system |
| 8 | Sunday, 09:00 | GP attendance; sprain confirmed |
| 9 | Monday | LTI commenced; investigation scoping |
| 10 | Following Saturday | Worker F returns; modified duties (no extended standing) |

### PEEPO summary
- **People** — Worker F: 14 months in role, food safety
  competency, no prior incidents. Head chef: 4 years, recently
  promoted, no prior people-management responsibility. Porter:
  6 months, casual.
- **Environment** — Kitchen floor: non-slip tile, worn in
  high-traffic sections; lighting adequate; ambient temperature
  high during service (~32°C); fryer station produces continuous
  steam and oil aerosol; spacing between stations narrow (~1.0 m).
- **Equipment** — Drainage grate: partially blocked since
  Tuesday's deep clean; deep fryer in good order; spot-mop kit
  available; non-slip footwear supplied as part of uniform with
  free-replacement policy.
- **Procedures** — Hourly spot-mop schedule during service;
  cleaning checklist for deep-clean; footwear replacement policy
  (in handbook); incident reporting policy.
- **Organisation** — Saturday peak service is the highest-load
  shift; porter is single point of failure for both
  dishwashing-recovery and spot-mopping; head chef recently
  promoted without people-management training; no
  housekeeping-during-service KPI tracked.

### ICAM analysis

| Absent or Failed Defences | Individual / Team Actions | Task / Environmental Conditions | Organisational Factors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spot-mop schedule not maintained during peak | Worker F walked through the section without pausing to check surface | Drainage grate partially blocked since Tuesday; condensate accumulation | Porter is single point of failure for two non-substitutable tasks (dishwashing recovery and spot-mopping) |
| Drainage grate not inspected after deep clean | Worker F's right shoe sole 70% worn; replacement not requested | High service load + narrow station spacing creates frequent traverses of the hazard zone | Worn non-slip tile coating last assessed 2023; no re-coating cycle scheduled |
| Worn floor coating not re-coated | Worn-shoe replacement not raised by head chef despite tread visibility | Lighting marginal in the section between stations | Verbal-only escalation of condensate pooling on Wednesday — no formal logging |
| Footwear inspection not embedded in pre-service routine | | Heat and steam reduce floor-surface friction further | Head chef promoted without people-management training; housekeeping-during-service not part of operational KPIs |

### Risk assessment
- **Actual**: Consequence 3 (LTI 5 days, recoverable) ×
  Likelihood Likely (given the conditions, a slip in this section
  was foreseeable) — Risk B (High)
- **Potential**: Consequence 4 (serious fall, possible fracture
  on hot equipment or against fryer side) × Likelihood Possible —
  Risk B (High)
- **Classification note**: potential Consequence 4 meets a
  potential-based HiPo definition (see `company.md` §5) — the case is
  both an LTI and a HiPo

Potential rationale: A slip immediately adjacent to a deep fryer
operating at ~180°C oil temperature, with a narrow gap to the next
station, carries credible scenarios of fracture, contact burn from
the fryer body, or scalding from oil if the slip caused the
worker to grab the fryer rim. The actual outcome (rolled ankle) is
the lower end of the distribution.

### Corrective actions (mapped to factors)

| Action | Factor addressed | Control level |
|---|---|---|
| Re-inspect and reinstate drainage grates after every deep clean; sign-off by head chef before service resumes | Drainage failure | Administrative |
| Re-coat non-slip floor in high-traffic kitchen sections; bring coating cycle to annual; add visual inspection to monthly site walks | Worn coating | Engineering |
| Schedule spot-mopping as a defined role on the shift roster, not as a porter back-fill; protect the role during dishwashing recovery | Single point of failure | Administrative |
| Add a pre-service footwear visual check to the shift opening routine; head chef confirms before service commences | Worn footwear | Administrative |
| Provide head-chef development including people-management and housekeeping-during-service KPI accountability | Capability gap | Administrative |
| Install matting or anti-slip flooring overlay at the fryer-to-pass section as an additional engineering control | Engineering control upgrade | Engineering |
| Add housekeeping-during-service to weekly operational reporting (peak shift compliance KPI) | KPI / surveillance gap | Administrative |

### Lessons
- "Slipped on a wet floor" is the classic seemingly-trivial
  incident — the systemic factors are the cleaning schedule, the
  flooring specification and maintenance, the staffing model, the
  footwear replacement cycle, and the supervisor capability. The
  same incident in a different system would not have happened
- Single-point-of-failure staffing for non-substitutable tasks is
  visible in advance — a roster review is a leading-indicator
  control
- Worn non-slip floor coating has a definite life; the absence of
  a re-coating cycle is an organisational learning failure
- Footwear policies that depend on worker self-request will degrade
  over time — a pre-service visual check is a cheap administrative
  upgrade

### Suggested training use
Excellent first case for new investigators because the analysis is
counter-intuitive — most read this as "worker slipped, it
happens." The ICAM forces eight to ten contributing factors that
all sit upstream of the slip. For supervisor and head-chef
training, it builds the discipline of housekeeping-during-service
as a recognised operational control.

> `references/hazards.md`; LTI claim handling in
> `references/compensation-rtw.md`.

## 7. Case 6 — Hazardous Chemical Decant

### Scenario
A worker decanting concentrated alkaline cleaner from a 20 L drum
into a 5 L secondary container spilled approximately 2 L on the
floor. Minor skin contact to the worker's forearm; flushed
immediately; no injury. Classified HiPo on the basis that the
chemical (pH > 13) is a category 1A skin corrosive and the
secondary container was unlabelled; a more significant spill
volume or different contact pathway would have produced serious
chemical burns.

### What happened
Worker G was decanting a concentrated alkaline cleaner (sodium
hydroxide based, ~30% w/w) from a 20 L drum into a 5 L plastic
jerry can for transfer to an industrial cleaning machine.
Decanting was performed in the chemical storage area at the rear
of the workshop, on a flat concrete floor with no bunding.

Worker G was wearing chemical-resistant nitrile gloves, safety
glasses, and standard cotton coveralls; no face shield, no apron,
no chemical-rated boots. The 20 L drum did not have a decanting
spout; the chemical was poured by manual tipping. A flexible funnel
was used to direct the flow into the smaller container.

Approximately two-thirds of the way through the decant, Worker G
adjusted grip on the drum; the drum tipped further than intended;
approximately 2 L of cleaner overflowed the funnel and the
secondary container, splashing onto the concrete floor and onto
Worker G's right forearm and lower coveralls.

Worker G immediately moved to the emergency eyewash and safety
shower in the adjacent corridor, flushed the affected forearm for
15 minutes, and removed the coveralls. The supervisor was notified
during the flush; the area was barricaded.

Investigation found the decanting area had no bunding, no
mechanical decanting equipment (drum pump or tipper), no spill kit
within the decanting area itself (kit was on the far side of the
workshop), and no labels on the secondary container (the jerry can
was a generic plastic container with no markings). The SDS
mandated face shield and apron for any decanting activity; the
SWMS for the task referred to "appropriate PPE" without
specification. The skin contact was flushed within 90 seconds and
no skin damage occurred; a 30-second delay or a different contact
pathway (eyes, large skin area, ingestion) would have produced
serious injury.

### Sequence of events

| # | Time | Event |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ~3 years prior | Decanting practice established at this workshop; no bunding, manual decanting, generic secondary containers |
| 2 | ~12 months prior | Workshop SWMS reviewed; PPE described as "appropriate" rather than specified |
| 3 | ~6 months prior | New worker (Worker G) inducted; SDS access available but not stepped through for decanting |
| 4 | Day 0, 09:15 | Worker G commences decanting in chemical storage area |
| 5 | Day 0, 09:22 | Worker G adjusts grip; drum tips further than intended; ~2 L overflows |
| 6 | Day 0, 09:23 | Worker G splashed on forearm and lower coveralls; moves to safety shower |
| 7 | Day 0, 09:24 | Worker G commences forearm flush; supervisor notified |
| 8 | Day 0, 09:39 | Flush concludes; coveralls removed; area barricaded |
| 9 | Day 0, 11:00 | Spill cleaned by competent operator with neutralising kit; floor neutralised and rinsed |
| 10 | Day 0, 14:00 | Incident raised in system; HiPo classification confirmed |

### PEEPO summary
- **People** — Worker G: 6 months tenure, general workshop
  induction; SDS access provided but not stepped through;
  chemical handling competency not formally assessed.
- **Environment** — Chemical storage area, concrete floor, no
  bunding, adjacent to corridor with eyewash and shower (~15 m).
  Ventilation general workshop extract; no local exhaust over the
  decanting area.
- **Equipment** — 20 L drum without integrated decanting spout;
  generic 5 L plastic jerry can (unlabelled); flexible funnel;
  no drum pump or tipper; spill kit located across the workshop
  (~25 m away).
- **Procedures** — SWMS specifies "appropriate PPE"; SDS specifies
  face shield, chemical apron, chemical-rated boots; pre-task
  risk assessment (Take 5) was completed on the day but did not
  identify the PPE gap.
- **Organisation** — Decanting practice unchanged for ~3 years;
  no chemical-handling-specific competency required; secondary
  containers not subject to labelling regime; bunding not present
  despite multiple decanting activities at the site.

### ICAM analysis

| Absent or Failed Defences | Individual / Team Actions | Task / Environmental Conditions | Organisational Factors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bunding not present at decanting location | Worker did not check SDS PPE requirement before task | Manual tipping of a 20 L drum is physically demanding and high-risk for grip slip | SWMS specifies "appropriate PPE" rather than chemical-specific PPE |
| Mechanical decanting equipment (drum pump, tipper) not provided | Worker did not reference labelling requirement for secondary container | Decanting on a flat unbunded floor allows spread of spill | Secondary container labelling regime not in place |
| Spill kit located ~25 m from decanting area (outside the workspace) | PPE used was the workshop default, not the SDS-mandated set | Drum without integrated decanting spout requires manual tipping | Decanting practice unchanged for ~3 years; not subject to risk review |
| Secondary container unlabelled (compounds the hazard for downstream users) | | Concentrated 30% sodium hydroxide is a category 1A skin corrosive | Chemical-handling competency not formally required for the role |
| Face shield, chemical apron, chemical boots not required by SWMS | | | Spill response equipment placement decision (kit central, not local) prioritised tidiness over response time |

### Risk assessment
- **Actual**: Consequence 1 (no injury, immediate flush) ×
  Likelihood Likely — Risk C (Moderate)
- **Potential**: Consequence 4 (serious chemical burn, possible
  permanent skin or eye damage) × Likelihood Possible — Risk B
  (High)

Potential rationale: Concentrated sodium hydroxide (pH > 13) is a
category 1A skin corrosive — direct contact causes immediate
tissue damage; eye contact can produce permanent blindness within
seconds. The actual outcome depended on a 90-second flush by a
worker who happened to be close to the shower. A 30-second delay,
contact to the eyes, or a larger spill volume would have produced
serious harm.

### Corrective actions (mapped to factors)

| Action | Factor addressed | Control level |
|---|---|---|
| Install bunded decanting station with mechanical drum pump or tipper | Bunding + manual handling | Engineering |
| Reposition spill kit to within 5 m of the decanting area; second kit if needed | Spill response time | Administrative |
| Revise SWMS to specify SDS-mandated PPE for each chemical (face shield, chemical apron, chemical-rated boots for this product) | PPE specification gap | Administrative |
| Implement secondary container labelling regime — every container labelled with product name, hazards, and decant date before use | Labelling gap | Administrative |
| Add chemical-handling competency assessment to role induction; stepped SDS walk-through with sign-off | Competency gap | Administrative |
| Audit other decanting activities at the site against this finding; extend across portfolio if pattern confirmed | Pattern likely beyond this task | Administrative |
| Substitute the concentrated 30% product with a lower-concentration alternative where the application allows (e.g., 10% or pre-diluted) | Substitution opportunity | Substitution |

### Lessons
- Decanting is the most common hazardous-chemical incident
  pathway — engineering controls (bunding, drum pumps, integrated
  spouts) are the most reliable mitigation
- "Appropriate PPE" is a defective procedural instruction —
  PPE must be specified by reference to the chemical, not by
  generic language. The SDS does the specification work; the
  SWMS must transcribe it
- Secondary container labelling is mandatory under model WHS Reg
  335 — unlabelled containers compound the hazard for every
  downstream user
- Spill response time is a function of equipment location, not
  just equipment presence — a spill kit 25 m away is
  effectively not there for the first 60 seconds
- The substitution question (lower-concentration product) should
  be asked at procurement — moving to a lower-hazard product
  reduces ongoing control burden across the lifecycle

### Suggested training use
Best used as a workshop or laboratory toolbox case, or as a
chemical-management capability case for new safety advisors. The
case demonstrates the difference between "the SDS exists" and
"the SDS is used," and illustrates the SDS-to-SWMS-to-work
specification chain that often breaks at the SWMS step. For
investigators, it's a useful calibration on HiPo classification of
no-injury chemical incidents.

> Cross-reference: hazardous chemicals operational detail in
> `references/hazards.md` §13; SDS, labelling, and segregation
> requirements under model WHS Regs Part 7.1; chemical spill
> response in `references/environment.md`.

## 8. Case 7 — Fatigue Vehicle

### Scenario
A 12-hour night-shift worker collided with a stationary vehicle at
a roundabout 22 minutes after leaving site at end of shift. Minor
property damage to both vehicles, low-speed impact, no injury.
Worker reported the collision to the supervisor on the following
shift. The compensation position is jurisdiction-specific:
Queensland retains journey claims (*Workers' Compensation and
Rehabilitation Act 2003* (Qld) s 35), so an injury on a direct
drive home would have been prima facie compensable — whereas NSW and
Victoria restrict ordinary-commute journey claims under their
workers compensation schemes. The WHS question is separate either
way: whether the work design contributed.

### What happened
Worker H was on the night shift (19:00–07:00) at a
manufacturing site in suburban Brisbane. The roster was a 4-on /
4-off pattern with 12-hour shifts; Worker H was on the fourth
consecutive night shift. Shift handover concluded at 07:05; Worker
H left the site car park at 07:15 and commenced the ~30 km drive
home.

At approximately 07:37, while approaching a roundabout
approximately 11 km from home, Worker H failed to brake adequately
and ran into the rear of a stationary vehicle waiting to enter the
roundabout. Speed at impact was estimated at ~25 km/h. Both
vehicles sustained rear/front damage; airbags did not deploy;
neither driver reported injury. Police attended; Worker H was
breath-tested with a zero reading; insurance details were
exchanged.

Worker H reported the collision when commencing the next rostered
shift (four days later) and the compensation position was discussed
with HR. Because neither driver was injured, no workers compensation
claim arose — the scheme responds to personal injury, not property
damage. Had Worker H been injured, the claim would have been prima
facie compensable: Queensland retains journey claims under s 35 of
the *Workers' Compensation and Rehabilitation Act 2003* (Qld),
which covers injuries sustained on a journey between the worker's
home and place of employment, subject to exclusions where the
journey involves a substantial delay, deviation, or interruption —
none of which applied to this direct drive home. The internal
incident report was retained for organisational learning purposes
and a fatigue investigation was conducted.

The investigation found that Worker H had slept approximately 5
hours per day across the four-day roster (typical for this
worker), and that the 4-on / 4-off pattern with 12-hour night
shifts on consecutive nights produces accumulated sleep debt by
the fourth shift. The site fatigue management plan referenced
journey-management for drive-in/drive-out workers (a separate
category) but did not address routine post-shift driving home for
local-resident workers.

### Sequence of events

| # | Date / Time | Event |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ~3 years prior | 4-on / 4-off 12-hour night shift roster pattern adopted at the site |
| 2 | ~18 months prior | Fatigue management plan adopted; covers DIDO workers but not local-resident workers |
| 3 | Night 1 (3 nights prior) | Worker H commences four-night roster sequence |
| 4 | Night 2 (2 nights prior) | Cumulative sleep debt building; Worker H sleeps ~5 hours during day |
| 5 | Night 3 (1 night prior) | Worker H sleeps ~5 hours; total sleep across 72 hours ~15 hours, against a ~24-hour need (8 h/night) — accumulated deficit ~9 hours |
| 6 | Shift 4, 07:05 | Shift ends; handover completed |
| 7 | Shift 4, 07:15 | Worker H leaves site car park |
| 8 | Shift 4, 07:37 | Low-speed rear-end collision at roundabout; both vehicles damaged; no injury |
| 9 | Shift 4, 07:50 | Police attendance; zero breath alcohol; details exchanged |
| 10 | Day +4 | Worker H reports collision at start of next rostered shift |

### PEEPO summary
- **People** — Worker H: 5 years at site, current on this
  roster pattern, no prior reported fatigue events, no known sleep
  disorder. No D&A factors. Single household with school-age
  children — daytime sleep environment includes noise and
  caregiving demands.
- **Environment** — Drive home: ~30 km of suburban arterial
  road; ~35 minutes end to end; morning light. Route includes 6 sets of
  traffic lights and 4 roundabouts. The collision occurred ~22 minutes
  into the drive, ~11 km from home (i.e. with ~13 minutes / ~11 km
  remaining).
- **Equipment** — Worker H's personal vehicle, current
  registration and roadworthy.
- **Procedures** — Site fatigue management plan covers DIDO
  workers and HVNL drivers; does not address routine post-shift
  driving home for local-resident workers; no nap room available
  on site.
- **Organisation** — 4-on / 4-off 12-hour night shift roster
  pattern with no scheduled forward rotation; roster reduces
  weekly commuting compared with shorter shifts but increases
  per-shift fatigue load.

### ICAM analysis

| Absent or Failed Defences | Individual / Team Actions | Task / Environmental Conditions | Organisational Factors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site fatigue management plan does not address post-shift driving home for local-resident workers | Worker H did not raise fatigue at end of shift | Sleep debt accumulated across 4 consecutive 12-hour night shifts | 12-hour night shift × 4 consecutive nights creates predictable cumulative fatigue at end of roster |
| No nap room or rest facility available on site for post-shift recovery before driving | Worker H drove home rather than waiting / napping (no policy supporting wait) | Daytime sleep environment includes household noise and caregiving demands | Roster design optimises commuting frequency over per-shift fatigue exposure |
| Pre-drive fatigue self-assessment not embedded in shift end procedure | | Drive home occurred within the post-night-shift wake-impairment window (the circadian low has passed but impairment persists) | Fatigue management plan scope does not cover the population that performs the longest cumulative driving (post-shift home commutes) |
| Roster forward-rotation principle not applied | | | DIDO-focused fatigue management plan misses the larger local-resident worker population |

### Risk assessment
- **Actual**: Consequence 1 (property damage, no injury) ×
  Likelihood Possible — Risk D (Low)
- **Potential**: Consequence 5 (fatal motor vehicle accident,
  worker or third party) × Likelihood Possible — Risk A (Critical)

Potential rationale: Fatigue-related motor vehicle accidents at
the end of a 12-hour night shift have a well-documented
distribution that includes head-on collisions and run-off-road
events with substantially higher consequence than the actual
outcome here. The 25 km/h impact is the low end of the
distribution; the same sleep debt has produced fatalities in
other documented cases (multiple coronial findings in QLD, NSW,
and WA).

### Corrective actions (mapped to factors)

| Action | Factor addressed | Control level |
|---|---|---|
| Extend the fatigue management plan to cover all post-shift driving, not just DIDO and HVNL | Plan scope gap | Administrative |
| Install nap-room facility at the site for post-shift use; protect access at shift handover | Engineering control upgrade | Engineering |
| Pre-drive fatigue self-assessment (digital tool or paper) embedded in shift end-out procedure | Self-assessment gap | Administrative |
| Roster review: assess current 4-on / 4-off pattern against fatigue science; consider forward-rotation and shift-length alternatives | Roster design | Administrative |
| Provide subsidised transport options (rideshare credits, taxi vouchers) for workers self-identifying as fatigued at shift end | Mitigation control | Administrative |
| Fatigue training for all shift workers including specifics of post-shift driving risk and self-assessment | Awareness gap | Administrative |
| Add post-shift driving incidents (where work-related) to incident management system for organisational learning, whether or not a compensation claim arises | Surveillance gap | Administrative |

### Lessons
- Compensability and the WHS duty are different questions, and the
  compensation answer is jurisdiction-specific. Queensland and the
  ACT cover ordinary-commute journey claims (in QLD, WCRA 2003 s 35,
  subject to the substantial delay/deviation/interruption
  exclusions); NSW restricts them — it requires a "real and
  substantial connection" between the employment and the accident —
  and Victoria's WorkCover scheme does not cover ordinary-commute
  journey claims (a transport accident on the commute is instead a
  TAC matter). Western Australia, under the Workers Compensation and
  Injury Management Act 2023, generally does not cover ordinary
  commutes. The Northern Territory recognises journey claims, but a
  motor vehicle accident on a normal commute is dealt with under the
  NT Motor Accidents Compensation scheme rather than workers
  compensation — so for the MVA in this case the NT route is the MAC
  scheme, not a journey claim. Confirm the current position in the
  relevant jurisdiction before relying on it. Either way, the PCBU
  duty to manage risks SFAIRP applies regardless of compensability.
  Work design that produces predictable fatigue, and the drive home
  that the work produces, are within scope
- 4-on / 4-off 12-hour night shifts produce predictable cumulative
  fatigue at end of roster — the evidence on cumulative sleep debt
  across consecutive 12-hour nights is well established, although the
  net risk of 12-hour shifts in general is context-dependent (see
  `hazards.md` §18); the roster is a management decision, not an
  inevitability
- Local-resident shift workers are the largest population doing
  post-shift driving — fatigue plans that focus on DIDO workers
  miss the bulk of the population at risk
- Nap-room provision is a cheap and effective engineering control
  that most sites can install in a meeting room or office; the
  barrier is usually cultural ("we don't sleep at work") rather
  than physical
- Officer due diligence (see `legislation.md` §6) requires
  attention to the foreseeable consequences of work design — a
  roster designed to optimise commuting frequency, without
  addressing post-shift fatigue, is a decision that an officer
  needs to know about and verify

### Suggested training use
Useful in three contexts. For ELT and officer-level audiences, the
case illustrates the work-design question that sits behind a
roster decision — the right audience to commission a roster review
is at this level. For HR and rostering teams, the case
demonstrates the fatigue-science basis for roster pattern
selection. For shift workers and supervisors, the case is good
toolbox content on the post-shift drive home — a topic that's
under-discussed in safety meetings.

## 9. How to Use These Cases

### For training — walk a team through one case progressively

The "stop here, what would you do?" pedagogy works well with these
cases because the analyses build incrementally and each case has
multiple decision points where the audience can engage. A typical
60-minute session covers one case and produces durable learning;
attempting two or three cases in one session produces a parade of
incidents and very little retention.

Suggested structure for a one-case training session:

| Phase | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Set the scene | 5 min | Read the Scenario and What Happened aloud; do not show the analysis |
| Initial reactions | 5 min | "What do you think happened? What would you investigate first?" |
| Walk the sequence | 10 min | Present the Sequence table; pause at each numbered step and ask what would have changed the outcome |
| PEEPO together | 10 min | Build PEEPO with the audience on a whiteboard; reveal the case's PEEPO at the end |
| ICAM analysis | 15 min | Build the 4-column ICAM with the audience; reveal the case's analysis; discuss differences |
| Risk assessment | 5 min | Discuss actual vs potential; the HiPo / classification question |
| Corrective actions | 5 min | What would you do? Map to hierarchy of controls |
| Transfer question | 5 min | "Where in our operation does this pattern appear?" |

The transfer question is the most important part — the case is
only useful if it generates local reflection. Workers and
supervisors should leave the session with at least one location
or task in their own work where they recognise the pattern.

### For ICAM calibration of new investigators

New investigators routinely under-investigate (stop at the
proximate cause) or over-investigate (chase organisational factors
that aren't in scope). The seven cases provide a calibration set
covering the range of severity and complexity the investigator
will encounter:

- **Cases 1 and 4** (forklift HiPo, electrical near miss) demonstrate
  HiPo investigation discipline — investigate to potential, not
  actual
- **Cases 2 and 3** (manual handling MSI, psychosocial complaint)
  demonstrate cumulative and complaint-based investigations where
  there is no single mechanism event — the analysis is across
  exposure over time
- **Case 5** (slip-trip-fall) demonstrates the
  seemingly-trivial case where the systemic factors are
  counter-intuitive — the same incident in a different system
  would not have happened
- **Case 6** (chemical decant) demonstrates the SDS-to-SWMS-to-work
  specification chain and the substitution-at-procurement
  question
- **Case 7** (fatigue vehicle) demonstrates the work-design
  question that sits behind a roster decision and the
  WHS-vs-workers-compensation framing distinction

A new investigator who can analyse all seven of these cases to
the depth shown is broadly calibrated for everyday investigation
work. Cases requiring deeper analysis (catastrophic events, MHF
events, multi-PCBU coordination failure on shared workplaces)
should still be supported by a senior investigator — see
`case-studies.md` for the catastrophe set.

### For toolbox content

Each case yields multiple toolbox talking points — typically 3-5
distinct topics from one case. Use the Lessons section as the
starting point; pick one lesson per toolbox and develop it for the
local audience.

Worked examples of toolbox content drawn from the cases:

| Case | Toolbox topics |
|---|---|
| Case 1 (Forklift HiPo) | Blind-spot discipline; 360° check before reversing; pedestrian-in-aisle behaviour; TMP drift |
| Case 2 (Manual handling) | Early symptom reporting; refusal authority; mechanical aid use; rostering for transfer load |
| Case 3 (Psychosocial) | Workload escalation pathways; respectful feedback; dashboard design; manager support |
| Case 4 (Electrical near miss) | Restricted-access asset awareness; permit-to-work discipline; SWMS specificity; the work-order chain |
| Case 5 (Slip-trip-fall) | Housekeeping-during-service; drainage inspection; footwear replacement; single-point-of-failure rostering |
| Case 6 (Chemical decant) | SDS use; PPE specification; secondary container labelling; spill kit placement |
| Case 7 (Fatigue vehicle) | Pre-drive self-assessment; nap-room use; roster design; the WHS-vs-comp framing |

For each toolbox, include one transfer question — "where does
this happen here?" — and one specific local action — "what will
we do differently this week?"

### For onboarding new WHS staff

Reading all seven cases as part of WHS staff onboarding builds a
baseline understanding of the work that fills most investigation
registers. The catastrophe set in `case-studies.md` is for
strategic context and board-level work; the everyday set is for
operational work that the new staff member will be doing within
weeks of starting.

Suggested onboarding sequence:

| Week | Reading | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | `SKILL.md`, `legislation.md` (overview), `investigation.md` | Understand the regulatory and methodological frame |
| Week 2 | Cases 1 and 5 (forklift HiPo, slip-trip-fall) | Calibrate on classic everyday incidents — a no-injury HiPo and an LTI that is itself HiPo on potential |
| Week 3 | Cases 2 and 6 (manual handling, chemical decant) | Calibrate on cumulative-injury and HiPo no-injury investigations |
| Week 4 | Cases 4 and 7 (electrical, fatigue vehicle) | Calibrate on contractor / multi-PCBU and work-design investigations |
| Week 5 | Case 3 (psychosocial) | Calibrate on complaint-based and psychosocial investigation, including Respect@Work positive duty |
| Week 6 | `case-studies.md` (catastrophes) | Build strategic context for board-level and training work |
| Week 7+ | Live investigations under senior investigator supervision | Apply the calibration to current work |

By week 8 the new staff member should be able to lead a Level 3
(medium-severity) investigation under remote oversight, with the
seven-case calibration set as the reference standard for analysis
depth.

### For organisation-specific framing

Severity rating numbers, risk levels, classification thresholds,
and corrective-action ownership conventions used in the cases above
follow generic conventions. For organisation-specific risk matrix
values, severity classifications, document numbering, system
references, and named programs, load `references/company.md` and
adjust the analysis accordingly.

The seven cases are deliberately written to be portable across
organisations — the systemic factors generalise, the technical
details transfer, and only the surface conventions (severity
labels, document codes) need to be reworked for the specific
organisation. A useful onboarding exercise is to ask the new staff
member to re-rate one case using the organisation's actual
classification system — the exercise surfaces both the case logic
and the organisation's matrix in one step.
