## 1. WHS Strategy Development

### What a WHS strategy actually is

A WHS strategy is a multi-year direction-setting document that defines where the function and the organisation are going on safety, why, and how progress will be evidenced. It is not:

- A management system manual restated
- A list of programs already scheduled
- A re-skinned operational plan with a longer horizon
- A compliance roadmap dressed as strategy

If the document could be replaced by a procedure or an annual plan without losing anything material, it is not a strategy.

A strategy answers four questions. If any answer is missing, the document is incomplete:

1. **Where are we now?** — current state assessment, brutal not flattering
2. **Where do we need to be?** — a defined future state with measurable characteristics, anchored to organisational strategy
3. **Why does it matter?** — the case for investment, including the cost of doing nothing
4. **How will we get there?** — capability uplift, structural change, investment, and the sequencing of both

### Typical horizon and refresh cycle

| Horizon | Use | Refresh |
|---|---|---|
| 3 years | Most common — long enough to land structural change; short enough to remain credible | Annually with light touch; full re-baselining at year 3 |
| 5 years | Heavy capital industries, infrastructure, mining; matches asset lifecycle and investment cycles | Year 1 detailed; outer years directional; refreshed every 2–3 years |
| 1 year | Operating plan only — not a strategy | N/A |
| 10 years | Aspirational / vision only; not a working document | N/A |

Three years is the practical default for most AU/NZ organisations. Anything longer drifts from organisational strategy refresh cycles; anything shorter is just a delivery plan with executive packaging.

### Anchoring to organisational strategy

WHS strategy that does not connect to organisational strategy reads as a parallel obligation — and is treated as one. Anchor to:

- **Growth ambitions** — new geographies, new sectors, new contracts carry new exposures; WHS strategy must position the function to absorb them without becoming the constraint on growth
- **Operating model changes** — outsourcing, in-housing, divestments, acquisitions; each one rewires the contractor and PCBU duty map
- **Workforce strategy** — labour hire dependency, casualisation, ageing workforce, FIFO/DIDO patterns, mental health prevalence — these shape exposure profile and capability demand
- **Customer and tender requirements** — major clients now mandate WHS maturity, ISO 45001 certification, named programs, and reporting cadence; WHS strategy must clear procurement bars before they bite
- **Regulatory trajectory** — industrial manslaughter, psychosocial regulations, engineered stone bans, silica dust monitoring, Respect@Work positive duty have all shifted the floor; strategy must position above the floor, not at it

A WHS strategy that doesn't name the organisational strategy document(s) it aligns to should be rejected at draft stage.

### Components of a credible WHS strategy

| Section | Purpose | Common failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Current state assessment | Honest read of performance, maturity, capability, exposure | "We're a good company doing good work" — useless |
| Strategic context | The why — organisational, regulatory, customer, workforce | Bullet-point cliches without specific anchors |
| Strategic objectives | 4–7 multi-year objectives, each with a clear so-what | Vague aspirations ("strengthen safety culture") |
| Capability uplift roadmap | What people, systems, structures need to change | Activity lists without capability framing |
| Investment case | Financial framing, ROI, optionality | Cost stated without value stated |
| Performance measurement | How progress will be evidenced — leading and lagging | TRIFR-only — see Goodhart caution below |
| Governance | How the strategy will be reviewed, refreshed, escalated | Owned nowhere; reviewed annually if at all |

### Setting strategic objectives beyond TRIFR

The single most common strategic failure in WHS strategy is anchoring to TRIFR as the primary outcome measure. TRIFR is a rare-event statistic that fluctuates randomly at most organisational sample sizes. It is necessary for external benchmarking and regulator transparency, but it is a terrible strategic objective on its own.

A balanced objective set covers four domains:

**Capacity** — the organisation's ability to do safety work well.
- Sufficient, competent WHS resourcing relative to workforce and risk
- Frontline leader capability in safety conversations and incident response
- Worker engagement infrastructure (HSR coverage, reporting culture, hazard identification volume)
- Critical control owner clarity and ownership at sufficient depth

**Leading indicators** — system inputs that predict outcomes.
- Hazard report rate per worker (engagement and reporting culture)
- CCV completion vs plan and degraded-control escalation rate (control health visibility)
- Corrective action close-out rate and quality (system discipline)
- HiPo investigation completion on time (response capability)

**Learning capability** — the organisation's ability to convert events into system improvement.
- Lessons learnt cycle time from incident to communicated outcome
- Cross-site application rate of corrective actions (does what one contract learns reach the rest?)
- Learning Team adoption and quality
- Investigation rigour, evidenced by external review or peer audit

**Regulatory posture** — how the organisation engages with regulators and obligations.
- Proactive vs reactive regulator engagement balance
- Notice quantum (improvement, prohibition) and trend
- Time to compliance on notices issued
- Officer due diligence evidence quality

A strategy with 4–7 objectives drawn across these four domains, each with 2–4 measurable indicators, is the working pattern.

### Goodhart's Law applied to safety metrics

> "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."
> — Charles Goodhart (paraphrased by Marilyn Strathern)

In WHS, this is not theoretical. The classic failure modes:

| Metric | Distortion when made a hard target |
|---|---|
| TRIFR | Suppression of reporting; reclassification of LTIs to MTIs; medical management pressure to limit days off |
| LTIFR | Same as TRIFR plus active pressure on injured workers to return on alternate duties prematurely |
| Hazard report rate | Bulk hazard report submission ("the bolt was tight", "the light worked") to hit a target |
| Toolbox completion | Attendance marked for 2-minute sessions; sign-in sheets without engagement |
| CCV completion | Verifications conducted without genuine field observation; pencil-whipping |
| Days since last LTI | Highest-risk metric in WHS — actively suppresses reporting and incentivises hiding |

Design principles to keep metrics honest:

- **Pair every outcome target with a leading indicator that rewards reporting, not suppressing.** If TRIFR is in the executive scorecard, near-miss reporting rate per worker must be there alongside it, with upward direction valued.
- **Never set "days since last LTI" as a public-facing metric.** It exists to be broken; the moment it becomes a target, the next injury becomes invisible.
- **Distinguish performance measurement from performance targets.** Track everything that is useful; only set targets where the gaming cost is lower than the management value.
- **Build counter-metrics into governance review.** If TRIFR is improving but hazard reports are also dropping, the improvement may be reporting suppression. Counter-metrics surface that.
- **Avoid league tables of injury rates by contract or BU.** League tables drive reporting suppression more reliably than any other mechanism. Use them sparingly and only with leading indicators alongside.

### Capability uplift planning

The capability uplift section is where most WHS strategies fall over. "Build capability" is not a plan. The credible structure:

| Capability domain | Target population | Uplift approach | Measurement |
|---|---|---|---|
| WHS function technical | WHS specialists | Chartered status pathway (AIHS ChOHSP, AIOH), structured technical learning | Chartered count, technical assessment |
| Frontline leader safety | Supervisors, foremen | Targeted skills program — incident response, safety conversations, hazard recognition | Observation assessments, leader feedback |
| Officer due diligence | Officers, executives, board | Officer briefings, safety walks, evidence pack discipline | Activity logs, post-event review |
| Worker engagement | All workers | HSR training to standard, hazard reporting capability, near-miss articulation | HSR coverage, report rate and quality |
| Critical risk technical | Critical risk owners | Deep technical knowledge per critical risk, control failure pathways | Critical risk owner interview, control verification quality |
| Investigation | Investigators (often shared with operations) | ICAM certification, peer review, mentoring | Investigation report quality, lessons captured |

Each row maps to a defined program with a budget, a timeline, and a named owner. If the strategy section reads "build capability across the organisation", the strategy is not ready for execution.

### From strategy to operating plan

A common confusion is whether the strategy itself contains the detailed activity. It should not.

- **Strategy** — multi-year direction, objectives, capability domains, investment envelope. Endures across refreshes.
- **Operating plan** — annual translation into named projects, budgets, milestones, accountable owners. Refreshed each cycle.
- **Project / program plans** — delivery-level activity for each named intervention. Owned by the program lead.

The strategy stays at the level of "we will lift critical risk management to a defined maturity level by year 3" — the operating plan specifies how year 1 advances that, and the project plan specifies the work breakdown.

### Strategy refresh cadence and gates

| Trigger | Action |
|---|---|
| Annual refresh | Light-touch review; adjust operating plan; refresh metrics; refresh exposure read |
| Strategic event (M&A, divestment, major contract win/loss) | Out-of-cycle review of objectives and capability roadmap |
| Major incident / fatality / prosecution | Out-of-cycle review of strategy assumptions; learning translated into uplift |
| Regulatory change (industrial manslaughter, psychosocial regs, sectoral) | Targeted update of relevant strategic objectives |
| Year 3 / strategy expiry | Full re-baselining — new current state, new objectives, new capability roadmap |

The strategy document itself should declare the refresh cadence explicitly. A strategy without an embedded refresh trigger drifts.

## 2. WHS Function Design

### Function size by organisation size

The default benchmarks below are indicative. Risk profile, regulatory intensity, sector, and geographic spread all distort the numbers. A 50-worker engineered stone fabricator carries more WHS function load than a 500-worker office-based consultancy.

| Org size | Typical WHS function structure | WHS leader title | Reports to |
|---|---|---|---|
| <500 workers, low-risk | 1 WHS Manager + 0–1 Coordinator | WHS Manager / HSE Manager | COO, HR Director, or directly CEO |
| <500 workers, high-risk (construction, mining, transport) | 1 Manager + 2–3 Advisors / Coordinators | HSE Manager | COO or CEO directly |
| 500–2,000 workers | Manager + 3–6 advisors; one dedicated specialist (hygiene, workers comp, or systems) | Head of WHS / HSE Manager | COO or Chief Risk Officer |
| 2,000–5,000 workers | Head of WHS + technical specialists (hygiene, claims, systems, programs) + BU-embedded advisors | Head of WHS / GM HSE | Often Chief Risk Officer, COO, or directly CEO |
| 5,000–15,000 workers | Group function with technical specialists + Divisional Heads of WHS + BU advisor population | Group GM WHS / Group HSE Director | Chief Risk Officer, CEO, or COO |
| >15,000 workers | Group function with multi-layer structure; centre of excellence model common | Chief Safety Officer / HSE Director / Group Head | Directly CEO or Chief Risk Officer; board engagement standard |

### Advisor-to-worker ratios

The ratios below are **unsourced practitioner heuristics** drawn from consulting
and recruitment practice — there is no published industry standard or validated
benchmark dataset for WHS advisor ratios. Use them as a starting point for a
resourcing conversation, never as a defensible standard in a board paper or
business case.

| Risk profile | Typical advisor : worker ratio | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Low-risk (office, retail, financial services) | 1 : 1,500 to 1 : 3,000 | Lean model; coordinator-heavy |
| Mixed-risk (services with field component) | 1 : 750 to 1 : 1,500 | Most common in AU services sector |
| Construction / infrastructure | 1 : 200 to 1 : 500 | Project-deployed advisors; intensive |
| Heavy industry / mining / processing | 1 : 150 to 1 : 400 | Statutory roles often demand higher density |
| Major hazard facilities | 1 : 100 to 1 : 250 | Plus dedicated process safety specialists |

These ratios are for **field-deployable WHS advisors**. They exclude specialists (hygiene, workers comp, systems, programs, leadership). Specialists are typically 1 per 1,000–3,000 workers regardless of risk profile, scaling with technical demand rather than headcount.

A common error is benchmarking against industry averages without adjusting for what the advisors actually do. An advisor primarily managing incident reporting needs different capacity than one running critical control verification programs. Benchmark by activity profile, not just ratio.

The non-negotiable principle: **safety is line accountable, supported by the WHS function — never the other way around.** A function that owns safety outcomes lets the line off the hook and inverts the duty structure. The WHS function provides the system, the technical expertise, the assurance, and the advice; the line owns the work and its risk.

### In-house vs outsourced models

| Model | When it works | When it fails | Best-fit organisations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fully in-house | Sufficient scale; sustained risk; strategic priority; talent attraction viable | Talent constraints; sub-scale function with no career path | Most >500 workers in moderate-to-high risk industries |
| Outsourced advisor model | Stable, low-complexity risk; geographic spread without scale; project-based work | Critical risk environments; ongoing regulatory engagement; cultural integration matters | Specialist contractors, project-based businesses, small organisations |
| Hybrid (in-house leadership + outsourced delivery) | Need senior leadership in-house but no scale for full team | If outsourcing becomes default rather than supplemented | Mid-size organisations with peaks of project work |
| Outsourced specialist support | Niche expertise needed periodically (hygiene, MHF, ergonomics, claims) | When outsourced specialists never engage operationally | Most organisations — universal model for specialised expertise |

Outsourcing the WHS function entirely is usually a sign that safety is being managed as a procurement problem rather than a duty. PCBUs cannot contract out their duty; outsourcing creates a layer of intermediation that obscures accountability without removing it.

The conventional anti-pattern: a 1,500-worker high-risk operation with a single outsourced part-time WHS consultant. The duty exposure is the same as if the function were fully in-house; the management capability to discharge the duty is materially weaker.

### Shared service vs embedded Business Partner model

| Model | Description | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shared service** | WHS team sits centrally; serves the business on a service-request basis | Consistency, efficiency, scale economies on technical work | Distance from operational context; lower influence; reactive |
| **Embedded BP** | WHS advisors physically and reportingly embedded in business units | Operational context, influence, proactive engagement | Inconsistency across BUs; cultural capture; harder to drive group standards |
| **Hybrid (matrix)** | BPs embedded in business + technical specialists central | Best of both — most common in mid-large organisations | Reporting line tension; requires clear protocols |

The matrix model is the dominant pattern in AU/NZ organisations of 1,000+ workers. The key design choices:

- **Solid-line reporting** — typically to WHS leadership for consistency, technical standards, and career development
- **Dotted-line reporting** — to the business unit GM or COO for operational alignment and influence
- **Conflict resolution** — explicit protocol for when the BP and the GM disagree on a safety call; escalation goes up the WHS line, not the business line

Anti-pattern: solid line to the BU GM, dotted line to WHS. This configuration makes WHS advisors line-of-business assets and compromises their independence on regulatory and critical risk matters. The advisor becomes a participant in the operational pressure they should be challenging.

### The WHS Business Partner role specifically

The WHS Business Partner (WHS BP) is the workhorse of the modern WHS function. The role is not an advisor with a longer title — it is a distinct discipline.

**Core accountabilities:**
- Trusted advisor to the BU GM or COO on all WHS matters
- Translates group WHS standards into operational reality for the BU
- Owns the BU's incident response, investigation rigour, and learning cycle
- Surfaces BU-specific risk signals into group reporting
- Develops frontline leader capability through coaching, not one-off training
- Holds the line on critical risk where commercial pressure pushes against it

**Capabilities required:**
- Operational credibility — has worked in or alongside the business
- Commercial literacy — reads a P&L, understands contract margin, can size an investment ask in business terms
- Influence without authority — most of the work is persuasion
- Regulatory expertise — must know the Act and Regs, not just the procedures
- Strategic awareness — connects operational decisions to multi-year exposure

**Common mis-hires:**
- Strong technical advisor with no commercial fluency — becomes the "safety person", marginalised
- Strong operator with no technical depth — gets captured by the business and fails on critical risk calls
- Strong corporate communicator with no field credibility — workers and supervisors won't engage

A WHS BP cohort is typically the single highest-leverage investment in the WHS function. They sit at the intersection where most of the material safety decisions are made.

### WHS leadership reporting line

Where the most senior WHS leader reports determines what kind of organisation it is on safety. The implications:

| Reports to | Implication | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| **CEO directly** | Safety is positioned as a strategic priority on a par with operations and finance; access to board is direct; signals seriousness to workforce, regulators, and customers | High-risk industries; organisations with material exposure; ASX-listed entities with active safety committees |
| **COO** | Safety embedded operationally; tight integration with operating discipline; access depends on COO's own posture | Operational businesses where safety and ops are inseparable; construction, mining, transport |
| **Chief Risk Officer** | Safety treated as enterprise risk; tight integration with risk frameworks, insurance, crisis | Financial services, complex multi-jurisdictional organisations, organisations with mature ERM |
| **CHRO / People & Culture Director** | Safety integrated with people, wellbeing, culture; tight on psychosocial; can underweight operational risk | Office-based organisations, professional services; risky for heavy industry |
| **Legal / Company Secretary** | Compliance-led posture; defensive rather than performance-led | Heavily regulated organisations in damage-control mode — usually a sign of past failure |
| **Below executive (HR Manager, Operations Manager etc.)** | Safety is administrative, not strategic; warning sign | Small organisations only; in larger organisations this is a structural problem |

The reporting line is not just an org chart question. Workers, supervisors, customers, and regulators read it. A WHS leader reporting to the CHRO in a mining business will struggle to be taken seriously in a Safety Case Forum, a Board of Inquiry, or a coronial inquest. A WHS leader reporting to the COO in a professional services firm may be operationally aligned but underweight psychosocial duty.

The safer default for AU/NZ organisations with material WHS exposure is **CEO or COO direct**. A Chief Risk Officer reporting line works well where the broader risk discipline is genuinely mature. CHRO works where the operational exposure is genuinely modest. Anything below executive in a 1,000+ worker organisation should be challenged.

## 3. WHS Budget and Economics

### The cost of WHS — visible and hidden

WHS cost discussions usually start and stop at the function budget. That captures less than half the picture.

**Visible costs:**
- WHS function payroll and on-costs (salaries, super, leave, training)
- Insurance premiums (workers compensation, public liability, D&O)
- Compliance costs (audits, certifications, licences, statutory training, regulator fees)
- Systems and tooling (incident management system, analytics, permit systems, training platforms)
- External advisory (hygienist, ergonomist, legal, crisis comms)
- Program delivery (Zero Harm campaigns, leadership development, collateral)

**Hidden costs:**
- Direct incident cost — medical, salary continuance, replacement labour, equipment damage, environmental cleanup
- Investigation and corrective action cost — internal time, external experts, system changes
- Claims management cost — case manager time, IMEs, legal
- Workers comp premium impact — claim experience drives premium rerating for 3+ years
- Lost time and productivity — backfill, retraining, ramp-up of replacement workers
- Reputation and brand — customer attrition, tender disqualification, ESG rating impact
- Cost of regulator engagement — investigation, prosecution defence, inspector visits, notices
- Cost of capital — investors increasingly price WHS performance into cost of capital for material exposures

Estimates of the hidden-to-direct cost ratio vary enormously across studies — from roughly **1:1 to 36:1** depending on industry, severity mix, and costing methodology. The often-quoted single multipliers (Heinrich's 4:1, Bird's iceberg) sit inside that span but have weak empirical foundations; treat any specific ratio as illustrative rather than established, and prefer organisation-specific costing where the data exists.

The strategic implication stands regardless of the multiplier chosen: a business case framed only on direct cost or on premium impact will materially underestimate the total cost of incidents.

### ROI quantification approaches

| Approach | Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct cost avoidance** | Calculate avoided incident cost × probability × frequency | Tangible, defensible | Underestimates hidden cost; speculative on probability |
| **Premium impact modelling** | Forecast workers comp premium under scenarios | Concrete; finance-friendly; uses real data | Captures only insured exposure; 2–3 year lag |
| **Productivity impact** | Lost time × labour cost; equipment downtime; project schedule impact | Significant in capital-heavy industries | Hard to attribute cleanly |
| **Avoided regulatory cost** | Expected value of notices, prosecution, defence | Material for high-exposure organisations | Hard to quantify ex-ante |
| **Reputation / customer value** | Tender qualification, customer retention, ESG rating | Increasingly material; sometimes the dominant value driver | Hardest to quantify; qualitative anchors common |
| **Optionality value** | Investments that create future flexibility (e.g. ISO 45001 cert opens tender market) | Captures strategic value | Often dismissed by finance as soft |
| **Cost of doing nothing** | What is the trajectory if we don't invest? | Effective framing in boards | Easy to mis-anchor |

The strongest business cases combine 3–4 of these approaches rather than relying on one. Premium impact alone is the most common framing and the weakest — it captures the insured exposure, not the total cost of the event.

### Premium impact modelling — basics

(See `compensation-rtw.md` for full mechanics.)

Across the AU schemes — the centrally underwritten schemes (icare NSW, WorkSafe VIC, WorkCover QLD, ReturnToWorkSA) and the privately underwritten ones (WA, regulated by WorkCover WA — note WorkSafe WA is the WHS regulator, not the compensation scheme — plus TAS, ACT and NT, where licensed private insurers underwrite under state/territory regulation) — and the NZ ACC scheme, premium is a function of:

- Industry classification rate (base rate by ANZSIC code)
- Wages declared
- Claims experience (3-year rolling window in most schemes)
- Performance adjustment factor (good / poor performance modifier)
- Various scheme-specific levies and rebates

The strategic levers an organisation can pull:
- Reduce claim frequency (prevention investment)
- Reduce claim duration and cost (RTW capability, early intervention, psychological injury response capability)
- Improve experience rating (3-year trend matters more than annual result)
- Restructure (genuine restructure, not avoidance — schemes have anti-avoidance provisions)

A single major claim (especially a psychological injury claim with long duration) can drive premium impact of $0.5M–$5M+ over the experience window for a mid-size employer. This is the most concrete financial framing available for WHS investment in a business case.

### Business case structure for WHS investment

A credible WHS business case follows the same structure as any capital or operating investment proposal:

```
1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (1 page)
   - The decision required
   - Investment ask (capital + operating, 3-year view)
   - Expected value (quantified where possible)
   - Strategic rationale (3 bullets max)
   - Recommendation

2. PROBLEM AND OPPORTUNITY
   - Current state — exposure, performance, trajectory
   - Cost of doing nothing — trended forward
   - What changes with this investment

3. PROPOSED INVESTMENT
   - What is being purchased / built / changed
   - Scope, sequencing, dependencies
   - Resourcing requirements

4. FINANCIAL ANALYSIS
   - Cost (year by year, capital + operating)
   - Benefits (quantified by category)
   - NPV / payback / IRR
   - Sensitivity analysis

5. STRATEGIC ALIGNMENT
   - Link to organisational strategy
   - Customer / tender / ESG implications
   - Regulatory positioning

6. RISK AND OPTIONALITY
   - What could go wrong with the investment
   - Mitigations
   - Optionality created (future flexibility)

7. GOVERNANCE
   - Sponsor
   - Owner
   - Steering / oversight
   - Decision rights

8. APPENDICES
   - Detailed financial model
   - Benchmarks
   - Comparable case studies
```

The single biggest weakness in most WHS business cases is the financial analysis section — it is either absent or weak. WHS leaders who can build a defensible financial case earn substantially more credibility with CFOs and boards, and unlock investment that function-budget framing cannot.

### Typical WHS function budget components

For a 5,000-worker mixed-risk organisation, indicative budget split:

| Component | % of WHS budget | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Function payroll | 55–70% | Largest line in almost all WHS budgets |
| Systems / platforms | 5–10% | Incident management, analytics, training, permit |
| Programs and engagement | 5–10% | Zero Harm campaigns, leadership development |
| External advisory | 3–8% | Hygiene, ergonomics, legal, crisis |
| Audit and assurance | 3–6% | Internal audits, ISO 45001 surveillance, third-party |
| Training (statutory + role-specific) | 5–10% | HRWL, white card, supervisor competency, first aid |
| Statutory fees, licences | 1–3% | Regulator fees, sector-specific licences |
| Contingency / unallocated | 3–5% | Crisis response, emerging issues |

Workers compensation premium typically sits outside the WHS function budget — usually in Finance or HR. This is a sub-optimal design — the function that controls the cost driver should have line of sight to the cost itself. Many mature organisations now report combined cost (function + premium + insurance) as the WHS economic envelope.

### How much WHS is enough

This is one of the hardest questions a senior WHS leader will be asked, and the temptation to answer "more" is always present and usually wrong. The defensible position:

- **Enough means meeting the duty SFAIRP, plus the strategic objectives the organisation has set.** Below that, the function is under-resourced. Above that, the question is whether the marginal investment generates marginal value.
- **There is a diminishing return curve on WHS investment.** The first dollars spent on basic system, capability, and risk management generate enormous return. The hundredth dollar spent generates almost nothing — and may generate negative return through compliance burden or distraction from the work.
- **Investment intensity should match exposure.** A high-exposure business with low investment is materially under-resourced. A low-exposure business with high investment is wasting capital that could be deployed elsewhere — including elsewhere in safety.
- **The honest answer is "enough to discharge the duty and meet the objectives".** Calibrate annually based on whether the function is doing both. If exposure is rising and the function is stretched, invest. If exposure is falling and the function is fully delivering, hold steady or reallocate to higher-leverage areas.

A common error is conflating the size of the function with the safety posture of the organisation. The relationship is non-linear and mediated entirely by what the function does and how the line manages work. A well-led 8-person team can outperform a poorly-led 25-person team in the same business.

## 4. WHS Leadership Selection and Development

### Competency framework for WHS leaders

A senior WHS leader (Manager and above) needs capability across four domains. Weakness in any one limits effectiveness:

**1. Technical**
- Deep WHS legislation knowledge (Act, Regs, Codes; relevant jurisdiction)
- Critical risk technical depth in industry-relevant hazards
- ISO 45001 / management system design
- Incident investigation methodology (ICAM at minimum)
- Workers compensation and RTW
- Data and analytics literacy

**2. Commercial**
- P&L literacy — reads a financial statement, understands margin, capital vs operating
- Business case development — NPV, payback, sensitivity
- Procurement and contracting — risk allocation, SLA design
- Tender support — WHS content in proposals, due diligence response
- Insurance and broker engagement

**3. Influence**
- Trusted-advisor capability with executives — speaks their language, earns the seat
- Negotiation — particularly in cross-functional or contested calls
- Stakeholder management — board, regulator, customer, union, HSR, media
- Public speaking and communication — clear, calm, credible under pressure
- Conflict and dissent — capable of holding the line under commercial pressure

**4. Strategic**
- Multi-year horizon — works on year-3 problems, not just year-1
- Sectoral and regulatory trajectory awareness
- Capability uplift design — builds people, not just delivers programs
- Function design and operating model
- Crisis management — leading, not just supporting

Few WHS leaders are equally strong across all four. The most common pattern is technical strength with influence and strategic gaps; the second most common is operator strength with technical gaps. Function design should pair leaders complementarily — a Head strong on commercial and strategic paired with a deputy strong on technical and influence, for example.

### Recruitment — what good looks like

A strong WHS leader candidate will demonstrate:

- A career story that shows progression through operational exposure (not just compliance roles)
- Evidence of leading through a major incident or regulatory event — and learning from it
- Credibility with senior operational leaders in their references — not just other WHS people
- A view on what they would change in their first 100 days, anchored in something specific to your organisation
- Chartered status or equivalent (ChOHSP, AIOH; UK CMIOSH; Canadian CRSP) — not mandatory but a signal of professional commitment
- Comfortable with data — can articulate what HiPo distribution tells them, not just TRIFR
- A view on Safety II / HOP / new view safety — not as a slogan, but as something they have applied
- A clear answer to "what would you do if the CEO asked you to sign off on something you weren't comfortable with?" — and the answer isn't "I'd find a way to support the business"

### Recruitment red flags

| Red flag | What it indicates |
|---|---|
| Talks about "safety culture" without defining it | Has absorbed industry language without underlying frameworks |
| Compliance-focused only; talks about audits, procedures, paperwork | Will deliver an administrative function, not a strategic one |
| No operational experience or credibility | Workers and supervisors won't engage; will be marginalised |
| The "safety person" stereotype — earnest, evangelical, slogan-heavy | Diminished credibility with executives and operators |
| Frames safety as separate from / opposed to operations | Will create the goal conflict they should be resolving |
| Speaks only in lagging metrics | Limited analytical depth; can't lead beyond TRIFR |
| Talks about people as the problem ("non-compliance", "bad attitudes") | Stuck in Safety I; HOP and Safety II will be theatre at best |
| Can't articulate what they got wrong in their last role | Defensive; will hide problems rather than surface them |
| Wants the role to "build culture from the ground up" without acknowledging existing strengths | Will alienate the existing function and operators |
| Cannot describe a business case they have built and won | Limited strategic and commercial credibility |
| Heavy emphasis on personal heroics (saved a life, stopped a job) without systemic framing | May be effective tactically but unable to lead an organisation |

### Chartered status pathway

| Body | Designation | Best for | Pathway |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AIHS** (Australian Institute of Health and Safety) | **ChOHSP** — Chartered OHS Professional | Generalist WHS managers and leaders | Membership + portfolio assessment + interview; INSHPO global capability framework |
| **AIHS** | **COHSProf** — Certified OHS Professional | Mid-career WHS professionals | Certification assessment below ChOHSP; pathway to Chartered |
| **AIHS** | **COHSPrac** — Certified OHS Practitioner | Practitioner tier — advisors, coordinators | Certification against the INSHPO practitioner capability framework |
| **AIOH** (Australian Institute of Occupational Hygienists) | **COH** — Certified Occupational Hygienist | Specialist hygiene work — silica, asbestos, chemical, noise | Examinations + practical assessment + experience |
| **HFESA** (Human Factors and Ergonomics Society of Australia) | **CPE** — Certified Professional Ergonomist | Specialist ergonomics / human factors | Portfolio + assessment |
| **NZISM** (NZ Institute of Safety Management) | NZISM professional accreditation (e.g. **ProfNZISM**) | NZ-based generalist WHS | NZISM graded membership/accreditation + listing on the HASANZ Register |
| **IOSH** (UK) | **CMIOSH** — Chartered Member | UK-aligned organisations; international portability | UK qualification framework |
| **BCSP** (US) | **CSP** — Certified Safety Professional | US-aligned organisations | US examination |

For AU/NZ generalist WHS leaders, **ChOHSP (AIHS) is the dominant designation**. For specialist hygiene work (silica, asbestos, respiratory protection, chemical exposure), **AIOH membership and COH** is the technical standard.

Chartered status is not legally required but increasingly expected for senior roles and for credibility with regulators, boards, and customers. Major clients now require chartered status in tender specifications for WHS leadership positions.

### Succession planning for the WHS function

Treat WHS succession the same as any other strategic function. The common pattern:

| Role | Successor pool | Typical lead time to readiness |
|---|---|---|
| Group Head WHS / CSO | Divisional Head WHS + external candidates | 12–24 months internal; immediate external |
| Divisional Head WHS | WHS Managers; senior BPs with leadership capability | 12–18 months |
| WHS Manager | Senior BPs; senior advisors with management aptitude | 6–12 months |
| Senior WHS BP | Advisors with operational credibility and commercial fluency | 6–12 months |
| Technical Specialist (Hygiene, MHF, Process Safety) | Specialist in-development; external recruitment | 24–36 months for senior |

Succession planning gaps in WHS are common because:
- The function is often too lean for natural succession depth
- Critical specialist roles (process safety, hygiene) have thin external markets
- Strong WHS BPs are sometimes the best candidates, but the leadership capability gap is underestimated

Build succession explicitly into the strategy. A 3-year strategy that doesn't name 2–3 successor positions for the senior WHS team is incomplete.

### The role of the WHS leader in setting the executive safety position

The most senior WHS leader has a specific role that no other executive can hold: shaping how the executive talks about, thinks about, and acts on safety. This includes:

- **Briefing the CEO and executives** for board, customer, regulator, and media engagements on WHS — what to say, what not to say, how to position questions about performance and exposure
- **Coaching executives on incident response** — how to respond when a fatality or major incident occurs, what to say to the family, the workforce, the regulator, the media; what not to say
- **Holding the line on critical risk decisions** at executive level — the WHS leader is the named voice that says "we won't do this work this way, regardless of commercial impact"
- **Advising on officer due diligence** — what evidence each officer needs to be able to demonstrate, what activity is sufficient, what isn't (see `legislation.md` §6)
- **Setting the safety tone of executive communications** — language, posture, response to events; ensuring it's grounded in HOP / Safety II thinking rather than blame or theatre

A senior WHS leader who is purely operational, never engaging at this level, is operating below the role. Likewise, a leader who is only strategic and never operational loses the credibility to influence executive thinking. The role spans both.

## 5. Crisis Management

### Distinct from emergency response

Emergency response is the immediate operational response — minutes to hours — to an incident in progress. Crisis management is what follows — days, weeks, months, sometimes years — when an event threatens the organisation's strategic position, reputation, operating licence, or executive viability.

The two disciplines overlap but require different teams, different playbooks, different cadence, and different leadership.

| | Emergency Response | Crisis Management |
|---|---|---|
| Timeframe | Minutes to hours | Days to months (years for prosecutions) |
| Primary objective | Stabilise the situation; protect people | Manage strategic, legal, reputational, financial exposure |
| Lead | Site / operations lead with WHS support | CEO or appointed Crisis Manager; legal, communications, HR, WHS |
| Activation criteria | Defined operational thresholds (fire, injury, spill) | Defined strategic thresholds (see below) |
| Documentation discipline | Incident report, investigation file | Privileged documents; chronologies; positions; media records |
| External engagement | Emergency services, regulator notification | Regulator, media, family, courts, customers, investors |

A common error is conflating the two — treating a crisis as just a longer incident response. The discipline, the team, and the leadership posture are different. An operational manager who is excellent at emergency response can be entirely wrong as crisis lead.

### Crisis triggers

Activation thresholds — the conditions that elevate an incident or issue into crisis status — should be defined ex-ante and embedded in the crisis management plan. Typical triggers:

| Trigger | Notes |
|---|---|
| Multiple fatality event | Always crisis status; activate immediately |
| Single fatality with significant aggravating factor | Aggravating: viral media, executive presence, contractor with established reputation, indigenous worker, young worker, apprentice |
| Industrial manslaughter charge or investigation | Highest-exposure trigger; potential officer prosecution |
| Hostile regulator action | Inspector with stated intent to prosecute; investigation team site presence |
| Prohibition notice with material operational impact | Sites stopped; revenue impact; cascading customer impact |
| Major incident with environmental dimension | Compounding regulatory exposure (EPA + WorkSafe) |
| Fatality with viral media component | Media presence at site, social media virality, named executives in coverage |
| Prosecution publicity | Court proceedings reaching mainstream media |
| Whistleblower disclosure | Particularly with sustained media interest |
| Major customer / regulator action | Suspension from supplier list; contract termination on safety grounds |
| Significant cyber incident touching safety systems | Integrity of incident reporting, permit systems, training records compromised |

A clear principle: **err on the side of activation.** A crisis team stood up unnecessarily can stand down quickly with minimal cost. A crisis missed in the first 24 hours rarely recovers.

### Crisis management team structure

A typical CMT structure for a mid-large AU/NZ organisation:

```
Crisis Sponsor (CEO)
  |
Crisis Manager (named in plan; typically Group Head WHS, COO,
                 or General Counsel for major exposures)
  |
  +-- Legal (General Counsel + external counsel)
  +-- Communications / Public Affairs
  +-- WHS / Operations
  +-- HR / People (worker support, family liaison, internal comms)
  +-- Finance (insurance engagement, financial impact)
  +-- IT / Cyber (if applicable)
  +-- Government Relations (if applicable)
  +-- External advisors (forensic, crisis comms, specialist legal)
```

Key principles:

- **The Crisis Manager is not always the WHS leader.** For industrial manslaughter or major regulatory exposure, General Counsel often leads. For media-dominant crises, the CEO or Communications lead. For operational continuity crises, the COO.
- **The CEO is always the Sponsor.** They may not be in the room every day, but they own the strategic decisions and the public positions.
- **External advisors are engaged early.** Specialist crisis lawyers, crisis communications firms, forensic engineers, family liaison specialists — engage in the first 24 hours, not when the situation has compounded.
- **Cadence is daily for the first week, then steps down.** Daily CMT meeting (often twice daily for the first 48 hours) with a clear agenda, action register, and chronology document.
- **Documentation is privileged where possible.** All CMT documents marked as confidential and prepared in contemplation of legal proceedings; managed by legal team for privilege preservation.

### Family liaison — the most underappreciated discipline

For fatality and serious injury crises, family liaison is the single most consequential discipline and the one most consistently mishandled.

The principles:

- **A named, trained family liaison is appointed within the first 24 hours.** Not the CEO, not the site manager, not the lawyer. A named role with sole accountability for the family relationship.
- **The liaison is the family's single point of contact.** All communications run through them. The family is never contacted ad-hoc by operations, communications, legal, or HR.
- **First contact is in person where possible**, with appropriate support (clergy, counsellor, cultural representative as relevant).
- **The liaison tells the truth, slowly.** No speculation, no attribution of cause until investigation is complete. "We don't know yet" is the honest answer for the first weeks.
- **Practical support is offered immediately and concretely.** Funeral costs, travel, accommodation, child care, financial guidance, counselling. Specific, paid for, no forms.
- **The liaison continues through the coronial process** — potentially 12–24 months. Family liaison is not a one-week role.
- **The liaison is supported.** This role causes significant vicarious trauma; ensure debrief and clinical support for the liaison themselves.

The strategic logic: the family's perception of how the organisation responded in the first weeks is the single biggest determinant of how they engage with media, legal proceedings, regulators, and the coroner. A family that feels respected, heard, and supported responds materially differently from a family that feels dismissed, unanswered, or treated as a legal exposure to be managed.

This is not cynical — done badly it is far worse than not done at all, because the cynicism is visible. Done well, it is what the organisation owes the family regardless of any other consideration.

### Coronial process management

For workplace fatalities, the coronial process is the dominant external proceeding for the 12–36 months following the event. Coroners have wide investigative powers and produce findings that become public record. The discipline:

- **Engage external coronial counsel early.** This is specialist work — not standard commercial litigation. Use lawyers with demonstrated coronial inquest experience in your jurisdiction.
- **Cooperate fully with the coroner's investigation.** Adversarial posture is counterproductive — the coroner has full powers and is not the regulator. Cooperation supports the family and produces better findings.
- **Anticipate the inquest 6–12 months in advance.** Witness preparation, document preparation, expert engagement, position development. Inquests are not surprise events — they can be prepared for thoroughly.
- **Recommendations from the coroner are public.** The organisation will be expected to respond publicly to any recommendations directed at it. Develop response positions in advance of the inquest, not afterward.
- **The family is present and represented.** Family counsel will ask the questions the family wants asked. Engagement with the family throughout the coronial period — through family liaison — affects the questions and the dynamic.

In most AU jurisdictions, coronial findings on workplace fatalities are referenced in subsequent prosecutions (or absence of prosecutions). The coronial process is also where the systemic issues become matter of public record — handled well, it can be a genuine learning opportunity for the organisation. Handled poorly, it locks in a narrative that follows the organisation for years.

### Media management

The principles:

- **One spokesperson.** Named, prepared, supported. Usually the CEO for major events. Never a rotating cast.
- **Prepared positions.** What we know, what we don't know, what we are doing, what we will do next. Reviewed by legal before each briefing.
- **Speed and accuracy in tension.** Speed builds trust; accuracy preserves position. Resolve in favour of accuracy with rapid follow-up acknowledgment.
- **Legal review before public statements**, particularly during active regulator investigation or prosecution. Some statements will be contested in subsequent proceedings — assume everything said publicly will be put to a witness.
- **Workforce communication first or simultaneously**, never after media. Workers reading about their workplace from external media before hearing internally is a major engagement failure.
- **No "thoughts and prayers" without specific action.** Empty sympathy is read as evasion. Sympathy paired with specific commitments is read as accountability.
- **Acknowledge harm without admitting liability prematurely.** Most jurisdictions have apology legislation that protects expressions of regret — but the protection applies in **civil proceedings only**; it generally does not extend to criminal prosecutions, including WHS prosecutions. Obtain legal advice before any public statement during prosecution exposure. Silence reads as callousness, but specific liability statements are contested in court.

### Legal privilege handling

During crisis — and particularly during prosecution exposure — legal privilege is a critical and contested concept.

The principles:

- **Privileged investigations are conducted at the direction of legal counsel**, with the dominant purpose of providing legal advice or in contemplation of litigation. Standard internal investigations are typically not privileged.
- **Mark all crisis documents** with privilege markings where applicable. The marking is not magic — the test is dominant purpose — but it supports the privilege claim.
- **Operate two parallel processes if needed.** A privileged legal investigation (limited circulation, dominant purpose: legal advice) and an operational investigation (broader circulation, for learning and corrective action). The two are not the same document.
- **Be cautious with email and internal messaging.** Anything not privileged is potentially discoverable. The crisis team operates on the assumption that everything written may be put to a witness in court.
- **Privilege can be inadvertently waived** by sharing privileged documents broadly, by including them in board papers without appropriate marking, or by using them externally. The crisis team protocols must address this explicitly.
- **Privilege is jurisdictionally specific.** AU and NZ have similar but distinct privilege regimes; the discipline must match the relevant jurisdiction(s).

Privilege handling is specialist work. The crisis team must include legal counsel with criminal and regulatory experience, not just commercial lawyers.

### Restorative engagement — Dekker's restorative just culture

For some crises — particularly fatalities and serious injuries involving identifiable workers — a restorative approach (Dekker, 2017 and onward) can run alongside the legal, regulatory, and operational response.

The four restorative questions (from Dekker):
1. **Who is hurt?** — workers, family, colleagues, supervisors, the organisation
2. **What do they need?** — specific to each affected party
3. **Whose obligation is it to meet that need?** — locating responsibility, not blame
4. **How can the future be different?** — system change, not just sanction

In practice, restorative engagement can include:

- Facilitated conversations between the organisation and the family, with skilled facilitators (often clinical psychologists or trained restorative practitioners)
- Worker and team support processes that acknowledge the impact and involve the affected workforce in shaping response
- Public acknowledgment of harm and commitment to specific change, beyond legal requirement
- Memorial and commemoration practices that honour the worker and acknowledge organisational responsibility
- System change processes that explicitly involve the affected family and workforce in design

This is not soft. Restorative practice is harder than legal adversarial response — it requires the organisation to acknowledge harm rather than defend against it, and it requires patience and sustained engagement. But it consistently produces better outcomes for the family, the workforce, and the organisation's long-term position.

Restorative practice runs **alongside**, not instead of, the legal and regulatory response. The organisation still cooperates with the coroner, defends prosecution as appropriate, and meets its statutory obligations. The restorative dimension addresses what the legal processes cannot — the human relationship and the organisational learning.

> See `frameworks.md` §12 (Dekker) for the theoretical grounding.

### Post-crisis review and learning

A crisis is not concluded when the operational situation stabilises or the media interest ends. A structured post-crisis review is essential and typically occurs at three points:

| Review point | Timing | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate hotwash | 1–2 weeks after stand-down | What happened, what went well, what went badly, immediate adjustments |
| Strategic review | 3–6 months post-stand-down | Systemic learning; what does this reveal about the organisation's strategy, function, and risk posture |
| Long-tail review | After all external proceedings conclude (often 18–36 months) | What did the coroner / court / regulator find; what is the long-term learning; what permanent changes are needed |

The strategic review (3–6 months) is most often skipped and most often valuable. By that point the immediate operational adjustments are complete and the organisation can look soberly at what the crisis revealed about its strategy, function design, and culture. Skipping this review is one of the most common failure modes — the organisation moves on without absorbing the systemic implications.
