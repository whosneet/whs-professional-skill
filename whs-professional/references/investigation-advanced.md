# Investigation Reference — Advanced Methods and Protocols

Advanced investigation methods and protocols for lead investigators,
reviewers and WHS managers. Load this file when the task goes past running
a standard ICAM: coding organisational factors for trend data, responding to
a workplace death, sequencing a multi-actor event, working in Tripod Beta or
HFACS, reviewing an investigation before sign-off, or dealing with the
people harmed once the event is over. The core method — ICAM, 5-Why,
AcciMap, bowtie, interviewing, statements and legal privilege — stays in
`investigation.md`; load both files for any serious event. Worked everyday
cases sit in `case-studies-everyday.md`, major-accident analyses in
`case-studies.md`, crisis governance in `strategy-function.md` §5 and the
standard report template in `output-templates.md` §6.

## 1. Scope and Method Selection

Each method answers a different question. Choose on the question, the actual
and potential severity, and who is implicated, not on habit or on what the
incident management system defaults to. Method choice never alters
notification or site preservation (model WHS Act s 38, s 39; OHS Act 2004
(Vic) s 38, s 39; HSWA 2015 s 56, s 55). After a death, or an injury where
death is possible, §3 runs from the first minute, before any method is
chosen.

### What lives where

| Task | Go to |
|---|---|
| Run an ICAM and write the report | `investigation.md` §1, `investigation.md` §5 |
| Quick analysis of a lower-severity event (5-Why) | `investigation.md` §2 |
| Multi-organisation or regulator-level analysis (AcciMap) | `investigation.md` §12 |
| Bowtie, before the event or reused to test barriers after it | `investigation.md` §4, `investigation.md` §13 |
| Interviews, witness statements, legal privilege | `investigation.md` §9, `investigation.md` §10, `investigation.md` §14 |
| Investigation bias, pitfalls, corrective-action framework | `investigation.md` §6, `investigation.md` §7 |
| Code organisational factors for trending (OFTs); calibrate coders | §2 |
| Respond to a workplace death: notification, site, police, coroner, compelled interviews | §3 |
| Sequence a multi-actor event (STEP) | §4 |
| Run or read a Tripod Beta tree; map it to ICAM or bowtie | §5 |
| Code human factors findings for trending (HFACS) | §6 |
| Review a report before sign-off; verify actions; investigator competency; learning reviews | §7 |

### Method selection

| Method | Use when | Output | Limits and when not to use it | Section |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ICAM | Serious incident inside one organisation; operational lessons are the output; a 1–2 day analysis workshop is feasible; the organisation's own procedure mandates it | ICAM chart and report (`investigation.md` §5); organisational factors coded to OFTs (§2) | Several organisations or the regulator implicated: use AcciMap. Pools factors by level, so it does not record which organisational factor defeated which defence, and effective barriers are not a standard chart element (§5) | `investigation.md` §1, `investigation.md` §11 |
| 5-Why | Lower-severity incidents (typically actual severity 1–3), as a supporting technique | Root cause entry on the standard investigation form | Less rigorous for complex or systemic failures; follows one causal chain when incidents usually have several; stops at individual behaviour unless pushed | `investigation.md` §2 |
| AcciMap | Several organisations or PCBUs with overlapping duties; regulatory failure possibly implicated; a royal commission or major inquiry anticipated; industry-wide lessons intended | Hierarchical diagram across six levels, government policy down to equipment and surroundings | Longer engagement, typically 2–4 weeks; needs a chronological account first (STEP can supply it, §4) | `investigation.md` §12 |
| STEP | Three or more actors acting concurrently; automated or remote actors; seconds matter; workers of several PCBUs interleaved; accounts conflict on timing; the emergency response shaped the outcome | Actor-by-time worksheet of tested event blocks with safety problems marked, feeding ICAM, AcciMap, Tripod Beta or HFACS | Shows what happened, not why; event-only, so standing conditions need a side list; single-actor events need only the ICAM timeline | §4 |
| Tripod Beta | A client, parent or joint-venture operator mandates it (common in process safety); events high on the risk matrix, actual or potential; barrier-by-barrier analysis wanted | Tree of trios, barriers and causation paths; underlying causes coded to BRFs | Needs an accredited lead and a priced guide; stops at the organisation's boundary; linear barrier model. Minor, low-potential events: Tripod Lite or the standard method | §5 |
| HFACS | Coding completed investigations for trends across sites, contractors or years; auditing investigation depth by tier fill; a client, parent or sector body mandates it; operator-centred work | Coded dataset of acts, preconditions, supervision and organisational influences | Classifies findings; does not explain a single event; no tier for failed defences or above the organisation; not for psychosocial harm, occupational disease, or findings headed for a disciplinary or restorative process | §6 |
| Learning review | Non-notifiable events with no HiPo potential; repeat low-severity events earlier investigations did not shift; a near miss with a good recovery; a cluster of weak signals; the HSR or crew asks for one | Account of the conditions that shaped actions and decisions, with learning products for different audiences; no cause statement | Not for notifiable or HiPo events, the top severity classes, a foreseeable prosecution, claim or inquest, a client or regulator requiring a causal report, or suspected deliberate conduct. Escalate once HiPo potential or a notifiable event appears | §7 |

### Combining methods

- **Sequence, then analyse.** A STEP worksheet does the work of the ICAM
  Step 3 timeline and populates AcciMap's two lowest levels (§4).
- **Analyse, then code.** Finish the ICAM before any coding. Code
  organisational factors as OFTs (§2) and acts and preconditions with HFACS
  (§6); never publish two competing trend sets.
- **A second method on top-tier events.** Every method finds only what its
  model looks for (§7), so use a second method on top-tier events; senior
  investigators and peer reviewers hold one (§7).
- **Mandated formats.** Where a client requires a Tripod tree and the
  procedure requires ICAM, analyse barrier by barrier once and deliver both
  formats; the ICAM report stays the record (§5).
- **QA and the people harmed.** Every report goes through QA before anyone
  relies on it (§7), and the people harmed are dealt with alongside the
  investigation, not after it (§8).

## 2. ICAM Organisational Factor Types (OFT Codes)

An Organisational Factor Type (OFT) is a two-letter code on each entry in
the Organisational Factors column of the ICAM chart (`investigation.md` §1;
layout in `investigation.md` §5). Within one investigation its only job is
the validation sweep (step 6); the factor statement carries the finding.
Its value is aggregation: one label set turns reports into a dataset.

### Where the list comes from

- The first eight OFTs (HW, TR, OR, CO, IG, PR, MM, DE) match eight of the
  eleven Tripod Basic Risk Factors (§5). Tripod's Housekeeping,
  Error-enforcing Conditions and Defences are not OFTs: ICAM records the
  untidy site and the error-enforcing condition as task or environmental
  conditions and the failed control as a defence, and codes the
  organisational failure behind each to an OFT (often OR, RM or DE).
- The Safety Wise Solutions Incident Investigation Reference Guide (Issue
  02, January 2005) lists 14 OFTs: those eight plus RM, MC, CM, OC, RI and
  OL. OHSA's ICAM Lead Investigator Student Guide (V24.4, January 2025)
  lists 16, adding VM (Vehicle Management) and MS (Management Systems).
- No legal framework or standard fixes the list. It belongs to the ICAM
  method BHP developed with James Reason (`investigation.md` §11) and now
  circulates in training providers' materials, which differ. Confirm which
  list the incident management system holds before coding or comparing
  sites or contractors. Add a local code (roster design, psychosocial
  hazards) only with a definition, a parent OFT and a dated list version.
- Providers also code the other three columns (OHSA: DF, IT, TE and HF).
  Queensland's mining reporting manual (August 2015, Question 24) asked for
  up to three coded factors per ICAM column. Keep a crosswalk from local
  codes to any regulator's list, and compare contractor data coded to
  Tripod BRFs (§5) or HFACS (§6) only at categories that map one-to-one.
- The published RM and CM definitions are written to ALARP. Rewrite them
  to the governing test: SFAIRP under the WHS Acts, the OHS Act 2004 (Vic)
  and HSWA 2015 (NZ) (`frameworks.md` §2); in Queensland mines, risk within
  acceptable limits and as low as reasonably achievable (Coal Mining
  Safety and Health Act 1999 (Qld) s 29; Mining and Quarrying Safety and
  Health Act 1999 (Qld) s 26; `sector-regimes.md` §2); ALARP in an offshore
  petroleum safety case (`sector-regimes.md` §3).

### The 16 OFTs

| Code | Factor type | Latent failure in | Example coded finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| HW | Hardware | Quality, availability and life-cycle stage of tools, equipment, components and materials — not their design (DE) or upkeep (MM) | Purchasing standard allowed unrated lifting slings to be bought on price |
| TR | Training | The system that supplies knowledge and skill: selection, needs analysis, delivery, competency assessment, matching ability to task | No competency verification for elevating work platform operators after licence issue |
| OR | Organisation | Structure of responsibility and accountability; coordination, supervision, planning, resourcing | No role accountable for permit sign-off on night shift |
| CO | Communication | Known information not reaching a known recipient, arriving late, or not confirmed as understood | No structured shift handover; isolation status passed on verbally |
| IG | Incompatible Goals | Conflict between production, cost, schedule and safety goals that leadership has not resolved with a stated priority | Contract KPI penalised late completion with no relief for a safety stop |
| PR | Procedures | How procedures are written, tested, controlled, made accessible and kept workable | Isolation procedure issued without operator trial; unworkable on older plant |
| MM | Maintenance Management | Planning, resourcing and strategy of the maintenance system — not the execution of one job | Preventive maintenance deferred two cycles on budget grounds with no risk review |
| DE | Design | Plant, equipment or workplace design that makes the task difficult or permits unintended use; designer remote from end user | Valve located where operating it means climbing the guardrail |
| RM | Risk Management | The system for identifying hazards, assessing risk, selecting controls and monitoring their effectiveness | Risk assessment process never reached non-routine cleaning tasks |
| MC | Management of Change | Assessment and control of change to plant, process, services, people or structure | Substitute chemical from a new supplier introduced with no change review |
| CM | Contractor Management | Evaluation, selection, engagement, monitoring and retention of contracted services, labour, plant and materials | Subcontractor selected on price with no evaluation of its WHS capability |
| OC | Organisational Culture | Shared values and unwritten rules that govern what people actually do | Interlock bypass openly tolerated by supervisors on every shift |
| RI | Regulatory Influence | Ambiguous, conflicting or duplicated regulatory requirements, or the way oversight shapes practice | Two regulators' overlapping requirements left the site unsure which inspection interval applied |
| OL | Organisational Learning | How lessons from investigations, audits, corrective actions and risk reviews are captured, shared and verified | Same failure recorded in a HiPo 14 months earlier; action closed with no effectiveness check |
| VM | Vehicle Management | The system for purchasing, maintaining and operating vehicles | Fleet specification set without input from the sites operating the vehicles |
| MS | Management Systems | The integrated safety management system and its assurance — audit, inspection, monitoring | Audit program sampled documents only and never tested the control in the field |

### How to code a finding

1. **Finish the analysis first.** Build the chart back from the defences
   (`investigation.md` §1, Steps 4–7) and state each factor as a condition
   before coding. Coding mid-workshop turns the list into a checklist.
2. **Entry test.** The condition existed before the event and (a) produced
   an adverse task or environmental condition or let it persist, (b)
   promoted or tolerated an error or violation, or (c) weakened or removed
   a defence. If not, it belongs in another column.
3. **Evidence test.** Each coded factor needs evidence that it pre-dated
   the event (a document, a record, or consistent accounts from more than
   one person) and a drawn link to another column. No evidence, no code.
4. **One factor, one best-fit OFT.** If two codes fit equally, the statement
   bundles two findings — split it and code each. If nothing fits, leave it
   uncoded and flag it; a forced fit corrupts the trend data.
5. **Code the failed system, not the missing fix.** "No procedure for
   cleaning the conveyor" is PR only if the procedure system failed. If the
   task was never risk-assessed, it is RM.
6. **Validation sweep.** Run forward through the list ("was HW implicated
   anywhere?") to find factors off the causal line. A sweep finding must
   pass steps 2 and 3; a latent weakness with no evidenced link to this
   event is reported as an other finding, uncoded, for the action register.
7. **The team codes; the reviewer confirms** (§7). A code chosen later by
   whoever enters the report into the system is a guess.

| Choosing between | Ruling |
|---|---|
| TR / PR / OR | Test the factor, not the incident: would a competent worker holding an accurate, workable procedure have removed this specific condition? If no, it is not TR or PR; look to OR (supervision, resourcing), RM or DE |
| HW / DE / MM | Wrong or poor-quality item supplied: HW. Item as designed makes the task error-prone: DE. Item degraded because maintenance was not planned or resourced: MM |
| RM / MC | Hazard never identified in steady-state work: RM. Hazard introduced or control degraded by an unassessed change: MC |
| IG / OC | Identifiable goal conflict (KPI, budget, schedule): IG. Unwritten norm with no single source: OC |
| CO / OR | A channel existed and the message failed: CO. Nobody was responsible for sending or receiving it: OR |

### Mapping to the contributing factors taxonomy

OFTs code only the organisational level of `investigation.md` §3. There,
"Procedure quality" is a task/environmental condition (the faulty document
in the worker's hand); PR codes the system that produced it.

| `investigation.md` §3 organisational sub-category | OFT |
|---|---|
| Training / competency | TR |
| Supervision; Resource allocation | OR (IG where the shortfall is a production-over-safety trade-off) |
| Risk assessment / SWMS | RM (PR where the document system failed) |
| Maintenance management; Management of change; Contractor management | MM; MC; CM |
| Communication systems; Organisational learning; Design | CO; OL; DE |
| Organisational culture | OC (explicit production pressure: IG) |
| No primary equivalent sub-category | HW, PR, IG, RI, VM, MS (PR and IG appear above only as secondary rulings) |

### OFT trends as organisational intelligence

- **Count investigations, not statements.** Report the share of
  investigations in which each OFT appears at least once, as a rolling
  12-month profile with n, in the HiPo pack (`analytics.md` §2) and board
  pack (`analytics.md` §7). Raw counts reward teams that write more factors.
  Show a percentage only for cells of 20 or more investigations; below
  that, pool business units or critical risks, or widen the window to
  24–36 months, and show counts.
- **Treat the profile as a pointer** to factor statements worth reading and
  audits worth aiming. Triangulate with CCV results (`analytics.md` §3) and
  audit findings before acting. Never trend individual/team action codes
  as a measure of crews (`frameworks.md` §5).
- **Check action alignment.** MC coded and "retrain the crew" actioned is a
  mismatch (`investigation.md` §7). Track the share of TR- and PR-coded
  factors closed with administrative actions only (§7).

Read the profile with these cautions:

- **Small numbers.** 6 of 20 investigations coded MC is 30%, 95% interval
  roughly 15% to 52% (Wilson). Moving to 9 of 20 next year is not a trend
  (`analytics.md` §9).
- **Selection.** Only events that triggered an ICAM are coded; the profile
  shifts whenever trigger thresholds change.
- **Taxonomy.** A code set returns only what it contains: "what you look
  for is what you find" (Lundberg, Rollenhagen and Hollnagel, 2009).
  Without local codes, roster design, workload and psychosocial hazards
  vanish into OR or IG. The codes name what failed, never why the same work
  normally succeeds (`frameworks.md` §4).
- **Privilege.** Code from the operational ICAM only. Lifting codes from a
  legal-led report into an operational dataset puts privilege at risk
  (`investigation.md` §14).

### Common coding errors

| Error | How it shows | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Defaulting to TR and PR | TR and PR dominate the profile; actions are retrain and rewrite | Apply the TR / PR / OR ruling; reviewer challenges every TR and PR code |
| Coding symptoms | An action or workface condition carries an OFT ("operator did not isolate" coded PR) | Apply the entry test (step 2): name the decision, process or practice that let the act or condition occur, code that, and leave the act in its own column |
| Evidence-free OC | "Culture" coded on the investigator's impression | Require evidence of the unwritten rule from more than one source |
| MS as catch-all | MS appears on most reports | MS only where monitoring and assurance itself failed; otherwise use the specific code |
| Double-coding | One fact carried under three codes | One factor, one code; split only where evidence shows two conditions |
| Outcome-driven coding | Serious outcomes attract more codes than near misses with the same facts | Reviewer codes blind to outcome (`investigation.md` §6) |
| Coder drift | Step change in the profile coinciding with a new investigator, training provider or edited list; investigators covering the same operation return different profiles | Split the profile by lead investigator; version-control the list; annotate the trend chart; re-code a sample |

### Inter-rater calibration

Coding schemes are often less reliable than users assume. Olsen and
Shorrock (2010) found low agreement between coders, and for the same coder
months apart, applying an ADF adaptation of HFACS (§6) in an air traffic
control unit. Measure your own reliability before reporting trends upward.

- **When.** Yearly, and whenever the list or the investigator pool changes.
- **Method.** Each investigator independently codes the same 50 or more
  de-identified factor statements from closed investigations, pooled over
  two or three sessions if needed. On 20 statements a kappa of 0.69 (75%
  agreement, 20% expected by chance) has a 95% interval of about 0.45 to
  0.92, too wide to test any threshold.
- **Measure.** Percent agreement plus Cohen's kappa (two coders) or Fleiss'
  kappa (three or more), with its 95% interval. Percent agreement alone
  flatters: chance agreement is high when two or three codes dominate.
- **Benchmark.** Landis and Koch (1977) offered arbitrary bands, not
  validated cut-offs: 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81–1.00
  almost perfect. Report trends to the ELT or board only when the lower
  confidence bound clears 0.61. Below it, fix the definitions, not coders.
- **Output.** Turn every disagreement into a written ruling with a worked
  example in a local coding guide; the guide holds the coding steady.
- **At sign-off.** For top-severity and HiPo investigations a second
  investigator codes the organisational factors blind (§7).

## 3. Fatality Response — First 24 Hours, Police and Coroner

A workplace death starts four processes inside the first hour: emergency
response, a police investigation for the coroner, a WHS regulator
investigation and the organisation's own response. Each runs on a different
statute with different rules about who must answer what. Apply this
protocol to any death and to any injury where death is possible. Crisis
governance (crisis team, media, family liaison) is in `strategy-function.md`
§5, the notification script in `output-templates.md` §23, and definitions
and contacts in `legislation.md` §5 and `legislation.md` §7. This section
covers the statutory and evidentiary mechanics, as at October 2026.

### Immediate statutory duties

| Duty | Model WHS Act (NSW, QLD, WA, SA, TAS, ACT, NT, Cth) | VIC — OHS Act 2004 | NZ — HSWA 2015 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Notify the regulator | s 38 — PCBU, immediately after becoming aware, by the fastest possible means (phone); written notice within 48 hours only if the regulator requires it; keep the record 5 years | s 38 — employer or self-employed person phones WorkSafe immediately; written record within 48 hours in every case; keep it 5 years | s 56 — PCBU, as soon as possible, by the fastest means; WorkSafe expects an immediate phone call for a death; keep records 5 years (s 57) |
| Preserve the site | s 39 — person with management or control of the workplace; site and associated plant, substances and structures undisturbed until an inspector arrives or directs earlier | s 39(1) — undisturbed until an inspector arrives or directs otherwise | s 55(1) — PCBU who manages or controls the workplace; undisturbed until an inspector authorises. An inspector can also require the site be left undisturbed for a reasonable period (s 168(1)(d)) |
| Permitted disturbance | Assist an injured person; remove the deceased (in practice only at police or coroner direction — the body is under the coroner's control); make the site safe or minimise the risk of a further incident; anything associated with a police investigation; inspector or regulator permission (s 39(3)) | Protect a person's health or safety; aid an injured person; essential action to make the site safe or prevent a recurrence (s 39(2)) | Assist an injured person; remove a deceased person; make the site safe or minimise the risk of a further notifiable event; anything done by or under the direction of a constable; inspector or regulator permission (s 55(2)). The duty does not apply while TAIC or an Armed Forces Discipline Act 1971 investigation is running (s 55(3)) |
| Report the death | Anyone aware of an unreported reportable death must report it to police or a coroner (Coroners Acts, below). If police did not attend, phone them and record the event number | Same (Coroners Act 2008 s 12) | Report to Police unless they already know (Coroners Act 2006 ss 13–14) |

| Situation | What changes |
|---|---|
| Several PCBUs on site | Every PCBU holds the s 38 duty. Agree who phones, get the reference number and share it — never assume the principal has called |
| Worker dies in hospital days later | A new notifiable incident (s 35) and a reportable death — in QLD a death at any time after the causative injury counts (Coroners Act 2003 s 8(6)). Phone again |
| Suspected work-related suicide | The Model WHS Legislation Amendment (Incident Notification) 2025, published by Safe Work Australia in December 2025, adds notifiable suicide (s 35B), notifiable extended absence (s 35A) and violent incidents (s 37A). | Mining, maritime, rail, heavy vehicle | A sector regulator is engaged too (regime selection in `sector-regimes.md` §1; each regime in `sector-regimes.md` §2, §3, §5 and §14). In NZ, Maritime NZ or the CAA may act as regulator (`legislation.md` §3) |
| Work-vehicle crash on a public road | Still a notifiable death — a vehicle is a workplace (s 8(2)). Police crash investigators lead; chain of responsibility applies (`sector-regimes.md` §14) |
| Death interstate, offshore or overseas | The coroner and regulator where it happened take jurisdiction; some Coroners Acts also reach residents who die elsewhere (Coroners Act 1997 (ACT) s 13(3)) |
| Site release | Needs two answers: police (holding the scene for the coroner) and the inspector, who can issue non-disturbance notices of up to 7 days each, renewable by further notices (ss 198–201; NZ HSWA ss 108–111). Record who released it and when |

### Who attends, and in what capacity

| Attendee | Capacity | What they can do in the first 24 hours |
|---|---|---|
| Police | Investigate the death for the coroner and compile the coronial brief; separately assess criminal offences such as manslaughter | Control the scene; take statements; identify the deceased; notify next of kin; arrange transfer to the mortuary |
| WHS inspector | Investigates offences under the WHS Act — Categories 1–3, officer due diligence, industrial manslaughter (`legislation.md` §6) | Enter without warrant (s 163); require documents and answers (s 171); seize items (s 175); issue notices — full table in `legislation.md` §10 |
| Coroner | Judicial officer controlling the body and the death investigation; rarely attends | Directs police; orders the autopsy; can require information and documents; decides whether to hold an inquest |
| HSR | Workers' elected representative | Inspect without notice after an incident (s 68(2)(a)(ii)); accompany the inspector; sit in on a worker's interview with the worker's consent (s 68(2)(c)). VIC: inspect immediately after an incident and accompany the inspector (OHS Act 2004 s 58(1)(a)(ii), (b)) |
| WHS entry permit holder (model s 117; not WA or VIC) | Union official inquiring into a suspected contravention affecting a relevant worker | Inspect, consult workers, copy directly relevant documents (s 118). Notice as soon as reasonably practicable after entry (s 119). QLD: notice 24 hours to 14 days before entry, unless the permit holder reasonably believes a relevant worker faces a serious risk from an immediate or imminent hazard (WHS Act 2011 (Qld) s 119(3)–(5)) |
| Union official — WA, VIC, NZ | WA: Industrial Relations Act 1979 (WA), WAIRC-issued permit — the WHS Act 2020 (WA) has no entry permit scheme. VIC: authorised representative of a registered employee organisation (OHS Act 2004 s 87). ### Compelled answers — the rule depends on who is asking

| Questioner | Must the person answer? | Self-incrimination |
|---|---|---|
| Police | No general duty to answer; statements are voluntary | Privilege intact. Anyone who may be a suspect gets independent legal advice before any statement |
| Inspector (s 171) or regulator (s 155 notice) — model WHS Act, not SA | Yes. s 171: at the workplace or, within 30 days of entry, by written notice to produce documents, give written answers or attend an interview (s 171(2A) — in force in at least the Cth, QLD and the ACT; check the local Act elsewhere). s 155: the regulator's written notice to give information, produce documents or attend and give evidence (`legislation.md` §10). Refusal without reasonable excuse is an offence | Abrogated (s 172(1)). The answer is not admissible against that individual in civil or criminal proceedings, except over its falsity (s 172(2)). No immunity for a body corporate. Under the model wording, evidence derived from the answer stays usable — except in QLD and the ACT, whose s 172(2) also covers evidence obtained directly or indirectly from it. Legal professional privilege survives (s 269) |
| Inspector or regulator — SA | Yes (WHS Act 2012 (SA) ss 155, 171) | Not abrogated for individuals: a natural person is excused from answering, or giving information or a document, that may tend to incriminate them (WHS Act 2012 (SA) s 172). A body corporate has no privilege |
| Inspector — VIC | Yes (OHS Act 2004 s 100) | A natural person may refuse to give information that would tend to incriminate them, but must still produce required documents (s 154); the inspector must tell them so (s 100(3)) |
| Inspector — NZ | The PCBU or person in charge must produce information and make statements as the inspector specifies (HSWA s 168(1)(e)–(f)); anyone with a duty under the Act must give reasonable assistance (s 176); name and address where an offence is suspected (s 175); obstruction is an offence and has been prosecuted (s 179) | Preserved: s 168 does not affect the privilege in Evidence Act 2006 s 60 (HSWA s 168(5)). Take NZ legal advice before any individual is interviewed |
| Coroner at inquest | Yes, on summons | The coroner may require incriminating evidence, which is then protected in other proceedings (NSW Coroners Act 2009 s 61 certificate; QLD Coroners Act 2003 s 39; VIC Coroners Act 2008 s 57 certificate; ACT Coroners Act 1997 s 51B) |

- The immunity attaches only to a compelled answer. The inspector must
  first give the s 173 warning; an informal chat is voluntary evidence with
  no immunity (s 173(3)). Have the record show s 171 was invoked.
- Interview rights: model s 171(1)(c) requires answers on the spot while
  the inspector is at the workplace; QLD requires attendance at a stated
  reasonable time and place. Private on request (s 171(3)); a
  representative may attend (s 171(4)), and a s 171(2A) notice must say so
  (s 173(1A)(e)); a person told to attend in person may ask for an audio
  or audiovisual link (s 171(2B)).
- The company's lawyers act for the company. A supervisor or officer with
  personal exposure needs separate advice; fund it, but do not direct it.
- Never tell workers not to talk to police or inspectors, or sit a manager
  in a worker's interview uninvited. Obstruction is an offence (s 188).

### Evidence and parallel investigations

Expect four investigations: police and coronial, regulator, the operational
investigation (`investigation.md` §1) and, where legal commissions it, a
privileged one (`investigation.md` §14). Decisions for the first four hours:

- **Privilege structure before the first document.** Purpose is judged
  document by document at creation. Notes, photographs and first accounts
  made outside a legal-led process are producible to the inspector (s 171;
  s 155 notices — `legislation.md` §10) and to the coroner. Write facts only.
- **Legal hold.** Suspend auto-deletion of CCTV, telematics, access logs,
  radio recordings, email and messaging. Quarantine the SWMS, permits,
  pre-starts, training records and plant maintenance history as found.
  Nothing is completed, corrected or signed after the event.
- **Evidence log.** List every item seized or copied and by whom; keep
  copies of what you hand over; get receipts (`legislation.md` §10).
- **Witnesses.** Record names and contacts. Sequence internal interviews
  (`investigation.md` §9) behind police and the inspector unless they agree.
- **Drug and alcohol testing.** Police testing runs under police powers
  and is separate from yours. Test only under an existing, consulted policy
  (`hazards.md` §5) that covers the defined group, not just the person
  nearest the event; a result is one input, never the finding on cause.
- **Officer exposure.** Industrial manslaughter, Category 1 and s 27 due
  diligence (`legislation.md` §6; VIC OHS Act 2004 ss 39G, 144; NZ HSWA s 44,
  no industrial manslaughter offence) turn on what officers knew and did
  beforehand; conduct afterwards is evidence too. Brief the board through
  counsel. Nobody records a view on cause, fault or discipline on day one.

### Family, workers and communications

- **Next of kin.** Police deliver the death notification; give them the
  emergency-contact details immediately. Nobody from the organisation
  phones the family, confirms the name or posts anything until police
  confirm the family has been told. Then a senior leader and the named
  family liaison make contact (`strategy-function.md` §5). Give the family
  the coroner's and the regulator's own contacts and support services.
- **Worker welfare.** The primary duty covers psychological health.
  Identify the exposed group — witnesses, first aiders, would-be rescuers,
  the crew, whoever ran the plant or supervised the job — and give
  psychological first aid: safety, practical help, accurate information,
  connection to family and support. Nobody in shock drives home alone.
  Check in within 24 hours, then at 72 hours, one week and one month. A
  worker who may feel responsible gets a named support person who is
  neither their line manager nor an investigator.
- **Internal first, then the holding statement.** Once the family knows,
  the workforce hears before the media: the facts in one sentence, what is
  stopped, what support exists, a request not to post. Then one spokesperson
  issues a legally reviewed statement: incident, sympathy, cooperation; no
  name, no cause.
- **Insurers.** Notify the workers compensation insurer
  (`compensation-rtw.md` §5; death benefits `compensation-rtw.md` §3; NZ
  ACC `compensation-rtw.md` §4) and the liability insurer before incurring
  legal costs. Insuring WHS
  fines is banned in every AU jurisdiction except TAS and the NT (as at
  mid-2026) and in NZ (HSWA s 29); defence costs stay insurable
  (`specialist-topics.md` §5).

### Coronial process

Police report the death, the coroner takes control of the body and orders
any autopsy, and police assemble the brief from their statements, the
regulator's file and your documents. The coroner finds who died, when,
where, how and from what cause, and may comment on prevention. Findings
cannot say a person is guilty of an offence (NSW s 81(3); QLD s 45(5); VIC
s 69; TAS s 28), but the evidence is open to the regulator and plaintiffs.

| Jurisdiction | Act | Points that change what you do |
|---|---|---|
| NSW | Coroners Act 2009 | Violent or unnatural death is reportable (s 6); anyone with reasonable grounds must report it to police or a coroner as soon as possible (s 35); inquest mandatory for suspected homicide, a death in custody or police operation, or where identity, date, place, cause or manner is not disclosed (s 27), otherwise discretionary; relatives presumed to have standing, others by leave on sufficient interest (s 57); coroner must suspend if a person is charged with an indictable offence that raises whether they caused the death (s 78) |
| QLD | Coroners Act 2003 | Violent or otherwise unnatural death is reportable (s 8); report immediately unless already reported (s 7); inquest mandatory for a mining related reportable death (s 27), otherwise a public-interest discretion (s 28); no inquest until charges in which causing the death is in issue are finalised (s 29); standing on sufficient interest (s 36); coroner must pass suspected offences to the DPP or the administering department (s 48) |
| VIC | Coroners Act 2008 | Unexpected, unnatural or violent death, or one resulting from accident or injury, is reportable (s 4); inquest discretionary for a workplace death (s 52); interested party by leave (s 56); a public statutory authority or entity that receives a recommendation must respond in writing within 3 months, and the response is published (s 72) |
| TAS | Coroners Act 1995 | A death at, or from an accident or injury at, the person's place of work is expressly reportable (s 3); inquest mandatory unless the coroner is satisfied of natural causes (s 24(1)(ea)), though the senior next of kin may ask within 14 days that none be held (s 26A) |
| ACT | Coroners Act 1997 | Coroner must hold an inquest where a person dies violently or unnaturally in unknown circumstances, or after an accident to which the death appears directly attributable (s 13(1)(a), (g)), but may dispense with a hearing (s 34A); failing to report is an offence (s 77); a person facing adverse comment gets 14 to 28 days to respond (s 55); standing by leave (s 42) |
| WA, SA, NT | Coroners Act 1996 (WA); Coroners Act 2003 (SA); Coroners Act 1993 (NT) | Same architecture — reportable death, police investigating for the coroner, standing by leave, prevention recommendations |
| NZ | Coroners Act 2006 | Unnatural, violent or unexplained death reported to Police (ss 13–14); hearing on the papers (s 77) or inquest (s 80); inquiry can be postponed or adjourned while a person may be charged (s 68) or another statutory investigation, such as WorkSafe's, runs (s 69); anyone a proposed recommendation is directed at gets 20 working days to comment, and comments can be published (s 57B) |

The statutory bar covers only charges that put causing the death in issue
(QLD s 29; NSW s 78), but coroners usually also wait for a WHS prosecution
to finish (`strategy-function.md` §5). Seek leave to appear once an inquest
is listed; individuals at risk of adverse comment need their own lawyer.
Put completed corrective action before the coroner and draft the response
to likely recommendations before the hearing.

### Timeline — do and do not

| Window | Do | Do not |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 h | Call 000 (NZ 111); rescue and first aid without creating a second casualty; isolate energy and make safe; cordon the scene and post a sentry; account for every worker; start a time-stamped log; escalate to the duty executive, WHS lead and legal; phone the regulator | Move plant, tools or the deceased beyond what rescue and make-safe require; clean up; allow photos or posts by bystanders; delay the regulator call to "get the facts straight" |
| 1–4 h | Give police and the inspector one named liaison; record names, agencies and every requirement made; engage WHS counsel and settle the privilege structure; issue the legal hold; give police the next-of-kin details; separate and support witnesses; notify the principal, client and other PCBUs; stand the crew down on pay | Contact the family before police have; let managers pre-interview witnesses or collect written accounts; message or email speculation about cause; hand over documents without listing them |
| 4–24 h | Brief the workforce, then issue the holding statement; family liaison makes first contact once police confirm notification; notify insurers; confirm in writing what each agency was given; arrange independent advice for individuals asked to interview; appoint the operational investigation lead and terms of reference (team independent of the work area; quality checks in §7) — interviews wait on police and the inspector; stop like work elsewhere until its controls are verified; brief the board | Restart because the inspector has left — confirm police release too; announce a cause, a "breach of procedure" or disciplinary action; run compulsory group debriefs (evidence note in `specialist-topics.md` §2); complete or backdate any record |
| Days 2–7 | Start investigation fieldwork and, separately, any privileged investigation; answer s 155 and s 171 notices through one channel by the due date; welfare check-ins at 72 hours and 7 days, and diarise the one-month check; interim controls across like work, chosen down the hierarchy — eliminate, isolate or engineer out the energy before relying on procedure, supervision or PPE (model WHS Regulations reg 36); attend the funeral only as the family wishes; open restorative contact (§8) | Park the investigation until the regulator reports — that can take years; circulate privileged work; let family contact lapse after the funeral; read the inspector's silence as closure (limitation periods below) |

Keep the legal hold until every process has closed (`legislation.md` §6):

| Regime | When a prosecution can still start |
|---|---|
| Model s 232 | Within the latest of 2 years after the offence comes to the regulator's notice and 1 year after a coronial report or inquest that discloses an offence; Category 1 later on fresh evidence (s 232(2)). ACT: any offence out of time with the court's leave (s 232(2)–(3)) |
| Industrial manslaughter | No limitation period (e.g. QLD s 232(3); ACT s 232(4)); VIC workplace manslaughter at any time (OHS Act 2004 s 132(3)) |
| VIC other indictable offences | Model-style periods, or at any time with the DPP's written authorisation (OHS Act 2004 s 132(1)(d)) |
| NZ | The latest of 12 months from when the regulator knew or ought to have known (the District Court can extend this by up to 12 months) and 6 months after a coroner's certificate of findings that discloses an offence (HSWA ss 146–147) |

### Common traps

- Treating the inspector as the only investigator. Police and the coroner
  run on separate powers and timelines, and a police statement carries no
  use immunity.
- Assuming the s 172 immunity protects the company, that the wider QLD and
  ACT immunity applies elsewhere, or that SA, VIC and NZ rules match the
  model Act — individuals there keep the privilege.
- First-hour emails naming a cause or a person. They are disclosable, and
  they fix the investigation on the individual instead of the system that
  set the work up (`investigation.md` §6).
- Standing the involved worker down unsupported — it reads as blame.

## 4. STEP — Sequentially Timed Events Plotting

STEP is a multi-linear sequencing method set out by Kingsley Hendrick and
Ludwig Benner Jr in *Investigating Accidents with STEP* (Marcel Dekker,
1987). It shares its actor-by-time worksheet of event building blocks with
Benner's multilinear events sequencing (MES), developed from investigation
work at the US National Transportation Safety Board. The premise: an
accident is not one chain. Several actors, people and things, act at once,
and the harm comes from how their actions interlock. Sklet's NTNU review
(2002) classes STEP as a primary, stand-alone method with its own
safety-problem and change-analysis steps, but its output is a tested
account of *what happened*, not *why*. Where ICAM is the organisation's
method (`investigation.md` §1), run STEP as the sequencing engine for ICAM
or AcciMap.

### When STEP earns its place

The ICAM sequence-of-events table (`investigation.md` §5) holds one line of
events in time order, with no actor dimension. For single-actor events (a
slip, a strain, a hand tool injury) that and the ICAM Step 3 timeline
(`investigation.md` §1) are enough. Build a STEP worksheet when:

| Trigger | Why a single-line timeline fails |
|---|---|
| Three or more actors acting concurrently | "Meanwhile" events are flattened into a false one-after-another order |
| Automated or remote actors (control system, interlock, remote operator) | What the system did drops out of a people-only narrative |
| Seconds matter — vehicles, mobile plant, rail, cranes, process upsets | The order of actions across actors decides who could have known or done what |
| Workers of several PCBUs interleaved (`legislation.md` §4) | Hides where one organisation's action set up another's |
| Accounts conflict on timing | Nothing forces the conflict into the open |
| The emergency response shaped the outcome | Response actions ran in parallel with the escalating event |

### Building the worksheet

1. **Fix the start and end**: from the first event that turned planned
   work into the accident process to the last connected harmful event.
   If unsure, start earlier and trim later.
2. **List the actors**, one row each: any person or thing whose action
   changed the course of events (driver, drive unit, controller, load).
   One name per actor, used without variation.
3. **Write event building blocks** — one actor, one action (physical, or
   mental for a person), active voice — recording time (and duration where
   it matters), location and evidence source. Flag blocks that are
   inferred, and mental blocks as the actor's account.
4. **Place each block** on its actor's row under a time mark. The scale
   need not be linear; order and simultaneity are what count.
5. **Draw linking arrows** only where the earlier event was necessary for
   the later one; dashed while the link is unproven.
6. **Run the three tests**, **BackSTEP** each gap, **mark safety problems**.

Block-writing rules, after Benner — each reject hides a gap or a judgement:

| Reject | Example | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| Collective actor | "The crew removed the guard" | "Technician removed the belt guard" |
| Passive voice | "The override was released" | "Help desk operator released the override" |
| Pronoun | "He restarted it" | Named actor, named object |
| Opinion or editorial adjective | "incorrectly isolated", "misjudged" | The action, physical or mental, without the judgement: "selected Off at the panel"; "read the override as left on from an earlier job". Mental blocks show why an action made sense at the time |
| Non-event | "did not lock out" | What the actor did do; raise the omission as a safety problem against the documented expected step |

### The three tests and BackSTEP

| Test | Question | Fail signal | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Row | Is this actor's row complete from start to end, and is the actor broken down far enough? | Actor vanishes for a period; one block hides several actions | Add blocks; split the actor into components |
| Column | Did this event occur after everything to its left, before everything to its right, and at the same time as everything in its own column? | Two sources place one event in different columns | Reconcile the time sources. For conflicting accounts, enter each version as a tentative block tagged with its source; carry the one that logs or physical evidence support over unaided recall and record why; if nothing separates them, report both |
| Necessary and sufficient | Were the linked earlier events enough on their own to produce this one? | "Yes, but only if..." | Find the missing event, add it, link it |

A gap on the worksheet is a gap in understanding. **BackSTEP** works
backwards from the event right of the gap ("what could have led to
that?"), writing a tentative block for each answer. Treat each pathway as
a hypothesis and seek the evidence that separates them. Tentative blocks
never enter the report as fact; an open gap is reported as a gap.

### Worked example — Meridian Facilities Group

Fictional. A Meridian Facilities Group technician on a university contract
is changing the supply fan belt on air handling unit AHU-3 when the fan
starts and the belt crushes two fingers. BMS = building management system;
VSD = variable speed drive. Eight blocks only; a real worksheet has more.

| Actor | 09:40 | 09:42 | 09:50 | 09:52 | 09:53:05 | 09:53:06 | 09:53:09 | 09:53:10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Technician | E1 selects AHU-3 "Off" at plant-room BMS panel | E2 removes belt guard | | E4 seats new belt on motor pulley by hand (continues) | | | | |
| Lecturer | | | E3 phones help desk: theatre hot | | | | | |
| Help desk operator | | | | | E5 releases AHU-3 override to "Auto" | | | |
| BMS controller | | | | | | E6 sends start command to VSD | | |
| VSD and motor | | | | | | | E7 ramps fan motor | |
| Fan belt | | | | | | | | E8 draws technician's fingers into pulley |

Sources: BMS audit log (E1, E5, E6); VSD event log (E7); call record (E3);
interview (E2, E4, E8). Links: E1→E2→E4→E8; E3→E5→E6→E7→E8; E1→E5.

- **BackSTEP.** Nothing first linked into E7. "What could have led to the
  drive ramping?" gave BMS start, VSD keypad start or fire-mode signal. The
  BMS log confirmed E5 and E6; VSD and fire panel logs excluded the rest.
- **Necessary and sufficient, E7.** A start command runs the motor only if
  the drive is still live. No block shows the isolator being opened — E1
  was a software stop. That is safety problem 1.

STEP's change analysis then identifies counter-changes, ranks their safety
effect, weighs trade-offs, selects and quality-checks. Rank by the
hierarchy of controls and SFAIRP (`frameworks.md` §1, §2) and run the
selected actions through `investigation.md` §7:

| # | Link | Safety problem | Counter-changes, highest order first |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | E1→E4 | Hands on the belt drive on a BMS software stop: no personal lock and tag at the local isolator, no try-start to verify zero energy (`hazards.md` §10; `inspections-audits-permits.md` §4; `case-studies-critical-risk.md` §4) | Lockable isolator at the unit, within reach of the task; then personal lock, tag and try-start before hands go on the drive |
| 2 | E1→E5 | Override showed no owner or reason at the remote workstation | BMS maintenance lock-out that shows owner and reason and that the help desk cannot release |
| 3 | E3→E5 | Comfort call closed by restarting plant with no check to site | No plant restart to close a comfort call without a site check |
| 4 | E6→E7 | Remote start needed no local permissive | Local permissive before any remote start |

"Retrain the technician" answers none of the four. Read down the 09:53
columns: neither the operator nor the technician could see the other's
action. That does not excuse the missing isolation; it shows isolation was
the only barrier independent of another person's knowledge, and hands ICAM
the real question: why a BMS stop was accepted as enough on this contract.
A single-line timeline buries both findings under "failed to isolate".

### From worksheet to analysis

- **Find safety problems** a block and an arrow at a time: does the
  earlier event's effect on the later one need safety action? The original
  marks them with diamonds; the Norwegian SINTEF variant uses triangles.
- **Do not pick "the cause".** The worksheet shows many event pairs;
  naming one narrows attention to one problem (`investigation.md` §6, root
  cause illusion). Report the safety problems plurally.
- **Into ICAM.** The worksheet does the work of the Step 3 timeline:
  derive the report's sequence-of-events table (`investigation.md` §5)
  from it and append the worksheet. Each safety problem becomes an absent
  or failed defence or an individual/team action, driven down to
  conditions and organisational factors and coded
  (§2).
- **Into AcciMap.** Blocks populate the two lowest levels, actor
  activities and equipment and surroundings (`investigation.md` §12); the
  upper levels come from asking why each safety problem existed. The same
  sequence feeds Tripod Beta or HFACS (§5, §6).

### Strengths and limits

| Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|
| Shows concurrency and one-to-many, many-to-one links a single line cannot | Describes what happened, not why. In Herrera and Woltjer's STEP and FRAM comparison (2008) the diagram showed a radio frequency change came late, not why it did. Systemic factors depend on the follow-on method and the analyst |
| Testable — a reviewer can re-run the three tests (§7) | Event-only: standing conditions (roster, design, contract terms) have no home on the worksheet. Hold them in a side list for ICAM, or use a distinct "state" symbol as Zotov did |
| Quick to learn: Sklet's NTNU review (2002) rates it usable by a novice after orientation. Doing it well takes practice; Zotov found MES hard to teach and advises building skill on small events first | Bimodal success/failure view of one sequence, silent on how the same work normally succeeds. Herrera and Woltjer find it suits tractable systems whose functioning is known; for tightly coupled, less tractable work add a systemic method such as FRAM (`frameworks.md` §4) |
| Gaps are visible early, while evidence can still be collected | Arrows invite tidy linear cause-and-effect; large events produce unwieldy worksheets (split by phase, below) |
| Language rules strip blame words out of the sequence | Only as good as its time sources |

### Practice notes and traps

- **Clock offsets.** CCTV, control-system logs, vehicle telematics, phone
  and radio records each keep their own time. Establish every offset
  against one reference before placing blocks, and note the correction.
- **Build it during evidence gathering**, on a wall with sticky notes, so
  gaps direct the next interview or data request. Interview first: showing
  a witness the worksheet before free recall contaminates the account
  (`investigation.md` §9). Then use each block to ask why that action made
  sense at the time.
- **Large events.** When the worksheet can no longer be read at a glance,
  or separate teams own separate phases, split it by phase (lead-up, event,
  emergency response) on one time reference, repeating boundary blocks.
- **Review tool.** The method also audits a finished report. Dmitri
  Zotov's MES re-analysis of the TAIC report on the 1995 Ansett Dash 8
  accident near Palmerston North found links that showed sequence only.
- **Records.** The worksheet and source list are producible: a regulator
  can compel them (model WHS Act s 155 notice, s 171 inspector powers on
  entry; NZ HSWA 2015 equivalents, `legislation.md` §10), as can a coroner.
  Only properly established legal professional privilege resists
  production; operational records such as BMS logs are not privileged. A
  legal-led worksheet stays in the privileged circle
  (`investigation.md` §10, §14). Keep dated snapshots, a source register
  and a change log.

## 5. Tripod Beta

Shell-lineage barrier-based analysis (`investigation.md` §11): enough to
choose it, read a tree, join a Tripod team and map it to ICAM or a bowtie.

### Origins and custodianship

- **Research** — Shell-funded work from the late 1980s (SPE 88488 gives
  1986) by Willem Wagenaar (Leiden) and James Reason (Manchester); it also
  fed Reason's organisational accident model (`frameworks.md` §12).
  Patrick Hudson co-authored the 1994 Tripod Delta paper (the proactive
  questionnaire tool); Beta is the reactive incident tree. Shell-era trees
  say hazard–target–event and active/latent failure: translate them.
- **Custodian** — Shell passed the methodology to the Stichting Tripod
  Foundation (Netherlands) in 1998. The Energy Institute (London), its
  partner since 2012, publishes the guide and administers accreditation.
- **Current guide** (as at October 2026) — *Tripod Beta: Guidance on using
  Tripod Beta in the investigation and analysis of incidents, accidents and
  business losses*, Version 6 (2023), ISBN 978 1 78725 361 2; priced.

### When to use it

| Situation | Method |
|---|---|
| A client, parent or joint-venture operator mandates Tripod (common in process safety) | Tripod Beta, led by an accredited practitioner |
| High on the risk matrix, actual or potential, or significant actual harm | Full Tripod Beta (the Tripod Beta User Guide's own threshold) |
| Minor, low-potential incidents and near misses | Tripod Lite (free), or the organisation's standard method |
| The organisation's own procedure mandates ICAM | ICAM (`investigation.md` §1). If a client contract also requires a tree, analyse barrier by barrier once and deliver both formats; the ICAM report stays the record |
| Several organisations or the regulator are implicated; Tripod stops at the organisation's boundary | AcciMap (`investigation.md` §12) |
| The aim is to understand normal work, not a defeated barrier | Learning Team (`frameworks.md` §5) |

### The core model

| Question | Tree element | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| What happened? | **Trio** — an *agent* (the hazard or energy) acts on an *object* (the person or thing harmed or changed) to produce an *event* | AND gate: agent and object must both be present. An event can become the agent or object of the next trio, so trios chain left to right in time order |
| How? | **Barrier** — on the agent–event leg (containing the agent) or the object–event leg (protecting the object) | Classify each: failed, missing, inadequate or effective |
| Why? | **Immediate cause** — the action, omission or occurrence that defeated the barrier | Exactly one per failed barrier; none for missing or inadequate barriers. Human actions typed as slip, lapse, mistake or violation (`frameworks.md` §12; HFACS, §6) |
| | **Precondition** — the situational or psychological state that made the immediate cause likely (haste, unfamiliarity, competing demands) | Several can feed one immediate cause |
| | **Underlying cause** — the organisational deficiency that created the precondition | System-level actions attach here. One causation path per barrier, not per incident |

| Barrier state | Meaning | Causation path |
|---|---|---|
| Failed | In place, defeated by an act, omission or occurrence | Immediate cause, precondition(s), underlying cause(s) |
| Missing | Called for by the organisation's own controls or recognised practice; never established | Straight to underlying cause |
| Inadequate | Established, but incapable of stopping this agent | Straight to underlying cause |
| Effective | Restored control or prevented further harm after an actual event | None; record it on the trio whose event it prevented |

### The Basic Risk Factors (BRFs)

Eleven codes for underlying causes, shared with Tripod Delta; optional on
one tree, valuable as a trend across many. Labels vary by edition. ICAM's
OFT codes (§2) overlap; never mix the sets.

| Code | BRF | One-line meaning |
|---|---|---|
| DE | Design | Layout or design of plant, equipment or tools that invites misuse or error |
| HW | Hardware | Poor quality, condition or availability of materials, tools and equipment |
| MM | Maintenance management | The system for assuring technical integrity of plant is not working |
| HK | Housekeeping | Tolerated untidiness; no resources for cleaning and waste removal |
| EC | Error-enforcing conditions | Time pressure, changed work patterns, heat, cold, noise — conditions that promote error or violation |
| PR | Procedures | Task information unclear, unavailable, incorrect or unusable |
| TR | Training | The system for giving people the awareness, knowledge or skill they need is deficient |
| CO | Communication | Information needed to operate safely is not transmitted |
| IG | Incompatible goals | Conflict between goals — typically safety and production — left unmanaged |
| OR | Organisation | Deficient structure, or deficiencies in the way the business is run |
| DF | Defences | Inadequate systems or equipment for containing the hazard or mitigating the consequences |

### Building the tree

1. **Facts first** (`investigation.md` §1, `investigation.md` §8; STEP,
   §4) — Tripod organises facts, never finds them.
2. **Main trio** — name the event, then the object, then the agent. Test
   it aloud: "[agent] acted on [object], resulting in [event]".
3. **Chain** — ask what earlier event produced this agent or put this
   object in its path. Each answer is another trio.
4. **Barriers** — list what should have contained each agent or protected
   each object, and classify. "Missing" needs a source: a standard,
   procedure, risk assessment or recognised industry practice. No source?
   Not a Tripod missing barrier, but test it against SFAIRP (model WHS Act
   s 18; VIC OHS Act 2004 s 20(2); NZ HSWA s 22; `frameworks.md` §2). If
   known and reasonably practicable, make it an underlying cause on the
   path of the barrier that did exist, usually the risk assessment. Only
   what nobody could reasonably have known is hindsight.
5. **Causation paths** — one per barrier, every node tied to evidence.
6. **Actions** — at least one per failed, missing or inadequate barrier and
   per underlying cause (`investigation.md` §7); review with line managers.

### Worked example

A facilities contractor's HVAC technician is scalded opening a strainer on
a hospital heating hot water loop. A serious burn needing immediate
treatment is notifiable (model WHS Act s 36(b)(iv)), as is the uncontrolled
release of pressurised hot water (s 37(d)). Notify (s 38) and preserve the
site (s 39) before any tree (VIC, NZ: §3).

```
TRIO 1  Agent: heating hot water, about 80 °C, under pump pressure
        Object: strainer housing being unbolted
        Event: hot water released into plant room (agent of Trio 2)
  B1 agent leg  Isolate, drain and prove                     FAILED
     Immediate:  cover unbolted without proving zero pressure
     Precond.:   upstream valve passing; no drain or gauge at strainer;
                 ward without heating, due back in service that shift
     Underlying: DE  design standard requires no isolation-proving points
                 PR  isolation procedure written for electrical energy
                 MM  defect process ignores passing isolation valves
  B2 agent leg  Permit to break containment                  MISSING
     Underlying: OR  site permit rule for hot water not applied here
TRIO 2  Agent: hot water release   Object: technician at the strainer
        Event: scald burns to forearm and chest (main event)
  B3 object leg Task PPE                                     INADEQUATE
     Underlying: PR  risk assessment covered mechanical hazards only;
                     the glasses and gloves specified were worn
TRIO 3  Agent: continuing release   Object: technicians in plant room
        Event: further, more extensive burns (did not occur)
  B4 agent leg  Second technician shut the header valve      EFFECTIVE
```

Four barriers, three causation paths, five underlying causes, and none
reads "failed to follow procedure". B3's path leads to the risk assessment
that missed thermal energy: fix that first, with proven isolation and a
drain and gauge point (B1), then re-specify PPE for the residual scald
risk (face shield, heat-resistant gauntlets, covered arms and torso), as
model WHS Regulations reg 36(5) and reg 44(3)(a)(i) still require. Cite the
source of each missing barrier: a regulator reads it as a control that
was known and available (s 18(c)–(d)).

### Tripod Beta, ICAM and bowtie

| | Tripod Beta | ICAM (`investigation.md` §1) | Bowtie (`investigation.md` §4, `investigation.md` §13) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Used | After the event | After the event | Before the event; reused afterwards to test barriers |
| Control that did not hold | Failed, missing or inadequate barrier | Absent or failed defence | Barrier and its degradation factors |
| Sharp-end action | Immediate cause | Individual / team action | Not shown |
| Local conditions | Precondition | Task / environmental condition | Degradation factor (nearest equivalent) |
| System cause | Underlying cause, coded to BRFs | Organisational factor, coded to OFTs | Not shown |
| Causal links | Drawn barrier by barrier | Pooled by level | Not drawn |
| What worked | Effective barrier node | Not a standard chart element | Barriers as designed, not as performed |

- A Tripod tree maps into an ICAM chart, but effective barriers and
  barrier-by-barrier links have no place there; carry them in the report
  narrative. The reverse needs re-analysis: ICAM does not record which
  organisational factor defeated which defence.
- A designed control on the tree but not on that critical risk's bowtie
  (`frameworks.md` §7): the bowtie is incomplete. An improvised recovery
  (B4) or a permit (B2) is a candidate barrier or degradation control, not
  proof of a gap. On the bowtie but never established: verification missed.
- Organisations running bowtie-based critical control verification will
  meet BSCAT, the bowtie-based barrier incident analysis method and Tripod
  Beta's nearest alternative; agree which method leads before the workshop.

### Practitioner accreditation (as at October 2026)

| Level | How it is earned | Valid |
|---|---|---|
| Bronze | Knowledge test at the end of an accredited course | 2 years |
| Silver | Case-study report and tree, submitted in three parts with assessor coaching | 2 years |
| Gold | Full report on a real or historical incident, verified by a Foundation-certified assessor (two attempts allowed) | 5 years |

Providers describe Bronze as team-member level and Gold as the level for
leading complex, high-potential analyses. Trainer and assessor are separate
accreditations certified by the Foundation. Confirm role expectations with
the Energy Institute, and each person's level and expiry, before staffing.

### Strengths and critiques

**Strengths**: the trio test and one-path-per-barrier rule expose evidence
gaps and unfinished paths; recording effective barriers opens the Safety II
question of what normally makes a barrier hold (`frameworks.md` §4).

| Trap | What goes wrong | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| Analyst-dependent depth | In SPE 88488 (2004; Verhoeve, Wolsak, Groeneweg and others, lead authors from Tripod Solutions, which developed the TRACK tool the paper promotes), one trained analyst analysed four incidents with Tripod Beta, then with TRACK. Over 40% of the "latent failures" on the Tripod trees were judged to be preconditions or active failures; the trees also used fewer BRFs, which the authors put down to analysts reaching first for familiar categories. Small and vendor-authored: read it as a warning, not an error rate | Test each underlying cause: who could change this, and through which system? Peer-review the tree (§7) |
| Linear barrier model | The incident is drawn as a chain of defeated barriers; interacting normal variability is not represented | On complex events, pair it with a work-as-done review (FRAM, `frameworks.md` §4) |
| Dated language | Older guides speak of "sub-standard acts" | Write immediate causes as observable actions in context (`frameworks.md` §5) |
| Overhead | Accredited lead, priced guide, trees that untrained readers cannot follow | Keep full trees for the events in "When to use it"; add a plain-language summary |
| Privilege | Trees and drafts are documents like any other | Keep them inside the privileged circle (`investigation.md` §14) |

## 6. HFACS — Human Factors Analysis and Classification System

HFACS is a classification taxonomy. Scott Shappell and Douglas Wiegmann
built it for US Navy and Marine Corps aviation by turning Reason's
active-failure and latent-condition model (`frameworks.md` §12) into fixed
categories, first published in 1997 as the "Taxonomy of Unsafe Operations"
(International Journal of Aviation Psychology 7(4)), then as FAA report
DOT/FAA/AM-00/7 (February 2000). The US military also uses it to prompt
investigators, but it gathers no evidence and builds no timeline, so this
skill uses it only to code established findings. ICAM finds one event's
factors (`investigation.md` §1) and codes the organisational ones as OFTs
(§2); HFACS makes acts, preconditions and supervision countable.

### The four tiers and 19 categories

| Tier | Categories (Wiegmann and Shappell, *A Human Error Approach to Aviation Accident Analysis*, 2003) | Nearest ICAM layer |
|---|---|---|
| Organisational influences | Resource management; organisational climate; organisational process | Organisational factors |
| Unsafe supervision | Inadequate supervision; planned inappropriate operations; failed to correct a known problem; supervisory violations | Organisational factors |
| Preconditions for unsafe acts | Environmental factors (physical environment, technological environment); condition of operators (adverse mental states, adverse physiological states, physical/mental limitations); personnel factors (crew resource management, personal readiness) | Task / environmental conditions |
| Unsafe acts | Errors (decision, skill-based, perceptual); violations (routine, exceptional) | Individual / team actions |

Each category carries example descriptors ("nanocodes") rewritten for each
sector. Two gaps against ICAM: no tier for absent or failed defences, and
nothing above the organisation. The contributing factors taxonomy
(`investigation.md` §3) lists fatigue and communication failure under
individual / team actions; code both as HFACS preconditions, because they
describe conditions acting on the person, not the act.

### Sector variants and Australian evidence

| Variant or study | What it is | Finding worth knowing |
|---|---|---|
| **HFACS-MI** (mining): developed by Clemson University (Patterson and Shappell) under commission from Queensland Mines and Energy, the then Department of Mines and Energy (the mines regulator is now RSHQ); figures from Tilbury and Patterson (2009) | Adds a fifth tier for outside factors (regulatory, other); "unsafe leadership" replaces "unsafe supervision"; "fitness for duty" and "communication and coordination" replace personal readiness and crew resource management; 21 categories | 508 Queensland incidents, mostly high potential: an unsafe act coded in 95%; skill-based errors 50% of unsafe-act codes, decision errors 41%, violations 5%, perceptual errors 4%; unsafe leadership in 36.6% of cases; an organisational influence in under 10%, which the authors said may reflect investigations that stopped short; no outside factor coded |
| Australian mining — Lenné, Salmon, Liu and Trotter (2012) | HFACS, two human factors specialists coding independently, 263 significant incidents from 2007–2008 | Skill-based errors in 64% of incidents, violations 57%, physical environment 56%, organisational process 65%; recommends targeting organisational climate, planned inappropriate operations and inadequate supervision |
| Rail — **HFACS-RR** (Reinach and Viale, 2006, US railroads); Baysari, McIntosh and Wilson (2008) | Baysari coded 40 Australian rail safety investigation reports with HFACS | Nearly half were equipment failures, mostly traced to maintenance or monitoring programs; attention slips were the most common unsafe act; nearly every event had an organisational influence |
| Maintenance — **HFACS-ME** (Maintenance Extension) | Aviation maintenance adaptation: the "operator" is the maintainer | Krulak (2004), 1,016 aircraft mishaps: inadequate supervision, attention/memory errors and judgement/decision errors most frequent |
| Defence — **HFACS-ADF**; **DoD HFACS 8.0** | ADF adaptation for classifying factors that contribute to incidents. DoD HFACS 8.0 (guide dated 25 May 2022; US Air Force mishaps from 1 April 2023) is written for all DoD occupational communities, not only aviation: the closest ready-made variant for ground and industrial work | Reliability findings below. 8.0 splits unsafe acts into errors (performance/skill-based; judgement and decision-making) and "known deviations", and requires at least one precondition for every unsafe act and an evidence-based supporting statement for every code |
| Civil aviation — ATSB research reports B2004/0321 (January 2007) and AR-2008-036 (December 2010) | HFACS applied retrospectively to 2,025 Australian accidents; ATSB added an "outside influences" group (maintenance, airport, regulatory, air traffic control, other persons) that sits outside the hierarchy and can link to any tier | 1,404 accidents (69%) had an aircrew unsafe act, 84% of those a skill-based error; each category counted once per accident; ATSB concluded that coding at the unsafe-acts level alone did not point to specific safety strategies |

Read the table as a warning as much as an endorsement:

- **The coder, the source reports and the sample decide the answer.** Two
  Australian mining studies of the same era: violations were 5% of
  unsafe-act codes in one and present in 57% of incidents in the other. No
  plausible conversion between the two measures closes that gap.
- **HFACS cannot code what the investigation never examined.** ATSB's
  2,025 accidents gave 1,333 skill-based errors against 16 organisational
  process, 1 organisational climate and 1 resource management. ATSB's
  explanation: not every accident was investigated, and without
  investigation higher-order factors are hard to identify.
- **The base taxonomy stops at the organisation's fence.** HFACS-MI added a
  fifth level; ATSB added a non-hierarchical "outside influences" group
  that attaches to any tier and found it associated with factors at every
  level. For regulator, client or multi-PCBU factors, use AcciMap
  (`investigation.md` §12).

### Reliability evidence

| Source | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cohen, Wiegmann and Shappell (2015) | Systematic review of 14 peer-reviewed studies, 2001–2014 | Most reported acceptable inter- and intra-rater reliability; higher with more training and with fewer departures from the original framework |
| Ergai et al. (2016) | 125 safety professionals coding 95 causal factors into the 19 categories | Reliable on Krippendorff's alpha; intra-rater slightly higher than inter-rater |
| Olsen and Shorrock (2010) | Three field studies of HFACS-ADF in an ADF air traffic control unit | Agreement low at both descriptor and category level; coders not confident they could apply it consistently; judged unreliable for incident analysis at unit level |
| O'Connor and Walker (2011) | 204 aviation safety officer students in 30 simulated mishap boards using DoD HFACS | Acceptable reliability for only 56.9% of nanocodes; agreement high on what did not contribute, low on what did |

The favourable papers are co-authored by the framework's developers; the
unfavourable ones independently tested modified military versions, closer
to a WHS team coding its own incidents. Reliability belongs to your coders,
training and source reports. Code at category level, not nanocode; re-test
agreement before editing categories; run the §2 calibration before any
HFACS trend reaches the ELT or board.

### Applying HFACS alongside ICAM

1. **Finish the ICAM first.** Code the contributing factors it produced
   (`investigation.md` §1 and `investigation.md` §3); never start from the
   category list and hunt for an error to fit it.
2. **Two coders, independently, one category per factor.** Record and
   reconcile differences; persistent disagreement means the category
   definition is not working on your site.
3. **No act without an evidenced precondition.** Adopt the DoD HFACS 8.0
   rule: every coded act carries at least one precondition, and every code
   a one-line evidence statement. If no precondition can be evidenced, send
   the investigation back (§7) rather than guess a code.
4. **Audit tier fill.** A top-tier report with an empty organisational tier
   and no recorded reason goes back (§7). If organisational influences
   appear in under about a quarter of coded events, report that as a
   finding about investigation depth, not a clean organisation.
5. **Patch the gaps.** Add fields for ICAM's absent or failed defences and
   for outside influences (any tier). Codes stay in the database; the
   report narrative explains how conditions shaped the action.
6. **Pick one taxonomy for trending.** Code organisational factors as OFTs
   (§2), fuller than HFACS's three categories, and use HFACS for acts and
   preconditions; never publish two competing trend sets.
7. **Trend only with volume, and state the counting unit.** Count each
   category once per event, as ATSB did, and say so on every chart. With 30
   coded events, a category in 15 (50%) has a 95% Wilson interval of about
   33–67%; report no shift unless the intervals separate (`analytics.md` §9).

### Worked example — Meridian Facilities Group

A HiPo in Meridian's Asset & Infrastructure Services (Isolation & Stored
Energy critical risk): a building management system (BMS) command restarted
a hospital chilled-water pump while a technician cleared its strainer;
the technician's hand was clear. The finished ICAM findings, coded:

| ICAM finding (neutral wording) | HFACS code | Evidence statement |
|---|---|---|
| Strainer cleared with the pump stopped at the local panel, not locked out at the motor control centre (MCC) | Unsafe act — deviation from procedure (routine) | Three of four technicians interviewed described the same method for this task |
| Local stop read as isolation; a BMS start could override it | Precondition — technological environment | Panel photographs; BMS event log |
| MCC in a hospital-controlled switchroom, after-hours access by call-out | Precondition — physical environment; outside-influences field (client) | Switchroom access log; call-out records |
| Work order allowed 30 minutes; contract abatement applies to chiller downtime | Organisational influence — organisational process | Contract schedule; work-order history |
| Isolation procedure lists no lockout point for the strainer | Organisational influence — organisational process | Procedure; plant register |
| One supervisor covers three hospital sites; the task was not observed in 12 months | Organisational influence — resource management | Roster; observation records |
| No interlock between the BMS start and the local stop | ICAM absent defence field (no HFACS tier) | Control drawings |

Organisational process appears twice and counts once for this event. Span
of control goes to resource management: coding it as inadequate
supervision would move the blame upstairs to a supervisor doing what three
sites allowed. The trend record holds no name; the report explains why the
local stop made sense after hours.

### New View critique

This skill's position, applying the New View lens in `frameworks.md` §4:
HFACS is a filing system for findings, and the label is not the learning.

- **Classification is not explanation.** Dekker ("Illusions of
  Explanation", International Journal of Aviation Psychology, 2003):
  calling an action a "decision error" re-describes it without showing why
  it made sense given the person's goals, knowledge and focus at the time.
- **The vocabulary carries a verdict.** "Unsafe act", "violation" and
  "supervisory violation" are judged with the outcome known (hindsight and
  outcome bias, `investigation.md` §6), and "adverse mental states" takes in
  "misplaced motivation"; the same action on shifts where nothing went
  wrong is never coded. Relabel on adoption. DoD HFACS 8.0 replaced
  "violations" with "known deviations", with a code for routine deviation
  it names normalisation of deviance; the Queensland regulator recast
  skill-based errors as "routine disruption errors". Go further: code
  "acts" and "deviations from procedure", and record why each made sense.
- **The model is a one-way chain.** ATSB describes HFACS as chain-of-events
  causation; its 2010 analysis found influences skipping tiers. HFACS codes
  where production pressure comes from (organisational process covers
  tempo, time pressure and quotas), not the trade-off the person made
  against it or the adaptations that normally make the work succeed.
- **The headline rarely changes.** Skill-based errors top almost every
  HFACS dataset: the base rate of skilled work, which invites "retrain and
  remind". Report preconditions and organisational tiers first, acts last.
- **Codes are discoverable.** The coded dataset is not privileged
  (`investigation.md` §14); a regulator's written notice (model WHS Act
  s 155; OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 9), an inspector (NZ HSWA 2015 s 168) or a
  coroner can require it (`investigation.md` §10). A "violation" code then
  reads as the organisation's finding about a named worker. Before coding
  starts, settle labels, de-identification and access with workers and
  HSRs (model WHS Act ss 47–49; OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 35; HSWA 2015
  ss 58–61; `frameworks.md` §10), and keep names out of the dataset.

### When HFACS is and is not the right tool

Neither the model WHS Act, the Victorian OHS Act 2004 nor HSWA 2015 (NZ)
prescribes an investigation methodology. HFACS arrives by contract or
corporate standard; adopt it only where it answers a question you have.

| Use HFACS when | Use something else when |
|---|---|
| A body of completed investigations needs a common coding frame for trending across sites, contractors or years | The job is to understand one event — ICAM (`investigation.md` §1) |
| Published sector profiles (Queensland mining, Australian rail, ATSB aviation) prompt questions about your own data — never as a benchmark score, because definitions, coders, counting units and source reports differ between studies | Regulator, client or multi-PCBU factors matter — AcciMap (`investigation.md` §12) |
| A client, parent company or sector body mandates it | The question is which barriers failed — Tripod Beta (§5) or bowtie (`investigation.md` §4) |
| Auditing investigation depth: a tier-fill count shows whether reports stop at the worker | The sequence across actors is unclear — STEP (§4) |
| Operator-centred work with a clear task and act: flying, driving, plant operation, maintenance | Psychosocial harm, occupational disease or long-latency exposure — there is no single "act" to code |
| Trained coders and an agreed, stable category list are in place | The findings will be used in a disciplinary or restorative process (§8) — the vocabulary prejudges conduct — or the aim is to learn from normal work (`frameworks.md` §4) |

Salmon, Cornelissen and Trotter (2012) compared AcciMap, HFACS and STAMP on
the Mangatepopo Gorge drownings (NZ) and recommended AcciMap plus a domain
taxonomy: a systems method to connect factors, a taxonomy to count them.

## 7. Investigation Quality Assurance

An investigation report is a work product. It commits capital, goes to the
board as assurance and may be read by a regulator or coroner, so it is
checked before anyone relies on it. The general WHS statutes set no
investigation quality standard, but two duties depend on the result:

| Duty | Model law (NSW, QLD, WA, SA, TAS, ACT, NT, Cth) | VIC | NZ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Review and revise a control measure that does not control the risk | Model WHS Regulations reg 38(2)(a); cited as s 38 in the WHS Regulation 2025 (NSW) | No general provision; the OHS Regulations 2017 set review triggers hazard by hazard | Health and Safety at Work (General Risk and Workplace Management) Regulations 2016 reg 8(2)(a), as at October 2026 (the GRWM Regulations take consequential amendments from 1 April 2027, `legislation.md` §3) |
| Officers ensure the PCBU has processes to receive, consider and respond to information on incidents, hazards and risks | Model WHS Act s 27(5)(d) | No positive officer duty; OHS Act 2004 s 144 (`legislation.md` §16) | HSWA 2015 s 44(4)(d), as at October 2026 (the HSWA Amendment Act 2026 clarifies officer due diligence from 1 April 2027, `legislation.md` §3) |

ISO 45001:2018 cl 10.2 (the edition in force as at October 2026; a revised
edition is expected in 2027) adds two tests: workers take part in
evaluating the event, and the effectiveness of every action is reviewed.

### What the research says goes wrong

| Source | What was studied | Finding | QA response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lundberg, Rollenhagen and Hollnagel (2009), *Safety Science* 47(10) | Eight accident investigation manuals | What-You-Look-For-Is-What-You-Find (WYLFIWYF): the accident model built into the manual decides which causes can be found | Name the method's blind spots in the terms of reference; use a second method on top-tier events (§4–§6, `investigation.md` §12) |
| Lundberg, Rollenhagen and Hollnagel (2010), *Accident Analysis & Prevention* 42(6) | Interviews with 22 investigators across sectors in Sweden | What you find is not always what you fix: analysis often stopped at "preventable causes", the level where a currently practical remedy existed | Accept findings before costing actions |
| Rollenhagen, Westerlund, Lundberg and Hollnagel (2010), *Safety Science* 48(7) | Questionnaire, 108 investigators in Swedish healthcare, transport, nuclear and rescue sectors | Investigation resourcing was scarce; risk analysis and audit data were under-used; investigators differed in how far "the human factor" was seen as a dominant cause; the remedial-action phase was brief and poorly supported | Reviewer asks what the risk register, audits and CCV data said before the event |
| Drupsteen and Hasle (2014), *Accident Analysis & Prevention* 72 | Focus groups in seven organisations | Learning broke down most often at action planning, the step between investigation and improvement | Review the actions as hard as the analysis |
| Hibbert et al. (2018), *International Journal for Quality in Health Care* 30(2) | 227 root cause analyses of sentinel events, Victorian public health system, 2010–2015 | Of 1,137 recommendations, 8% were strong, 44% medium and 48% weak; 72% of analyses made no strong recommendation | Measure action strength; expect a similar baseline |
| Transportation Safety Board of Canada review of the ATSB (1 December 2014) | ATSB methodology applied in three investigations, including the 2009 Norfolk Island ditching | All three peer reviews of the Norfolk Island draft raised issues with its facts, analysis and conclusions; many were never followed up, and no second-level review checked that peer-review feedback had been addressed; 14 recommendations | A review step without close-out of comments is not QA |

### QA rubric

Score each criterion 0 (absent), 1 (partial) or 2 (met). A 0 on criterion
2, 5, 7 or 8 returns the report whatever the total. Otherwise accept at
16–20 with no 0; accept with recorded conditions at 12–15, or at 16+ with
a 0; return below 12. Record the score and the reviewer's name on the
report. These thresholds are a practitioner convention: recalibrate them
after the first calibration round.

| # | Criterion | The reviewer must be able to see | Red flags |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Scope and terms of reference | Written before analysis: event, boundaries, method, team, sponsor, privilege status (`investigation.md` §14), due date; scope changes recorded | Terms written after the findings; design, procurement, client or contractor decisions scoped out |
| 2 | Evidence sufficiency and preservation | Evidence register covering all five PEEPO categories (`investigation.md` §8); each finding traced to two independent sources or labelled single-source; custody of physical and digital evidence recorded | A finding resting on one interview; CCTV, telematics or control-system data not secured before overwrite |
| 3 | Timeline integrity | Each entry has a time, a source and a confidence level; conflicts between sources shown, not smoothed; starts at planning and includes earlier runs of the same task; STEP tests re-run on top-tier events (§4) | Timeline starts at the start of shift; inferred events not marked |
| 4 | Work-as-done vs work-as-imagined | Normal work described from observation or from peers who were not involved; how often the "deviation" occurs on uneventful days | The procedure quoted as the description of the job; nobody outside the crew interviewed |
| 5 | Causal logic | Each contributing factor passes the ATSB test for existence (evidence it was present) and test for influence (without it, the event, the severity of its outcome or another contributing factor would probably not have occurred), per Walker and Bills (2008); mechanism stated. Factors that exist and fail the influence test but still matter to future safety are reported as "other safety factors" | "Lack of" lists; factors with no stated link listed as contributing; other safety factors deleted rather than reported; a single root cause |
| 6 | Hindsight and counterfactual judgement of people | Decisions explained from the information available at the time (`investigation.md` §6); counterfactuals used only as the criterion 5 test on factors, never as a verdict on what a person should have done | "Failed to", "should have", "if only" applied to a person's actions |
| 7 | Organisational factors reached | At least one evidenced organisational factor, coded by the team and confirmed by the reviewer (§2), or a recorded reason there is none | Deepest finding is training or supervision; "culture" asserted without evidence; empty upper tiers (§6) |
| 8 | Corrective-action strength and hierarchy | Every factor has an action or a recorded decision not to act; administrative and PPE actions carry the SFAIRP justification in `investigation.md` §7; each action names its effectiveness measure | Retrain, re-brief, toolbox talk, "remind workers" or a procedure rewrite as the only actions |
| 9 | Independence | Lead investigator (Serious tier and above) or peer reviewer (Moderate) outside the line management of the work; sponsor does not own the implicated system; conflicts declared; workers or HSRs took part | Site manager investigating own site with no independent member |
| 10 | Bias checks | First hypothesis written down and disconfirming evidence sought; the `investigation.md` §6 biases checked by a named challenger | Final narrative identical to the preliminary notification |

Cheap first pass: search the draft for "failed to", "should have", "did not
follow", "human error", "complacen" and "lack of awareness". Each hit is a
criterion 5 or 6 query.

### Peer review, challenge panel and sign-off

Tier on the higher of the actual outcome and the credible potential
outcome. Where the active company profile (`company.md` §5) sets severity
classes, investigation leads, reviewers or approvers, it overrides this
table; the table is the default only where the profile is silent.

| Tier | Review before sign-off | Signs off |
|---|---|---|
| Low — first aid or minor, no HiPo potential | Supervisor review; WHS samples 10% each quarter against criteria 1–8 and 10 (criterion 9 applies from Moderate up) | Line manager |
| Moderate — medical treatment or lost time, not notifiable | Desk peer review by a trained investigator from outside the team | Site or contract manager; WHS endorses method |
| Serious — notifiable incident (`legislation.md` §5) or HiPo | Peer review, blind second coding of organisational factors (§2), challenge panel | Business unit executive; head of WHS endorses method and rubric score |
| Fatality, permanent incapacity or industrial manslaughter exposure | As above plus a reviewer from outside the business, and legal review (§3, `investigation.md` §14) | CEO, or the board WHS committee chair where officers' decisions are within scope; conflicts declared (criterion 9); summary and rubric result to the board committee, through counsel where privilege is claimed |

Challenge panel (investigation review board) and review arrangements:

| Element | Design |
|---|---|
| Composition (4–6) | Chair: a senior line executive from outside the business unit involved. Members: a worker or supervisor who does the task and was not involved; a trained investigator from outside the team; an HSR; the manager who holds the budget for the likely actions. WHS advises on method and does not chair |
| Two gates | Gate 1 after evidence and timeline, before the analysis is locked (criteria 1–4). Gate 2 on the draft (criteria 5–10): findings are accepted first and actions tested second, so investigators stop trimming findings to what they expect to be funded |
| Challenge log | Every comment gets a recorded disposition, accepted or rejected with reasons, and the chair signs the log. The Norfolk Island failure was unresolved review comments, not an absence of review |
| Factual accuracy check | People directly involved see the factual sections before sign-off, as the ATSB does with draft reports under the Transport Safety Investigation Act 2003 (Cth) s 26. Where prosecution or an inquest is foreseeable, only on legal advice (`investigation.md` §14) |
| Documents | Drafts, reviewer comments and the challenge log of an operational investigation can be required by the regulator (model WHS Act s 155 and s 171, with the NZ HSWA equivalents, in `legislation.md` §10; OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 9 and s 100). Write them to be read |
| Privileged investigations | Under the parallel model (`investigation.md` §14) the operational investigation takes the full QA process. The legal-led investigation never goes to the panel: counsel engages its peer reviewers under the engagement letter, and the board receives its rubric result through counsel |
| Multi-PCBU events | Before analysis the PCBUs agree in writing who leads, who applies the rubric, who signs off and how evidence is shared (model WHS Act s 46; HSWA 2015 s 34; the OHS Act 2004 (Vic) has no general equivalent, only the labour-hire duty in s 35A). A client scores a contractor-led report against this rubric and returns it if it fails rather than rewriting it; consequences run through `whs-procurement.md` §5 |

### Verifying corrective actions

`investigation.md` §7 sets the three verification questions. QA adds:

- **Three states.** Implemented (owner's evidence), verified in place
  (field check by someone other than the owner), effective (evidence the
  work changed). Close only at effective.
- **Strength.** RCA2 (National Patient Safety Foundation, 2015) grades
  actions stronger, intermediate or weaker and asks for at least one
  stronger or intermediate action per cause; training and policy changes
  used alone are weaker. Report the strength mix for top-tier reports.
- **Failure.** A failed check reopens the original action and is itself a
  review trigger (model WHS Regulations reg 38(2)(a); GRWM Regulations
  (NZ) reg 8(2)(a)). Raising a fresh action only restarts the clock.
- **Feedback before closure.** Findings and actions go back to the injured
  worker (or the family, §3 and §8), the crew and the HSRs, and their
  response is recorded (ISO 45001 cl 10.2; model WHS Act s 47–49; OHS Act
  2004 (Vic) s 35; HSWA 2015 s 58–60). Under privilege, what is shared
  follows legal advice (`investigation.md` §14).

| Action type | First check | Second check | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elimination, substitution, isolation, engineering | At commissioning | 3 months | Function test; observation of the task |
| Administrative (procedure, permit, training, supervision) | 3 months | 12 months | Observation of work-as-done and CCV data (`analytics.md` §3), not a document check |
| Organisational (resourcing, roster, contract, procurement) | 6 months | 12 months | Budget, roster and contract records; worker feedback |

### Investigator competency and calibration

The general WHS statutes prescribe no investigator qualification or licence.
The PCBU defines competence and resources it.

| Role | Tier | Minimum |
|---|---|---|
| First-line reviewer | Low | Scene preservation, 5-Why (`investigation.md` §2), basic interviewing |
| Lead investigator | Moderate, serious | Training in the mandated method (`investigation.md` §11); BSBWHS515 *Lead initial response to and investigate WHS incidents* (core unit of the BSB51319 Diploma of Work Health and Safety, as at October 2026) or equivalent; interviewing (`investigation.md` §9); two investigations as a team member before leading |
| Senior investigator, peer reviewer | Top tier, panel | A second method (§4–§6, `investigation.md` §12); human factors grounding; five or more investigations led. Benchmark: from 2019 the ATSB moved its investigator training to a Graduate Certificate in Transport Safety Investigation delivered by RMIT |

- **Currency.** Lead or peer-review two investigations a year, or co-lead
  the next one. Proprietary methods carry their own accreditation (§5).
- **Calibration.** Once a year every lead investigator scores the same two
  closed reports against the rubric, alone. Compare, and write a ruling for
  each criterion where scores split 0 and 2. Coding agreement is measured
  separately (§2). `case-studies-everyday.md` §9 has a calibration case set.

### Process metrics and their perverse incentives

| Metric | Perverse incentive | Report instead, or alongside |
|---|---|---|
| Days to close; % closed on time | Premature closure (`investigation.md` §6); scope trimmed to fit the date | Due date set per tier in the terms of reference; sponsor-approved extensions are not failures; timeliness shown with rubric score |
| Actions closed on time | Easy actions chosen; closure at "implemented" | % of actions verified effective; overdue verification checks |
| Number of actions raised | Action inflation and clutter | Strength mix: % of actions above administrative level |
| % of events coded to human error or non-compliance | The form finds what it lists (WYLFIWYF) | Organisational factor distribution, reported only when coder agreement meets the §2 threshold |
| Investigations completed per reported event | Shallow investigation of everything | Tiering by potential; learning reviews for the rest |
| Rubric score | Reports written to the rubric | Challenge log; repeat-event rate |

`analytics.md` §5 and `frameworks.md` §9 list investigation and action
timeliness as leading indicators. Report them only beside the rubric score
and the % of actions verified effective: on-time completion measures
throughput, not investigation quality. The outcome measure is the
**repeat-event rate**: the share of serious and HiPo events with a
contributing factor already identified in an investigation closed in the
previous three years.

### Learning reviews for non-notifiable events

The Learning Review was developed by Ivan Pupulidy for the US Forest
Service, which adopted it in 2013–2014 in place of its Serious Accident
Investigation Guide and applied it to fatalities and other serious
accidents. It makes no cause statement: it sets out the conditions that
shaped actions and decisions and produces learning products for different
audiences. Here it is limited to non-notifiable, non-HiPo events because
an AU/NZ regulator, coroner or court will look for a causal report on the
rest, not because the method cannot handle them. A Learning Team
(`frameworks.md` §5) is a facilitated session; a learning review is a
documented alternative to an investigation report.

| Use a formal investigation (ICAM or equivalent) | Use a learning review |
|---|---|
| Notifiable incident, including a dangerous incident with no injury (model WHS Act s 37; OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 37; `legislation.md` §5) | Non-notifiable event with no HiPo potential |
| HiPo or the top severity classes (`company.md` §5) | Repeat low-severity events that earlier investigations did not shift |
| Prosecution, claim or inquest foreseeable (`investigation.md` §14) | Near miss with no HiPo potential and a good recovery: study why it went right |
| Client, contract or regulator requires a causal report | A cluster of weak signals with no single event |
| Suspected deliberate or criminal conduct (separate HR or police process) | HSR or crew asks for one (non-notifiable, non-HiPo events only) |

Rubric criteria 1–4, 6, 9 and 10 apply unchanged. Criterion 5 becomes
"conditions are evidenced and linked to decisions"; criterion 7 becomes "at
least one condition traced to an organisational decision, resource or
design choice, or a recorded reason there is none". Criterion 8 applies in
full: a learning review that ends in a toolbox talk fails. Escalate to a
formal investigation as soon as the review shows HiPo potential or a
notifiable event. Method choice never alters notification or site
preservation (model WHS Act s 38, s 39; OHS Act 2004 (Vic) s 38, s 39;
HSWA 2015 s 56, s 55). Restorative work with the people affected is at §8.

## 8. Restorative Practice After Harm

An investigation explains the event. It does not repair the people the event
damaged, and a culpability flowchart repairs nobody. Restorative just culture
(Sidney Dekker, *Just Culture*, 3rd ed. 2016; `frameworks.md` §12) is the
structured response. It runs beside the operational and legal-led
investigations (`investigation.md` §14) and the crisis response
(`strategy-function.md` §5). It replaces none of them.

### Two models of accountability

| | Retributive just culture | Restorative just culture |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Reason's culpability decision tree (1997), including the substitution test; Marx's error / at-risk / reckless algorithm | Dekker, drawing on restorative justice practice |
| Questions | Which rule was broken? How culpable is the person? What consequence fits? | Who is hurt? What do they need? Whose obligation is it to meet that need? |
| Accountability | Backward-looking: the person answers for the act and carries the sanction | Forward-looking: those involved give the account, repair what can be repaired and change the conditions |
| Who decides | Management and HR, applying the chart once the outcome is known | The people affected, with a facilitator; obligations are agreed, not imposed |
| How it fails | Hindsight and outcome bias pick the category; the line is drawn by those with most to protect; reporting dries up | Treated as amnesty; harmed people pressed to forgive; obligations agreed and never delivered |

Dekker asks three questions; the fourth in `strategy-function.md` §5 (how can
the future be different?) is the skill's own. Keep the substitution test as an
investigation question, not a sanction sorter: would a peer with the same
training, information and pressures plausibly have done the same? If yes, the
finding belongs to the system and discipline changes nothing
(`investigation.md` §6).

### Evidence base: what it shows and what it does not

- **Mersey Care NHS Foundation Trust (UK)** replaced a suspend-and-investigate
  default with a restorative "just and learning culture" from 2016. Kaur,
  de Boer, Oates, Rafferty and Dekker (2019, *MATEC Web of Conferences* 273)
  estimated the economic benefit at about £2.5 million, with fewer suspensions
  and dismissals, more adverse-event reporting and a slower rise in sickness
  absence. The trust later reported disciplinary investigations down about
  85 per cent and suspensions about 95 per cent on 2014 while its workforce
  more than doubled.
- **Limits.** One organisation, before-and-after, no comparison group,
  co-authored by trust executives and Dekker; the paper says the gains
  "coincided with" the change. It is evidence about reporting, suspension cost
  and staff experience, not injury rates. Brief an ELT in those terms.
- **Second victims.** Albert Wu (*BMJ*, 2000) named the clinician involved in
  a patient-harm event the "second victim"; in WHS, the operator, the spotter,
  the supervisor who signed the permit. Keep the term in-house; families hear
  it as a claim to equal standing.

### Map who is hurt before anyone meets

| Who | What they typically need | Whose obligation |
|---|---|---|
| Injured worker; family of a worker who died | The facts, in plain language, before the media has them; practical and financial support without forms; acknowledgement; a say in what changes | Officers and senior management, through the family liaison (§3; `strategy-function.md` §5) |
| People closest to the event | Not to be stood down by reflex; to give their account without it becoming a charge sheet; clinical support; a part in the fix | Line manager and HR; the investigation lead for how the account is taken (`investigation.md` §9) |
| Witnesses, first responders, the crew | Information, time off the task, screening and follow-up rather than a single debrief (`specialist-topics.md` §2) | Site management and the WHS function |
| Other PCBUs' workers, the client, the community | The same facts at the same time; agreed shared actions | The PCBUs together (model WHS Act s 46; HSWA s 34, narrowed for small PCBUs from 1 April 2027: `legislation.md` §3) |

### Protocol after a serious incident

**Trigger and owner.** A death, a notifiable serious injury or illness, or a
HiPo that physically or psychologically harmed identifiable people. The most
senior officer on the crisis team decides to start or pause, on legal and WHS
advice, and records why.

1. **First 72 hours: map and stabilise.** Build the table above for this
   event. Confirm the family liaison was appointed within the first 24 hours
   (§3; `strategy-function.md` §5) and brief them on the map. Default for
   the people closest to the event is no suspension; stand a person down
   only for a stated safety or welfare reason, on full pay, with a review
   date.
2. **Sequence and clear.** No facilitated contact between participants until
   regulator and police interviews are done or expressly cleared, and legal
   has settled the apology wording against the tables below. Get the insurer's
   written consent to any statement or commitment that could be read as an
   admission: liability policies commonly make that a claims condition, and a
   breach can prejudice cover (`specialist-topics.md` §5).
3. **Prepare each party separately.** An independent facilitator (not the line
   manager, investigator, HR or the lawyer) with formal restorative justice
   training and supervised serious-harm casework meets each party alone.
   Participation is voluntary and revocable. First tell each person in writing
   that nothing said is protected from a regulator, police or coroner (No
   privilege, below) and that, unlike compelled answers to an inspector (model
   WHS Act s 172), their own admissions carry no use immunity. Offer
   independent legal advice to anyone who may be a subject of the
   investigation, including a worker with duties under model WHS Act s 28 or
   HSWA s 45. Each may bring a support person, HSR or union representative.
4. **Facilitated conversation.** Four parts: what happened (facts established
   to date, no speculation on cause); how each person has been affected; what
   each needs; who will do what. A senior officer with authority to commit
   attends and mostly listens.
5. **Written commitments.** Each has a named owner and a date, sits in the
   corrective action system (`investigation.md` §7) and is reported back to
   the people it was made to.
6. **Follow through.** Review at 3, 6 and 12 months and at each legal
   milestone (charge decision, inquest, sentence). An undelivered commitment
   does more damage than none. Tell the family early about their formal
   voice, which the process must never crowd out: impact statements at
   sentence (for example Crimes (Sentencing Procedure) Act 1999 (NSW) Pt 3
   Div 2; Victims' Rights Act 2002 (NZ) ss 17AA–22B, s 4 treating a
   deceased person's immediate family as victims) and standing at inquest
   (§3).

Standard commitments: findings in person before release; worker, family and
crew test corrective actions before sign-off; support never conditional on a
release or deed; a named contact through inquest and prosecution; nothing said
in the process used for discipline by the organisation, with participants told
a regulator, police or coroner can still compel it.

### Interaction with legal process

**Apology legislation** protects an apology from use as an admission in civil
proceedings. The protection differs by jurisdiction (as at October 2026):

| Jurisdiction | Provision | Apology admitting fault protected? |
|---|---|---|
| NSW | Civil Liability Act 2002 ss 67–69 | Yes |
| ACT | Civil Law (Wrongs) Act 2002 ss 12–14 | Yes |
| QLD | Civil Liability Act 2003 ss 68–72 (regret), ss 72A–72D (apology) | Yes, under the apology provisions |
| SA | Civil Liability Act 1936 s 75 | No on its face: s 75 only bars inferring an admission from an expression of regret, so treat SA as regret only |
| VIC | Wrongs Act 1958 ss 14I–14J | No; regret only |
| WA | Civil Liability Act 2002 ss 5AF–5AH | No; regret only |
| TAS | Civil Liability Act 2002 ss 6A–7 | No; regret only |
| NT | Personal Injuries (Liabilities and Damages) Act 2003 ss 11–13 | No; regret only |
| NZ | Accident Compensation Act 2001 s 317 bars most injury damages claims, so civil exposure is narrow | Not the live issue; apology and amends go to sentence (Sentencing Act 2002 s 10) |

| Limit that catches organisations out | What it means |
|---|---|
| Civil only | No provision above applies in a criminal court. An apology admitting fault is admissible in a WHS prosecution as an admission, including one made by a manager acting within authority (uniform Evidence Acts ss 81 and 87; common law to similar effect) |
| Your own worker may be outside the shield | The NSW provisions do not apply to a worker's damages claim against the employer under the Workers Compensation Act 1987 (NSW) Pt 5 Div 3, nor to dust disease claims. QLD's Civil Liability Act s 5 excludes compensable work injuries; the Workers' Compensation and Rehabilitation Act 2003 (Qld) ch 5 pt 14 fills the gap for damages claims notified on or after 30 October 2019. Read your jurisdiction's exclusions first |
| No privilege | Nothing said in a restorative meeting is privileged, and "without prejudice" binds neither a regulator nor a criminal court. Facilitator notes and the commitments register can be required under a model WHS Act s 155 notice and participants questioned under s 171 (`investigation.md` §10). Work from the operational investigation only; quoting the legal-led report to a family risks waiver (`investigation.md` §14) |
| Remorse needs responsibility | In sentencing, remorse mitigates only with evidence the offender accepted responsibility and acknowledged or repaired the harm (for example Crimes (Sentencing Procedure) Act 1999 (NSW) s 21A(3)(i)) |
| Working rule | Say what is known and cleared with legal, that you are sorry it happened and what you will do. Legal settles any statement about fault or cause. Silence on legal advice is itself a harm, so name who will speak to the family and when |

**Regulators, courts and schemes (as at October 2026):**

| Mechanism | Where | Restorative content |
|---|---|---|
| WHS undertaking | Model WHS Act s 216 (`legislation.md` §6) | Not an admission of guilt. SafeWork NSW's guidelines require a statement of regret, seek the views of the injured worker or family, and weigh the support given to them |
| Court-ordered undertaking; WHS project order | Model WHS Act ss 239, 238 (`legislation.md` §6); NZ, HSWA ss 156, 155 | Sentencing options that can carry repair: propose conditions or projects shaped with the people harmed rather than a fine alone |
| Enforceable undertaking; court-ordered health and safety undertaking | VIC, OHS Act 2004 s 16 (WorkSafe policy, 18 May 2026); s 137 (WorkSafe policy, updated June 2026) | s 137: proceeding adjourned for up to 2 years, offender discharged on compliance; WorkSafe weighs the views of victims in deciding whether to seek one |
| Enforceable undertaking | NZ, HSWA s 123 | WorkSafe policy (18 December 2025): acknowledgement of harm and amends to victims, tested against likely court reparation; victims' views go to the decision panel; unavailable for s 47 conduct |
| Court-referred restorative justice | NZ, Sentencing Act 2002 s 24A | After a guilty plea in the District Court, where there is a victim, no restorative justice process has already occurred and the Registrar confirms one can be accessed, the court must adjourn to test whether a conference is appropriate, taking account of the victims' wishes. Both sides must agree. The facilitator's report goes to the judge; outcomes and amends count at sentence (ss 8(j), 10; HSWA s 151(2)(a)). Coordinate any employer-led meeting with the prosecutor and provider so it does not pre-empt this process |
| Reparation | NZ, Sentencing Act 2002 s 32 | Step one of four in *Stumpmaster v WorkSafe New Zealand* [2018] NZHC 2020: emotional harm plus consequential loss, the latter on the ACC "statutory shortfall" basis (*Oceana Gold (New Zealand) Ltd v WorkSafe New Zealand* [2019] NZHC 365). If the offender can pay reparation or a fine but not both, reparation wins (s 14(2)) |
| Remorse at sentence | NZ, Sentencing Act 2002 ss 9L–9Q (from 29 June 2025) | A remorse reduction is recorded, and for offending committed after that sentencing none is available unless the sentence would be manifestly unjust; the 40 per cent cap on personal mitigating reductions applies to imprisonment, not to a PCBU's fine |
| General restorative justice scheme | ACT, Crimes (Restorative Justice) Act 2004 ss 12, 14–15, 22 (table 22), 27 | Covers offences against ACT laws, including the ACT WHS Act, by child, young and adult offenders, all individuals: it can reach an officer or worker, not on its terms a corporate PCBU. Courts can refer before plea or after a guilty plea or finding |
| Proposed conferencing | VIC | Sentencing Advisory Council report (26 February 2025) recommended enabling people affected by OHS offences to join a victim-centred restorative justice conference, with safeguards such as informed consent, and broadening who can make an impact statement. No government response as at July 2026; a Sentencing Act review issues paper is due early 2027 |

No Australian WHS or OHS Act provides for restorative justice conferencing.
Ask the prosecutor before sentence whether a general scheme can take the
matter; otherwise practice is voluntary and employer-led.

### When it is not appropriate, and cautions

| Situation | Do instead |
|---|---|
| Evidence of reckless or intentional conduct, violence or sexual harassment | Disciplinary, regulator or police pathway first; restorative contact only later and only if the harmed person asks |
| The harmed person declines or is ambivalent | Meet the needs anyway; leave the offer open; never press |
| The organisation is instructed to contest everything and can acknowledge nothing | Give support and information through the liaison; defer the meeting, because a meeting where the organisation says nothing is a second injury |
| Participants have not yet been interviewed by the regulator or police | Wait; joint sessions contaminate accounts (`investigation.md` §9) |
| Steep power imbalance (labour hire, visa-dependent or small-contractor worker) | Independent facilitator chosen with the worker; support person; neutral venue |
| The motive is a cheaper settlement or a signed release | Stop; tying support to a release converts repair into leverage |
| The worker or family is Māori, Pasifika or Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander | A caution, not a bar: ask first who should be in the room and whose process applies (`diversity-inclusion.md` §1) |

### Worked example — Meridian Facilities Group

Fictional (`company.md`), and a separate event from the §4 example. An MFG
Facilities Management technician in NSW loses two fingers when a rooftop
air handling unit fan restarts during a belt change; his apprentice had
restored power at the switchboard, believing the job was finished. The
amputation is notifiable (model WHS Act s 36(b)); the event is Class 4, so
the Crisis Management Team meets.

| Who | What they need | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Technician and his partner | Facts before site rumour reaches them; income top-up and travel costs paid without forms; a say in the isolation redesign | BU General Manager, through the named liaison |
| Apprentice | Not stood down; clinical support; account taken once, by the lead investigator; a part in testing the fix | Contract manager and HR; lead investigator |
| Rooftop crew; client facility team | The same facts at the same time; like-for-like isolation points checked | BU WHS Manager with the client |

Legal advises that an apology to the technician falls outside the NSW shield
(his damages claim would sit under Workers Compensation Act 1987 Pt 5 Div 3),
so it covers established facts and regret, with the insurer's written
consent. The apprentice gets the written warning and independent advice
first. Commitments: findings in person before release (BU General Manager);
the technician and apprentice test the redesigned isolation before sign-off
(BU WHS Manager, six weeks); a group-wide check of rooftop plant isolation
(Group GM WHS, 90 days); each reviewed with the technician at 3, 6 and 12
months. The substitution test points to the isolation design, so the Fair
and Just Culture Procedure (MFG-WHS-PR-014) records no discipline.
