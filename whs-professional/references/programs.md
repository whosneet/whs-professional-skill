## 1. Program Design Fundamentals

### Start with the Problem, Not the Solution
A program without a defined problem is an activity budget.
Before designing any component, answer:
1. What is the specific problem this program is solving?
2. What evidence do we have that this is the right problem?
3. Who is most affected and in what context?
4. What does success look like — and how will we know?

Typical evidence sources: HiPo distribution, injury classification trends, worker
feedback, facilitator debrief data, near miss reports, CRO gap analysis.

### Theory of Change
Define the causal logic: if we do X, workers will Y, which will lead to Z.
Example: "If frontline workers receive facilitated discussion on electrical isolation
controls [X], they will more consistently identify and challenge inadequate isolation
conditions before energised work [Y], reducing electrical HiPo frequency [Z]."

Without a clear theory of change, it's impossible to know whether the program worked
or what to adjust.

### Proportionality
Match program intensity to risk profile:
- High critical risk concentration → intensive, multi-format, multi-week program
- Specific BU issue → targeted intervention, not division-wide rollout
- Compliance gap → structured training with verification, not engagement campaign

Don't run a 13,000-worker 8-week program to solve a problem affecting 200 workers
in one contract cluster.

### Design for Work-as-Done
Programs designed against Work-as-Imagined (procedures) have limited impact.
Design for the actual work environment:
- What pressures are workers facing at the time of the critical task?
- What competing goals are in play?
- What does the control failure pathway actually look like in practice?
This requires engagement with frontline workers and supervisors in the design phase,
not just in delivery.

## 2. Sustained Campaign Architecture

A high-coverage, multi-format, sustained campaign model is
designed to lift engagement and reinforce critical risk behaviours across a large,
dispersed workforce.

### Program Parameters

| Element | Indicative scale | Guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Target population | Tens of thousands of workers possible | Scale program to workforce under direct organisational control |
| Duration | 8 weeks | Minimum 6 weeks for behaviour change; 8–12 weeks for maximum reach |
| Cadence | Weekly themed toolbox session | Weekly is optimal — monthly loses momentum, daily is unsustainable |
| Critical risk topics | 15 topics, one per week + buffer weeks | Cover highest-HiPo critical risks in first 4 weeks |
| Facilitators | 200+ toolbox facilitators | Target 1 per 50–60 workers for manageable load |
| Physical collateral | Jigsaw puzzle poster + stickers | Completion mechanic drives return engagement |

### Campaign Architecture Components

**1. Theme and Narrative**
Every program needs a unifying narrative that workers can identify with.
- Avoid generic: "Safety is Everyone's Responsibility"
- Use context-specific, aspirational, or identity-based themes
- Seasonal example: summer hazard management + return-to-work-after-break framing
- Themed example: F1-themed critical risk verification — competitive, visual, aspirational (see your organisation's campaign brand in `company.md`)

**2. Weekly Toolbox Structure**
Each weekly toolbox session should follow a consistent format to reduce facilitator
cognitive load and enable non-specialist delivery:

```
OPENING (2 min)
  - Reference previous week's discussion — continuity builds commitment
  - Brief scene-setter for this week's topic

CORE DISCUSSION (8–10 min)
  - One scenario or case study (real incident, near miss, or work situation)
  - 2–3 discussion questions designed to surface WAD, not test procedure knowledge
  - Facilitator guidance notes: what to listen for, common misconceptions to address

ACTIONS (2–3 min)
  - What will we do differently as a result of this discussion?
  - What will we check or report before next week?
  - Record commitments where practicable

CLOSE (1 min)
  - Collateral distribution (sticker, card, etc.)
  - Tease next week's topic
```

**3. Physical Collateral**
Tactile, collectible collateral drives return engagement:
- Jigsaw puzzle: each weekly sticker builds toward a complete image
- Completion creates a visible metric (how many pieces does your team have?)
- Poster displayed in crib/lunchroom creates ongoing visual reinforcement
- Worker-generated additions (names, team contributions) increase ownership

**4. Video Content**
Frontline-made video outperforms produced safety videos on:
- Credibility ("that's a real place / real person / real task")
- Relevance ("that's actually how we do this job")
- Attention ("I know that person")

Target: 1–2 short (60–90 second) worker-generated videos per campaign.
Content: near miss story, control demonstration, hazard recognition walkthrough.
Production: phone camera quality is acceptable; authentic > polished.

**5. Incentive Structure**
- Team-based incentives outperform individual incentives for safety programs
- Avoid incentives tied purely to injury statistics — distorts reporting
- Appropriate: recognition for hazard reports, near miss submissions, toolbox quality,
  worker-generated content, full campaign completion
- Sustained campaign: contract manager award + team recognition through completion collateral

## 3. Gamification Principles

### What Gamification Is (and Isn't)
Gamification is the application of game design elements to non-game contexts to
increase engagement and motivation. It is NOT:
- Making safety "fun" by trivialising it
- Adding points to a compliance exercise
- Replacing substantive engagement with token rewards

Done well, gamification increases intrinsic motivation — workers engage because
the activity is satisfying, not just to earn a reward.

### Core Gamification Elements for WHS Programs

| Element | Description | WHS Application |
|---|---|---|
| **Progress mechanics** | Visible progress toward a goal | Jigsaw puzzle completion; CRO dashboard milestones |
| **Achievement / recognition** | Acknowledgment of contribution | Worker-generated content credits; facilitator leaderboard |
| **Social proof** | Visibility of others' engagement | Team completion board; peer-nominated recognition |
| **Narrative / theme** | Storyline that gives purpose to activities | Themed verification programs; seasonal campaign journey |
| **Feedback loops** | Regular, visible data on how you're doing | Weekly CRO completion rate; hazard report count |
| **Autonomy** | Choice in how to engage | Workers can contribute alerts, toolboxes, or videos |
| **Mastery** | Increasing challenge / skill development | Facilitator development tiers; escalating topic complexity |

### Pitfalls to Avoid
- **Completion gaming**: if the metric is "toolbox completed", facilitators will mark
  attendance even if the session was low quality. Build quality indicators.
- **Extrinsic crowding out intrinsic**: large cash prizes can reduce intrinsic motivation
  if workers start engaging only for the reward, not the purpose
- **Exclusion**: gamification benefits workers with literacy, language access, and
  digital access. Design for the full workforce, not just office-based staff
- **One-size gamification**: what motivates facilities maintenance workers ≠ what
  motivates rail workers ≠ what motivates subcontractors

### Themed Critical-Risk Verification Model (worked example)
A themed critical risk verification dashboard structure (illustrative — replace theme with one that resonates with your workforce):
- Each critical risk = a racing circuit section
- CCV completion = track progress
- Degraded controls = pit stop required
- BU dashboard = team constructor standings
- Visual: F1 car graphic, team colours, lap time metaphors

Works because: competitive framing appeals to operations managers; visual progress
is motivating; the theme is aspirational and widely understood.

## 4. Facilitator Framework

### Why Facilitator Quality Is the Critical Variable
In a large-scale toolbox program, the facilitator is the point of contact between
the program and the worker. High facilitator quality can transform a mediocre session
into a genuinely useful discussion. Low facilitator quality can destroy program credibility.

### Facilitator Selection Criteria
- Credibility with workers on the relevant work type (ideally from the same discipline)
- Willingness to facilitate discussion rather than lecture
- Ability to manage group dynamics (dominant voices, reluctant participants)
- Basic literacy and language fluency appropriate to the team

Avoid: mandatory assignment of facilitators who don't want to do it. Reluctant
facilitators damage engagement. Build a volunteer-and-selection model where possible.

### Facilitator Training Program (8-session programs)

**Day 1 — Orientation and Purpose (2 hours)**
- Program overview: why this program, what success looks like
- Role of the facilitator: guide, not instructor
- How the session materials work: structure, timing, flexibility
- Q&A and concerns

**Day 2 — Facilitation Skills (3 hours)**
- Discussion vs lecture: what's the difference and why it matters
- Questioning techniques: open questions, probing, pausing
- Managing dominant voices and drawing out quiet participants
- Handling incorrect answers or unsafe statements
- Practice session: facilitators deliver a 5-minute sample toolbox, peer feedback

**Day 3 — Content Deep-Dive (2 hours per critical risk cluster)**
- Detailed context on the critical risk topics in the program
- Common WAD patterns for each topic — what actually happens in the field
- Misconceptions to correct; key learning points to land

**Ongoing Support**
- Facilitator resource pack: all session guides + FAQ + escalation contact
- Weekly SMS or email: short tip or reminder for the upcoming week's topic
- Debrief channel: mechanism for facilitators to feed back on session quality
  and worker responses (your incident system, Teams channel, or paper debrief card)

### Facilitator Accountability
Track facilitator-level completion rates. Flag outliers — very high completion with
no debrief feedback may indicate checkbox completion rather than genuine delivery.
Recognise top facilitators publicly: feature in program communications, team award.

### Attendance Tracking System
For large-scale programs, automated tracking reduces administrative burden:
- Indicative tooling: a custom attendance system with facilitator dashboard supports large dispersed workforces
- Tracks: sessions delivered, worker attendance, completion progress
- Outputs: contract-level completion heat map, overall program reach
- Integration: ideally linked to HRIS or your WHS management system platform for accurate headcount denominator

## 5. Frontline Content Strategy

### Why Frontline-Generated Content Outperforms Top-Down Content
Workers trust peers over corporate communications. Content that shows their actual
workplace, their actual tools, and their actual colleagues has:
- Higher initial attention and engagement
- Greater perceived relevance
- Longer retention post-program
- Secondary effect: content creators become program advocates

### Content Types and Guidance

**Worker-Generated Safety Alerts**
- Format: your organisation's safety alert template (see `company.md`)
- Process: worker or supervisor submits incident story; WHS team edits for compliance,
  not style — preserve voice and authenticity
- Distribution: same channel as standard safety alerts
- Recognition: name the worker/team as contributor (with consent)

**Worker-Generated Toolbox Sessions**
- Workers with relevant expertise co-facilitate or present a section of a toolbox
- Works particularly well for critical risk topics where the worker has direct experience
- Example: electrician walks through isolation failure near-miss they experienced
- WHS team provides structure and debrief support, not script

**Short Video Content (60–90 seconds)**
- Phone camera quality is acceptable
- Content: task demonstration, near-miss story, hazard recognition walkthrough
- Script optional — genuine unrehearsed content often more effective
- Production: WHS team provides coaching on content focus, not production quality
- Distribution: toolbox session intro, intranet, safety channel

### Editorial Governance
All frontline content must be reviewed before distribution:
- Factual accuracy (no incorrect procedure or control information)
- Privacy (no identifiable injured workers without consent)
- Legal clearance for any incident content (check with legal if significant injury involved)
- Brand/format compliance (your organisation's template requirements)

## 6. Engagement Measurement

### Activity Metrics (what happened)
- Toolbox sessions delivered vs planned
- Worker attendance count and % of target population reached
- Facilitator participation rate (how many of 200 facilitators delivered all sessions?)
- Collateral distribution count
- Worker-generated content submissions

### Outcome Metrics (what changed)
These are the hard measures — take 4–8 weeks to appear in the data.

| Metric | Data source | Leading or lagging |
|---|---|---|
| Hazard report rate per worker | Incident system | Leading (engagement proxy) |
| Near miss report rate | Incident system | Leading (psychological safety) |
| HiPo rate (critical risk categories covered by program) | Incident system | Lagging |
| Worker-reported awareness of critical controls | Survey | Leading (perception) |
| Facilitator debrief quality scores | Debrief cards / Teams | Process quality |
| CCV completion rate on program-focused critical risks | CRO system | Leading |

### Survey Design for Program Evaluation
Short pulse survey (5–7 questions) at program midpoint and end:
- "This program helped me understand [critical risk topic] better" (agree/disagree)
- "I learned something new about how to keep myself safe at work" (agree/disagree)
- "The toolbox discussions were useful and relevant to my work" (agree/disagree)
- "I have already done something differently based on this program" (open text)
- "What would you change about this program?" (open text)

Deploy via QR code on collateral, Teams, or paper where digital access is limited.
Minimum 30% response rate for meaningful data; aim for 50%+ with incentive.

### Presenting Program ROI to Leadership
Connect program activity to outcome data:
- "Contracts with >80% toolbox completion showed 23% higher hazard report rates in
  the following 6 weeks compared to contracts with <50% completion"
- Frame in terms of HiPo prevention, not activity volume
- Link to critical risk categories to demonstrate targeting logic

## 7. Critical Risk Topic Integration

### Topic Priority Framework
Prioritise critical risk topics based on:
1. HiPo distribution: highest frequency critical risks first
2. CRO alignment gap: critical risks with largest CRO deficit
3. Seasonal or project-specific factors: e.g., summer heat, new contract start
4. Regulatory focus: risks with recent regulator attention or code updates

### Common Critical Risk Topics in AU/NZ Industry
Working at Height, Electrical Safety, Isolation of Stored Energy, Vehicles and Mobile
Plant, Working in Confined Spaces, Hazardous Substances, Manual Handling, Hot Work,
Working Near Water, Structural Collapse, Dropped Objects, Explosives and Flammables,
Working in Proximity to Live Services, Atmospheric Testing, Worker Fatigue.

> For your organisation's specific critical risk taxonomy, see `company.md` Section 8.

### Topic Development Checklist
For each toolbox topic:
- [ ] Defined the specific WAD failure pathway (not just the general hazard)
- [ ] Used a real scenario (organisation incident, industry incident, or realistic composite)
- [ ] 2–3 discussion questions that surface WAD, not test compliance knowledge
- [ ] Identified the key critical controls to reinforce
- [ ] Included a "what we'll check" commitment question
- [ ] Facilitator notes cover common misconceptions and how to address them
- [ ] Reviewed by a relevant technical expert (e.g., electrician for isolation topic)
- [ ] Language and literacy level appropriate for target workforce

## 8. Program Governance

### Program Governance Structure (for large-scale programs)

```
Program Sponsor (EGM / GM Zero Harm)
  ↓ strategic accountability
Program Owner (Zero Harm Specialist / Manager)
  ↓ operational accountability
BU Coordinators (Zero Harm BPs)
  ↓ contract-level delivery
Facilitators (site/contract level)
  ↓ session delivery
Workers
```

### Governance Documents (recommended minimum)
- **Program Charter**: purpose, target population, success metrics, timeline, budget
- **Facilitator Register**: names, contracts, training completion, session delivery records
- **Content Register**: topics, owners, review dates, version control
- **Risk Register**: program-specific risks (facilitator dropout, content errors, low uptake)
- **Lessons Learnt Register**: captured at midpoint and close, actioned for next iteration

### Budget Estimation Framework

| Component | Cost driver | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Content development | WHS team time + design | Allow 4–6 hours per weekly toolbox session |
| Collateral production | Print run + fulfilment | Jigsaw posters: quote based on size and quantity |
| Facilitator training | WHS team time + venue | 1 day per cohort of 20–30 facilitators |
| Tracking system | Dev time if bespoke | Python system: 20–40 hours one-time development |
| Video production | Worker time + minimal editing | Budget for 2–4 hours per video including debrief |
| Recognition/awards | Contract manager awards | Keep modest; symbolic recognition often sufficient |

### Post-Program Review
Conduct a structured post-program review within 4 weeks of completion:
- Compare activity metrics to plan
- Analyse leading indicator trends (hazard reports, near misses) vs pre-program baseline
- Facilitator debrief synthesis: what worked, what didn't
- Worker survey results analysis
- Key decisions for next iteration: topics to change, format improvements, facilitator
  adjustments, collateral changes
Document in your organisation's Lessons Learnt template (see `company.md`).
