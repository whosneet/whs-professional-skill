# Whistleblower Protections — Intersection with WHS

Reference for WHS practitioners on the Australian whistleblower legislative landscape and how it overlays the WHS Act worker duty (s 28). WHS reporting (incidents, hazards, near-misses) functions as everyday whistleblowing — formal whistleblower regimes provide statutory protections that apply when the matter is serious enough to engage the corporate or public sector regimes. WHS managers don't typically own the whistleblower process, but the WHS function is often the first place a serious concern surfaces, and the function must be designed so the protection attaches by default.

## 1. Why This Matters for WHS

Every hazard report, near-miss, and incident notification is, in effect, a worker disclosing information about something they consider unsafe — exactly the function that whistleblower regimes are designed to protect. The WHS Act s 28 worker duty obliges workers to take reasonable care for the health and safety of themselves and others; that duty is reinforced by s 104 (model Act) prohibiting discriminatory or coercive action against workers who exercise WHS rights. The formal whistleblower regimes — Corporations Act Part 9.4AAA, the Cth PID Act, and state PID Acts — sit on top of that, providing statutory protections that elevate the disclosure into a different legal category.

**Why a WHS practitioner needs to understand this:**

- A worker raising a serious WHS concern may simultaneously be an eligible whistleblower under one or more formal regimes — the matter does not need to be labelled "whistleblowing" by the worker for the protections to attach
- The WHS function is frequently the first point of contact for matters that should also enter the whistleblower process — concealment of incidents, falsification of records, officer-level direction to ignore hazards, suppression of HSR reports
- If the WHS reporting system is the only path and it has integrity gaps, the organisation has effectively channelled disclosures into a path with weaker protections than the law makes available
- A worker who reports a serious WHS matter and is subsequently victimised has remedies under both the WHS Act (s 104) and, if the matter qualifies, the Corporations Act (s 1317AD) or applicable PID Act
- Officers and senior managers reading this should understand that the corporate whistleblower regime applies to "danger to the public" — WHS dangers are explicitly within scope and the regime is not a parallel HR process

**The practical design principle**: the WHS reporting framework should capture the whistleblower protection by default. That means the formal whistleblower policy (mandatory under Corporations Act s 1317AI for public and large proprietary companies) should reference the incident management system as an authorised channel, and the incident system should route serious matters through whistleblower-grade confidentiality controls when triggered.

## 2. Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) Part 9.4AAA

Part 9.4AAA is the principal corporate whistleblower regime in Australia. In its current form it commenced **1 July 2019** under the Treasury Laws Amendment (Enhancing Whistleblower Protections) Act 2019, which substantially strengthened the prior 2004 framework. The regime applies to regulated entities — companies under the Corporations Act, ADIs, insurance companies, superannuation entities — and to disclosures about those entities.

### 2.1 Eligible whistleblower (s 1317AAA)

The definition is broad and intentionally so, but it covers **individuals only** — a company or other entity is never itself an eligible whistleblower (s 1317AAA). An eligible whistleblower is an individual who is or has been a:

| Category | Detail |
|---|---|
| Officer | Director, secretary, senior manager, person making or participating in decisions that affect the whole or substantial part of the business |
| Employee | Current or former employee of the regulated entity |
| Contractor | Including individuals supplying goods or services to the entity |
| Supplier | Individuals supplying goods or services to the entity, and employees of those suppliers |
| Associate | An individual who is an associate of the regulated entity (e.g. a director of a related body corporate) |
| Relative | Spouse, child, dependant, or other relative of any of the above |

A worker on a labour-hire contract is an eligible whistleblower in respect of any regulated entity to which they supply services — this includes their direct employer where it is a regulated entity, and can include the host PCBU where the host is itself a regulated entity (eligibility under s 1317AAA attaches to the discloser's relationship with a specific regulated entity, not automatically to every entity in the supply chain). Former workers retain eligibility — there is no time limit after departure.

### 2.2 Disclosable matter (s 1317AA)

A disclosure qualifies for protection if the discloser has reasonable grounds to suspect the information concerns "misconduct, or an improper state of affairs or circumstances" in relation to the regulated entity, or that the entity (or an officer/employee) has engaged in conduct that:

| Limb | Coverage |
|---|---|
| (a) Contravenes Corporations Act, ASIC Act, banking/insurance/super legislation | Direct corporate or financial law breach |
| (b) Constitutes an offence against any Cth law punishable by 12+ months imprisonment | Catches serious criminal conduct generally |
| (c) Represents a danger to the public or the financial system | Explicit inclusion — **WHS dangers fall within this limb** |

The "danger to the public" limb is the operative basis for most WHS-relevant disclosures. The Explanatory Memorandum to the 2019 amendments specifically identified workplace health and safety as a category of danger contemplated by the section. A worker disclosing that an employer is concealing a notifiable incident, that critical safety controls are being bypassed under management direction, or that a fatality has been mischaracterised — all sit within the disclosable matter definition.

**Reasonable grounds to suspect** requires more than mere suspicion or speculation, but the discloser need not be correct. A genuine, evidence-supported belief is sufficient.

**Excluded matters (s 1317AADA)** — personal work-related grievances are not disclosable matters unless they have a significant implication for the entity beyond the discloser. Pure HR complaints (disagreement about leave, performance management, individual interpersonal conflict) sit outside the regime.

### 2.3 Eligible recipient (s 1317AAC)

The disclosure must be made to an eligible recipient for the protection to attach. Eligible recipients are:

| Recipient | Detail |
|---|---|
| Officer of the entity | Director, company secretary, senior manager |
| Senior manager | A person who makes decisions affecting a substantial part of the business |
| Auditor or member of audit team | Internal or external |
| Actuary | For insurance and super entities |
| Person authorised by the entity | Designated whistleblower officers; typically named in the whistleblower policy |
| Regulator | ASIC, APRA, or another Cth authority prescribed |
| Legal practitioner | For the purpose of obtaining legal advice |
| Journalist or parliamentarian | Only as an "emergency disclosure" or "public interest disclosure" — strict pre-conditions in s 1317AAD |

**Practical implication for WHS managers**: a WHS manager who is also a "senior manager" within the company is an eligible recipient. A line supervisor typically is not, unless designated under the whistleblower policy. The policy must make clear which roles in the WHS function are authorised recipients — and those people must understand their obligations from the moment a disclosure is made.

**Anonymous disclosures are protected** — a disclosure made anonymously (or under a pseudonym) qualifies for the same protections if it otherwise meets the requirements. The discloser is never required to identify themselves, at the time of disclosure or later, for the protections to attach.

**Public interest and emergency disclosures (s 1317AAD)** — disclosure to a journalist or parliamentarian is protected only where strict preconditions are met. For a *public interest disclosure*: a prior disclosure must have been made to ASIC, APRA, or a prescribed Commonwealth authority; at least 90 days must have passed; the discloser must have reasonable grounds to believe that no adequate action is being or has been taken and that further disclosure would be in the public interest; and the discloser must first give the original recipient body written notification identifying the earlier disclosure and stating the intention to make a public interest disclosure. For an *emergency disclosure*: a prior disclosure must have been made; the discloser must have reasonable grounds to believe the information concerns a substantial and imminent danger to the health or safety of one or more persons or to the financial system; written notification is again required; and the extent of the information disclosed must be no greater than necessary to inform the recipient of the danger. A worker considering going external should obtain advice first — a disclosure that misses the s 1317AAD preconditions loses the protection.

### 2.4 Protections (ss 1317AAE, 1317AB–AE)

The protections are substantial and automatic — they attach by operation of law once the disclosure meets the requirements, without any formal labelling by the discloser.

| Protection | Section | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality | s 1317AAE | Identity of discloser must not be disclosed (limited exceptions — to ASIC/APRA/AFP, to a legal practitioner, or with consent). A separate exception permits disclosing *information likely to lead to identification* of the discloser where reasonably necessary to investigate the matter, provided reasonable steps are taken to reduce the risk of identification — that exception never permits disclosing the identity itself |
| Immunity from civil/criminal/administrative liability | s 1317AB | The discloser is not subject to civil, criminal, or administrative liability for making the disclosure; not subject to contractual or other remedies on the basis of the disclosure |
| No victimisation | s 1317AC | Causing or threatening detriment to the discloser (or another person on the basis of the disclosure) is prohibited — s 1317AC is both a criminal offence and a civil penalty provision; detriment includes dismissal, demotion, harassment, intimidation, harm, damage to property or reputation, financial loss |
| Contractual termination void | s 1317AB(2) | A contractual term that purports to terminate on the basis of disclosure is void |
| Compensation and remedies | ss 1317AD–1317AE | s 1317AD is the gateway to civil remedies for detriment, with a shifted onus of proof (s 1317AD(2B)); s 1317AE sets out the orders the court may make — compensation, reinstatement, injunctions, exemplary damages, apologies |

The shifted onus in s 1317AD(2B) is significant — once the claimant points to evidence of detriment and a qualifying disclosure, the burden shifts to the defendant to prove the disclosure was not part of the reason for the conduct. This is materially more protective than ordinary civil litigation.

### 2.5 Mandatory whistleblower policy (s 1317AI)

Public companies, large proprietary companies, and corporate trustees of registrable superannuation entities must have a whistleblower policy. Required content includes:

- Protections available to whistleblowers
- To whom disclosures may be made and how
- How the entity will support and protect whistleblowers
- How investigations into disclosures will be conducted
- How fair treatment of employees mentioned in disclosures will be ensured
- How the policy will be made available
- Any matters prescribed by regulations

**Penalty for non-compliance**: 60 penalty units for the entity (s 1317AI(5)) — but the reputational and operational consequence of a non-compliant policy in a litigated whistleblower matter is materially greater.

The policy must be operationally real — it cannot be a static document on the intranet. ASIC's Regulatory Guide 270 sets out expectations including training, escalation pathways, governance oversight, and periodic review.

### 2.6 Penalties for breach (s 1317AC and related)

The same conduct can engage both a criminal offence and a civil penalty provision — keep the two streams distinct.

**Criminal offences:**

| Offence | Maximum penalty (individual) | Maximum penalty (body corporate) |
|---|---|---|
| Victimisation — causing or threatening detriment (s 1317AC) | Up to 2 years imprisonment and/or 240 penalty units ($87,360 at $364/unit) | 2,400 penalty units ($873,600) |
| Breach of discloser confidentiality (s 1317AAE) | Up to 6 months imprisonment and/or 60 penalty units ($21,840) | 600 penalty units ($218,400) |

**Civil penalty provisions:**

| Contravention | Maximum civil penalty (individual) | Maximum civil penalty (body corporate) |
|---|---|---|
| Victimisation (s 1317AC) or confidentiality breach (s 1317AAE) | The greater of: 5,000 penalty units; or 3× the benefit derived or detriment avoided | The greater of: 50,000 penalty units; 3× the benefit derived or detriment avoided; or 10% of annual turnover (capped at 2.5 million penalty units) |
| Failure to have a compliant policy (s 1317AI) | — | 60 penalty units |

The Commonwealth penalty unit is **$364 from 1 July 2026** (it was $330 from 7 November 2024); the value is indexed — take the current value from `assets/penalty_units.json` and validate before quoting dollar figures. At $364 per unit, the corporate civil maximum runs to the tens of millions of dollars for large entities. Criminal liability for individuals (officers, managers, or any person involved) is available where the conduct meets the criminal threshold.

**Case anchor — ASIC v TerraCom Ltd (No 3) [2025] FCA 1017**: the Federal Court ordered TerraCom to pay a $7.5 million civil penalty (plus ASIC's costs) over ASX announcements and an open letter that publicly disparaged a whistleblower's allegations — the first civil penalty imposed for whistleblower victimisation under Part 9.4AAA. The detriment was reputational and the conduct was the public messaging itself; no dismissal was needed to engage the provisions. Validate case details against the judgment before citing in formal outputs.

## 3. Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 (Cth)

The PID Act 2013 (Cth) is the parallel regime for the Commonwealth public sector — Commonwealth agencies, statutory authorities, Commonwealth companies, and contracted service providers performing services on behalf of the Commonwealth. It is administered by the Commonwealth Ombudsman and the Inspector-General of Intelligence and Security.

The Act was substantially amended by the **Public Interest Disclosure Amendment (Review) Act 2023 (Cth)**, with the main changes commencing **1 July 2023** alongside the National Anti-Corruption Commission (NACC). Two changes matter most in practice: *personal work-related conduct* (individual workplace grievances with no broader integrity dimension) is now expressly excluded from disclosable conduct, and the Act now interfaces with the NACC regime — serious or systemic corruption issues flow to the NACC, and agencies must manage PID handling and NACC referral obligations side by side.

### 3.1 Disclosable conduct

The PID Act covers a broader range of conduct than the corporate regime — reflecting the public sector context:

| Category | Coverage |
|---|---|
| Contravention of a law | Cth, State, Territory, or foreign law |
| Corruption | Including bribery, abuse of public office |
| Maladministration | Conduct based on improper motives, unreasonable conduct, unjust conduct |
| Abuse of public trust | Misuse of public office |
| Wastage of public money | Significant inefficiency or financial impropriety |
| Conduct unreasonably endangering H&S | **Of any person** — broad and includes WHS dangers to public sector workers and members of the public |
| Conduct endangering the environment | Including unreasonable environmental damage |
| Conduct involving abuse of office | Specific to public sector |

The "endangering health and safety" limb is the WHS-equivalent provision. Cth public sector WHS practitioners (Comcare-regulated entities) operate within both the WHS Act (Cth) and the PID Act simultaneously.

### 3.2 Structural features

| Feature | PID Act provision |
|---|---|
| Eligible discloser | Public official (current or former) — broad definition including employees, contractors, secondees |
| Authorised internal recipient | Each agency must have a Principal Officer (typically the agency head) and Authorised Officers — mandatory |
| External disclosure | Available where internal disclosure has not been adequately handled (subject to conditions) |
| Emergency disclosure | Available for substantial and imminent danger to life, health, safety, or the environment |
| Protections | Equivalent in structure to Corporations Act — confidentiality, immunity from liability, prohibition on reprisal, remedies |
| Reprisal | Prohibited under s 13–19; criminal and civil consequences |
| Investigation duty | Mandatory — Authorised Officer must allocate the disclosure and the investigator must investigate within statutory timeframes |

The mandatory authorised internal recipient model is a key difference from the corporate regime. Public sector agencies must designate, train, and resource Authorised Officers — the disclosure pathway is built into the agency structure by statute, not designed at the entity's discretion.

### 3.3 Practical implication for WHS

Cth public sector WHS practitioners (Commonwealth Public Service agencies, ADF civilian operations, federal statutory authorities, and Commonwealth companies such as Australia Post) operate under the PID Act regime for public sector WHS matters — Comcare coverage alone does not bring an entity within the PID Act, and privatised Comcare self-insurance licensees sit outside it. A WHS coordinator in a Cth agency receiving a report of a serious safety concealment must understand both their WHS Act obligations and the PID Act pathway — the two operate concurrently, not as alternatives.

## 4. State Public Interest Disclosure Acts

Each Australian state and territory has its own PID Act covering its public sector. The regimes are broadly similar in structure to the Cth PID Act — disclosable conduct, authorised recipients, protections, anti-reprisal provisions — but vary in detail, coverage, and oversight body.

| Jurisdiction | Act | Oversight body | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| NSW | Public Interest Disclosures Act 1994 (replaced by PID Act 2022, commenced 1 October 2023) | NSW Ombudsman + ICAC for corruption | The 2022 Act materially strengthened protections and expanded coverage |
| VIC | Public Interest Disclosures Act 2012 | Independent Broad-based Anti-corruption Commission (IBAC) | Renamed from Protected Disclosure Act 2012; significant reforms in 2019 |
| QLD | Public Interest Disclosure Act 2010 | Queensland Ombudsman + CCC for corruption | Public sector + some private entities providing public services |
| SA | Public Interest Disclosure Act 2018 | Office for Public Integrity (OPI) / ICAC SA + Ombudsman SA | Replaced the Whistleblowers Protection Act 1993 (repealed 1 July 2019); covers environmental and health information disclosed by any person, and public administration information disclosed by public officers |
| WA | Public Interest Disclosure Act 2003 | Public Sector Commissioner | Each public authority designates a PID Officer |
| TAS | Public Interest Disclosures Act 2002 | Tasmanian Ombudsman + Integrity Commission | Covers state public sector + local government |
| ACT | Public Interest Disclosure Act 2012 | ACT Ombudsman + ACT Integrity Commission | Public sector focus |
| NT | Independent Commissioner Against Corruption Act 2017 | ICAC NT | The Public Interest Disclosure Act 2008 was repealed; since 2018 whistleblower protections sit within the ICAC (NT) regime (protected communications and anti-retaliation provisions) |

**Common structural elements across state PID Acts:**

- Eligible discloser is typically a public officer (current or former), and in some jurisdictions extends to members of the public
- Disclosable conduct includes corruption, maladministration, substantial waste of public funds, and conduct endangering health and safety or the environment
- Mandatory authorised recipient structure within each public sector entity
- Strong confidentiality obligations
- Civil and criminal sanctions for reprisal
- Some Acts (notably NSW PID Act 2022) cover private entities providing public services under contract

**Practical implication for WHS**: state public sector WHS practitioners (state health departments, education departments, transport agencies, state-owned corporations) operate under the state PID Act for their jurisdiction in addition to the WHS Act. A WHS adviser in NSW Health, for example, receives the protection of WHS Act s 104 and the NSW PID Act 2022 concurrently for serious safety disclosures. Contracted private operators providing public services (private operators of state-owned facilities, contracted health services, prison operators) often fall within the state PID Act scope by virtue of the contract — not just the Corporations Act regime.

## 5. Intersection with WHS

This is where the WHS practitioner's operational responsibility crystallises. The frameworks above are not academic — they shape how the WHS function should design its reporting infrastructure, train its people, escalate serious matters, and protect workers who raise concerns.

### 5.1 The worker raising a critical WHS concern is potentially an eligible whistleblower under multiple regimes simultaneously

A construction worker who reports to their site supervisor that a labour-hire crew is being directed by management to operate plant without functioning interlocks is:

- A worker exercising their s 28 WHS Act duty
- An eligible whistleblower under Corporations Act Part 9.4AAA where the host is a constitutional corporation / regulated entity — frequently but not always the case in construction, since many construction PCBUs are partnerships, sole traders, or trusts that may fall outside the corporate regime — the disclosure represents "a danger to the public"
- Potentially an eligible discloser under the relevant state PID Act if the project is a state public sector project or contracted public service
- Protected from victimisation under WHS Act s 104, Corporations Act ss 1317AC–1317AE, and the relevant PID Act

The WHS function should not be the only path. A worker who reports through the incident management system is engaging with the WHS framework, but the matter may also need to enter the whistleblower pathway. The two should be designed to interoperate, not compete.

### 5.2 Confidential reporting mechanisms separate from the incident system

The standard incident reporting system is designed for transparency — incidents are investigated, lessons shared, controls strengthened. That works for the vast majority of matters. It does not work for matters that allege concealment, falsification, or officer-level direction to ignore hazards — because the people implicated in the concern may be the people receiving the report.

**Design implication**: the organisation needs a separate, confidential pathway for serious matters that bypasses the line management chain and reaches an eligible recipient (under the whistleblower policy) directly. Common models:

- External whistleblower hotline (independent third-party provider) with case management
- Direct email/phone line to the General Counsel, Chief Risk Officer, or Audit Committee Chair
- Anonymous reporting through a portal with case ID and follow-up mechanism
- Direct access to the entity's Authorised Whistleblower Officer named in the policy

The WHS function should reference these pathways in induction, in the whistleblower policy training, and in incident system documentation — so a worker reporting a hazard understands what their options are if the hazard is symptomatic of something more serious.

### 5.3 Investigator competency and confidentiality requirements

Whistleblower investigations are not WHS investigations. They are typically:

- Conducted by HR, Legal, Risk, or an external investigator — not the WHS function
- Subject to mandatory confidentiality under s 1317AAE (Corporations Act) or equivalent
- Run on a tighter need-to-know basis than a standard ICAM
- Documented in a way that protects the discloser's identity even from internal stakeholders
- Subject to higher evidence preservation standards because of the litigation exposure

A WHS practitioner who receives a disclosure that triggers the whistleblower regime should escalate immediately — not investigate. The protection of the discloser's identity is the first obligation, and an untrained investigator can breach confidentiality in the first hour by simply asking the wrong follow-up question to the wrong person.

### 5.4 When to escalate WHS matters into the whistleblower framework

The following categories of WHS concern should always be escalated into the formal whistleblower pathway, regardless of how they were initially raised:

| Trigger | Why it escalates |
|---|---|
| Suspected concealment of notifiable incidents | s 38 model Act / s 56 HSWA obligation; concealment is a contravention; danger to the public limb engaged |
| Suspected falsification of safety records | Records, audit trails, training records, CCV — falsification engages corporate offence regime |
| Suspected suppression of HSR reports or worker concerns | s 104 victimisation; potentially Corporations Act s 1317AD if regulated entity |
| Suspected officer-level direction to ignore identified hazards | Engages officer due diligence (s 27 model / s 44 HSWA) and disclosable matter limb (c) |
| Suspected deliberate non-compliance with regulator directions | Improvement/prohibition/non-disturbance notices ignored — contravention of WHS Act |
| Fatalities or serious incidents where the initial narrative does not align with available evidence | Risk of cover-up; needs independent escalation |
| Bullying, sexual harassment, or psychosocial misconduct by officers or senior managers | Intersects Respect@Work positive duty (see §5.5) |

The WHS function does not investigate these matters — it triages, protects the worker, preserves the evidence, and escalates through the documented pathway. The pathway should be specified in the WHS procedure suite, with the contact points named (not just role titles).

### 5.5 Relationship to Respect@Work positive duty disclosure pathways

The Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth) s 47C positive duty (effective 13 December 2022, the day after the Anti-Discrimination and Human Rights Legislation Amendment (Respect at Work) Act 2022 received assent on 12 December 2022) imposes a proactive obligation on PCBUs to take reasonable and proactive measures to eliminate sexual harassment, sex-based harassment, sex discrimination, and related victimisation. The AHRC has investigation and enforcement powers from 12 December 2023.

A worker disclosing sexual harassment, sex-based hostile environment, or related victimisation may be:

- Raising a psychosocial hazard under the model WHS Regulations psychosocial provisions (rr 55A–55D)
- Engaging the s 47C positive duty pathway
- An eligible whistleblower under Corporations Act Part 9.4AAA if the conduct involves an officer or systemic management failure
- Protected under state anti-discrimination legislation independently

The reporting infrastructure should be designed so that a single disclosure can engage the relevant pathways without requiring the worker to file in multiple places. A central intake — typically run by HR or Legal with WHS involvement — should triage to the applicable regimes. The WHS function's role is to ensure the WHS framing (hazard identification, risk management, controls) is applied alongside the harassment-specific response, not as an alternative.

Cross-reference: `references/legislation.md` §9 covers the SDA s 47C positive duty, AHRC enforcement powers, and the seven standards for compliance in detail.

### 5.6 Document and record retention

Whistleblower matters typically require longer retention than standard WHS records because:

- Investigations may run for months or years, with subsequent litigation
- Reprisal claims can be brought after the underlying matter is concluded
- Regulatory proceedings (ASIC, AHRC, state PID Commissioners) have their own timeframes
- The shifted onus in s 1317AD(2B) means the entity may need to evidence the absence of reprisal connection years after the disclosure

| Record type | Typical WHS retention | Whistleblower retention |
|---|---|---|
| Incident reports | 7 years post-closure | Same |
| Investigation files | 7 years post-closure | Permanent or until all related proceedings concluded + 7 years |
| HR decisions affecting the discloser | 7 years standard | Permanent or until all proceedings concluded + 7 years |
| Communications about the disclosure | Standard correspondence retention | Restricted access, indefinite retention |
| Identity-protected records | Not typically tracked separately | Separate restricted-access repository |

The records system supporting whistleblower matters should be designed at the start — building it after a matter arises is materially harder.

### 5.7 Why the WHS function needs to know but typically does not own

In most organisations of any scale, the whistleblower policy is owned by Legal, Risk, or HR — not WHS. This is correct: the policy needs to cover all categories of disclosable matter (financial misconduct, fraud, corruption, harassment, governance failures, environmental harm) and the WHS-specific lens is one of many.

What the WHS function contributes:

- **WHS-specific dimension** — the WHS function understands what a danger to the public looks like in the operational context, which other functions may not
- **Operational reach** — the WHS function is often the first point of contact for workers, and the first to see signals that something is wrong (incident pattern shifts, sudden under-reporting, key HSRs going quiet)
- **Investigation rigour** — the WHS function's investigation methodology (ICAM, contributing factors analysis) is often more developed than other parts of the business and can support whistleblower investigators
- **Worker engagement infrastructure** — toolboxes, inductions, hazard reporting platforms — all of these can carry awareness of the whistleblower pathway
- **Officer briefing input** — when officers are briefed on WHS, the whistleblower regime should be referenced in the context of the danger-to-the-public limb and the officer's potential exposure if a serious WHS matter is mishandled

What the WHS function should not do:

- Investigate the whistleblower matter itself — that is the policy owner's role
- Sit between the discloser and the authorised recipient — escalate, don't filter
- Treat the disclosure as a standard incident in the WHS system — it may simultaneously be one, but the whistleblower confidentiality requirements override
- Discuss the disclosure with line management without confirming the policy permits it — early casual conversations are the most common breach of s 1317AAE

The relationship between the WHS function and the policy owner should be documented — who triages, who escalates, who briefs the executive, who briefs the board. This relationship is best established before a disclosure arises, not during one.
