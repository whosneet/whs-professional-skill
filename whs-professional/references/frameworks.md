## 1. Hierarchy of Controls

The hierarchy of controls is a globally adopted framework for prioritising risk
treatments. Higher-order controls remove the risk-generating mechanism; lower-order
controls rely on worker compliance. Many organisations layer additional framing —
e.g. "above the line" (hard controls: elimination through engineering) and
"below the line" (soft controls: administrative and PPE) — see `company.md` for
your organisation's local framing.

| Level | Description | Type |
|---|---|---|
| **Elimination** | Remove the hazard entirely from the workplace | Hard — most preferable |
| **Substitution** | Replace with safer alternative; reduce magnitude of hazard | Hard |
| **Isolation** | Physical separation by distance or barriers; remote operation | Hard |
| **Engineering** | Physical devices or redesign: guards, interlocks, ventilation, hoists | Hard |
| **Administrative** | Procedures, standards, signs, training, work methods, rosters | Soft |
| **PPE** | Specialised clothing/equipment as last resort | Soft — least preferable |

**Critical principle**: Low-level controls (administrative, PPE) must not be the
sole risk treatment where higher-order controls can be implemented. The cost of
higher-order controls must be **grossly disproportionate** to the risk before it
justifies not implementing them — this is the SFAIRP test (see Section 2).

## 2. So Far As Is Reasonably Practicable (SFAIRP)

### Definition
SFAIRP = that which is, or was at a particular time, reasonably able to be done
to ensure safety, taking into account and weighing up all relevant matters
including:

1. **Likelihood** — the more likely a risk will eventuate, the more must be done to eliminate/minimise it
2. **Degree of harm** — greater potential severity requires more to be done
3. **Knowledge** — what the duty holder knows or ought reasonably to know about the hazard and available controls
4. **Availability and suitability** of ways to eliminate or minimise the risk — available if on the open market or reasonably possible to manufacture; suitable if feasible to implement
5. **Cost** — relevant, but with a clear favourability of safety ahead of cost; cost must be **grossly disproportionate** to the risk before it justifies not implementing a control. If a duty holder cannot afford a reasonably practicable control, they should not engage in the activity.

**Key distinction**: SFAIRP (the standard in AU and NZ WHS law) is not the same
as ALARP (As Low As Reasonably Practicable — used in UK and petroleum sectors).
SFAIRP requires weighing all factors and applying the best available controls
unless cost is grossly disproportionate. When advising under AU/NZ WHS frameworks,
use SFAIRP, not ALARP.

- Leadership participation not evidenced (clause 5.1) — documented but not visible

- Corrective action effectiveness not verified (10.2)

## 4. Safety II and New View Safety

**Safety I** (traditional): Safety is the absence of accidents. Improve by counting failures
and removing causes. Response to failure is blame and fix.

**Safety II** (Hollnagel): Safety is the presence of things going right. Work usually succeeds
because workers adapt intelligently. Improve by understanding and amplifying what goes right.

### Core Safety II Principles

**Work-as-Imagined (WAI) vs Work-as-Done (WAD)**
Procedures and plans (WAI) never perfectly match how work actually happens (WAD). The gap
exists because workers constantly adapt to fill in what the procedure doesn't cover.
Investigations and program design must start from WAD, not correct WAD to align with WAI.

**Performance Variability**
Human performance varies — this is not a problem to eliminate but a resource to understand.
Workers adjust and compensate constantly. When this adaptive capacity is overwhelmed or
mis-directed, failures occur.

**Resonance**
Accidents occur when variability from multiple sources resonates — circumstances align such
that no one compensation is sufficient. This is why "human error" explanations are insufficient.

**The FRAM (Functional Resonance Analysis Method)**
A Safety II analysis tool. Maps functions (activities) and their relationships — outputs,
inputs, resources, controls, preconditions, time. Identifies how variability in one function
propagates to others. Use for complex task/system analysis, not standard incident investigations.

### Safety II in Practice

**In investigations**: Alongside ICAM, ask "what did people do right?" and "what allowed
work to succeed most of the time?" Document these as much as the failures.

**In program design**: Build programs that strengthen worker capacity and skill, not just
compliance. Create conditions where workers can surface concerns without consequence.

**In metrics**: Include positive indicators — near miss reporting rates (not just counts),
hazard identification quality, workforce engagement, corrective action closure rates — not
just LTI frequency and severity.

**In audits**: Replace compliance tick-boxes with conversations. Ask workers what they actually
do, not whether they've read the procedure.

## 5. Human and Organisational Performance (HOP)

Developed from human factors research; articulated for safety practitioners by Todd Conklin,
Bob Edwards, and others. Closely aligned with Safety II and resilience engineering.

### The 5 Core HOP Principles

**1. Error is Normal**
People make mistakes — all people, all the time. Error is a characteristic of human
cognition, not a character flaw. Designing systems that assume error-free performance
is itself a system failure.
*Practical application*: Design procedures, environments, and tasks to catch and recover
from errors before they become incidents — not to punish people for errors after the fact.

**2. Blame Fixes Nothing**
Blaming individuals for errors neither prevents recurrence nor improves the system.
It suppresses reporting and makes the next event less visible and more likely.
*Practical application*: Separate accountability (did people act in good faith within
the system?) from blame (who can we punish?). Accountability means understanding what
happened; blame means attributing fault without system change.

**3. Context Drives Behaviour**
Workers don't behave randomly — they respond to the environment, pressures, incentives,
and tools that the organisation provides. If the system creates conditions where unsafe
behaviour is the most expedient choice, workers will make that choice — even knowing it
is unsafe.
*Practical application*: Before introducing a new rule or training, ask "what in the system
makes the undesired behaviour the path of least resistance?"

**4. Learning and Improving is Vital**
Every event — incident, near miss, or successful response to a hazard — is an opportunity
to learn about how the system works. The most important learning happens at the front line.
*Practical application*: Build formal mechanisms (Learning Teams, pre/post job briefings,
storytelling forums) for capturing and sharing front-line learning, not just incident reports.

**5. How Leaders Respond to Events Matters More Than What They Say**
If leadership responds to a serious incident by immediately looking for who to discipline,
the reporting culture is degraded. If they respond with curiosity and a genuine learning
orientation, the culture improves.
*Practical application*: Train leaders to respond to incidents with "help me understand
what happened" before "who is responsible?"

### Learning Teams

A HOP-influenced technique for learning from events (incidents and normal work).
Differs from formal ICAM investigations: smaller, faster, worker-led, curiosity-based.

**Process**:
1. Gather 4–8 frontline workers who were involved or would know the context
2. Facilitator asks questions — no agenda, no finding fault
3. Explore: What were the pressures? What was hard? What didn't the procedure cover?
4. Output: specific system improvement ideas from the workers themselves
5. Commit to action and communicate back quickly

Learning Teams are complementary to ICAM, not a replacement for formal investigation
of notifiable incidents.

## 6. Resilience Engineering

Developed by Erik Hollnagel, David Woods, Nancy Leveson, and others.
Resilience = the ability of a system to adjust its functioning before, during, and after
changes and disturbances so that it can sustain required operations under both expected
and unexpected conditions.

### Four Abilities of a Resilient System

**Respond**: Know what to do — have a plan and the capacity to act when things go wrong
**Monitor**: Know what to look for — understand the leading signals of degrading performance
**Learn**: Know what has happened — capture lessons from events and near-misses effectively
**Anticipate**: Know what to expect — identify threats before they manifest as incidents

### Resilience Engineering in Practice

**System brittleness** (opposite of resilience): A system is brittle when it only works
under expected conditions. Small deviations cause disproportionate failures because the
system has no adaptive capacity. Signs of brittleness:
- Incident rates spike during peak operational periods
- Workers regularly develop undocumented workarounds
- New employees have significantly higher incident rates
- The system performs well in audits but poorly in practice

**Building adaptive capacity**:
- Create slack in schedules — rushed work degrades all controls
- Train workers to recognise early warning signals, not just to follow procedures
- Ensure decision-making authority sits close to where work happens
- Reward raising concerns; never penalise stopping work

**Capacity Framework**:
Safety performance = f(worker capacity, system demands)
When demands exceed capacity → failure is predictable and the system bears responsibility.
Capacity includes: knowledge, skill, tools, time, information, energy, social support.
Demand includes: task complexity, time pressure, concurrent tasks, environmental stressors.

## 7. Critical Risk Management

### What is a Critical Risk?
A hazard scenario with the potential to cause a fatality or catastrophic outcome.
Also called: fatal risk, life-altering injury risk, Tier 1 risk.

Not all serious injuries are critical risks. A critical risk is defined by potential
consequence under credible worst-case conditions — not typical outcomes.

### Critical Controls
Controls specifically designed to prevent or mitigate a critical risk event.
Characteristics:
- If absent, the likelihood of a fatality increases significantly
- There are a limited number for each critical risk scenario (typically 3–8)
- Each can be independently verified at the front line

### Critical Risk Program Structure

**1. Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment**
Identify all credible critical risk scenarios for the operation. Document as a bowtie.
Typical critical risk categories (vary by industry):
- Working at height
- Struck by / caught in moving plant or machinery
- Electrical contact (live electrical work, switchboard, overhead lines)
- Confined space entry
- Explosive or flammable substance — fire, explosion
- Hazardous substances — chemical, asbestos, silica
- Drowning / water immersion
- Structural collapse
- Vehicle / mobile plant collision (worker-machine interface)
- Dropped objects

**2. Critical Control Identification**
For each scenario: identify the minimum set of controls whose failure would make a fatality
or critical injury probable. These are the critical controls.
Document in a Critical Control Register: Risk | Threat/Cause | Critical Control |
Control Owner | Verification Method | Frequency

**3. Critical Control Verification (CCV)**
Regular, structured verification that critical controls are in place and functioning.
Not the same as a general safety audit. CCV is targeted, brief, and front-line.
- Frequency: daily/weekly for highest-consequence controls
- Conducted by: supervisors, workers, and WHS team
- Output: CCV records, trend data, escalation for degraded controls

**4. Reporting and Escalation**
Failed or degraded critical controls must be immediately escalated.
Define the escalation pathway: who is notified, what actions are triggered, what
work is stopped until the control is restored.

**5. Performance Reporting**
Track: number of CCVs completed vs planned; degraded controls found; escalations triggered;
corrective actions closed. Report to management and board as a leading indicator.

## 8. Board and ELT Safety Reporting

### Purpose
Board and ELT safety reports demonstrate governance over safety performance.
Must move beyond lagging metrics to include systems health, program outcomes, and
strategic risk insights.

### Recommended Report Structure

**1. Safety Performance Snapshot**
Headline lagging metrics — TRIFR, LTIFR, AIFR, fatalities, HiPo count.
Include trend (rolling 12 months vs previous year) — not just point-in-time numbers.
Benchmark against industry where available.

**2. High Potential Incidents (HiPo) Intelligence**
Detail on HiPo events for the period: count, type, classification, business unit.
Include: investigation status, root causes emerging, corrective action progress.
HiPos are the most important predictive signals available — give them disproportionate
attention in board reporting.

**3. Critical Risk Status**
Summary of critical control verification activity: completion rates, failed controls,
escalations, corrective actions.
Heatmap format recommended: critical risk × business unit.

**4. Program Performance**
Update on strategic safety programs: status, outcomes, key milestones.
Include both activity data and outcome data (e.g., not just "200 toolboxes delivered"
but "15% increase in hazard reporting in units that participated in the program").

**5. Regulatory and Compliance**
Notifiable incidents reported to regulators in the period.
Active regulatory notices (improvement, prohibition).
Significant legislative changes.

**6. Outlook and Focus Areas**
What the safety team is monitoring as emerging risks or priorities.
What decisions or support is needed from the Board/ELT.

### Principles for Effective Board Reporting
- Lead with risk, not activity — "we're exposed to X" not "we did Y"
- Quantify where possible; qualify where not — avoid purely narrative reports
- Distinguish between systems performance (how well the safety system works) and
  outcome performance (what happened as a result)
- Tell the board when they need to do something — don't bury calls to action
- Use plain language; define all acronyms; avoid WHS jargon with non-specialist directors

## 9. Leading and Lagging Indicators

### Lagging Indicators
Measure outcomes — events that have already occurred.
| Metric | Definition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| TRIFR | Total Recordable Injury Frequency Rate per million hours | (Recordable injuries / hours worked) × 1,000,000 |
| LTIFR | Lost Time Injury Frequency Rate per million hours | (LTIs / hours worked) × 1,000,000 |
| LTISR | LTI Severity Rate | (Lost days / hours worked) × 1,000,000 |
| AIFR | All Injury Frequency Rate | Includes FAIs |
| HiPo rate | HiPo events per million hours | Most predictive lagging metric |
| Fatalities | Count | Non-negotiable in any report |

### Leading Indicators
Measure inputs, activities, and system health — predictors of future performance.
| Category | Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Hazard management | Hazard reports per worker per month | Workforce engagement; reporting culture |
| Hazard management | % hazards closed within due date | System responsiveness |
| Investigation | % incident investigations completed on time | Investigation rigour |
| Investigation | % corrective actions closed on time | Systemic follow-through |
| Critical risk | CCV completion rate vs plan | Critical control management |
| Critical risk | % degraded controls escalated | Control failure visibility |
| Engagement | Toolbox completion rate | Program reach |
| Engagement | Near miss reports per worker | Psychological safety of reporting |
| Audit | WHS audit conformance rate | System compliance |
| Officer | Officer due diligence activities completed | Leadership accountability |

**Caution**: Leading indicators are only valuable if they are genuinely independent from
management gaming. Completion rates are easily inflated. Design indicators that require
substantive engagement to satisfy — not just checkbox activity.

## 10. Worker Engagement and Participation

### Legislative Basis
Duty to consult is legislated (s 47–49 model WHS Act). Not optional. Workers must be
engaged when identifying hazards, assessing risks, making decisions about controls,
changing processes that affect safety.

### Health and Safety Representatives (HSRs)
Workers have the right to elect HSRs. HSRs have statutory powers:
- Right to accompany an inspector
- Right to request a workplace inspection
- Can issue Provisional Improvement Notices (PINs)
- Can direct workers to cease unsafe work (in most jurisdictions)
- Protected from discrimination for exercising HSR functions

PCBUs must facilitate HSR elections if requested and provide HSR training (5 days,
approved course, paid time off).

### Health and Safety Committees
HSCs support the consultation function. Mixed membership: management + workers.
Not a substitute for worker representation — complementary.

### Effective Worker Participation Design Principles
- Engagement must be genuine — not retrospective notification of decisions already made
- Workers closest to the hazard have the most useful knowledge — they are experts in WAD
- Psychological safety is a precondition — workers must be able to report concerns
  without fear of discipline, ridicule, or being labelled a troublemaker
- Engagement at design stage is more effective and cheaper than engagement at corrective action stage
- Frontline worker-generated content (safety alerts, toolbox talks, videos) has
  higher credibility and uptake than top-down communications

### Measuring Engagement Quality
Near miss reporting rate, HSR election participation rate, corrective action suggestion
rates from front line, worker-reported satisfaction with consultation processes.
High completion rates on mandatory training without engagement is not participation —
it is compliance.

## 11. Forge Works Blueprint

The Forge Works Blueprint is a consulting framework developed by Dr David Provan
of Forge Works (Melbourne). It provides a systems-level approach to safety
performance improvement and is most useful where safety programs have plateaued,
where lagging metrics are static despite high compliance activity, or where
program design needs a step-change in approach.

> **Attribution**: This section summarises publicly available concepts from David
> Provan's body of work (Forge Works, the Safety of Work podcast with Drew Rae,
> Griffith University Safety Science Innovation Lab papers, conference talks).
> The summary is intended for practitioner use; refer to <https://forgeworks.com>
> and Provan's published work for the authoritative source.

### Core Principles

**1. Safety I vs Safety II**
- Safety I: safety = absence of accidents; primary strategy is reactive control
- Safety II: safety = presence of successful outcomes; primary strategy is understanding
  and amplifying what works well (WAD)
- In practice: both are needed — Safety I is necessary but not sufficient

**2. Systemic Goal Management**
Safety goals compete with operational goals in the same decision-making environment.
Workers constantly navigate production pressure, time constraints, and safety requirements.
When safety and production goals conflict, workers typically choose production — not because
they don't value safety, but because the system rewards production outcomes.
- Implication: safety program design must address the goal conflict, not just the hazard

**3. Decentralised Decision-Making**
High-performing safety systems push authority to the point of risk. Centralised rule
systems create compliance theatre — workers follow rules when observed, adapt when not.
- Design principle: give workers the tools, authority, and psychological safety to
  make good safety decisions independently
- Practical: shift from "did they follow the rule" to "do they have what they need
  to make the right call"

**4. Resilience Capacity Frameworks**
Four resilience capacities (Hollnagel):
- **Anticipate** — ability to identify future threats and opportunities
- **Monitor** — ability to recognise when a threshold is being approached
- **Respond** — ability to adapt when conditions change
- **Learn** — ability to incorporate lessons from past events (both failures and successes)
Measure these as organisational capacities, not just compliance rates.

**5. Advanced Metrics**
The Forge Works model challenges over-reliance on TRIFR/LTIFR as performance proxies:
- These metrics are rare-event statistics — they fluctuate randomly at small sample sizes
- High TRIFR ≠ unsafe workplace; low TRIFR ≠ safe workplace
- Recommended alternative metric portfolio:
  - Capacity indicators: workforce coverage in CCV, HiPo investigation completion rates
  - Engagement indicators: near miss reporting rate, worker-initiated hazard reports
  - Learning indicators: corrective action quality, lessons applied across sites
  - Alignment indicators: CRO activity vs HiPo distribution gap

**6. Worker Engagement as Intelligence**
Workers performing the work have real-time intelligence about WAD that no procedure,
audit, or dashboard captures. High-performing safety programs have structured mechanisms
to extract, value, and act on this intelligence:
- Regular work observations (not punitive — curiosity-driven)
- After-action reviews that capture what went well, not just what went wrong
- Frontline-generated safety content (alerts, toolbox contributions, video)
- Psychological safety as a precondition — if reporting concerns is risky, the intelligence
  loop is broken

### Applying Forge Works to Zero Harm Programs
When applying Forge Works at division or business-unit level:
- Use HiPo distribution vs critical control verification alignment as the primary
  strategic insight — the gap indicates where attention is needed vs where it's
  currently concentrated
- Frame facilitator toolbox programs as intelligence-gathering, not just message
  delivery
- Design sustained campaign programs to generate leading indicators (hazard
  reports, near misses) rather than only lagging outcomes (injury reduction)
- Board/ELT reporting should reflect system health (capacity and engagement
  indicators) alongside traditional lagging metrics

## 12. Safety Science — Named Thinkers

Many of the concepts in Sections 6–9 trace to identifiable researchers whose work
underpins modern safety practice. Knowing the source of a concept matters when
arguing it to a sceptical board, peer-reviewing a Zero Harm program design, or
demonstrating the evidence base behind a position. The mapping below connects the
concept used in practice to the person whose work established it, so the skill can
cite the source when credibility, audience expectation, or evidentiary rigour
demand it.

### James Reason — Organisational Accidents, Swiss Cheese, GEMS, Just Culture
- **Swiss Cheese Model** (*Human Error*, 1990; *Managing the Risks of Organizational
  Accidents*, 1997) — accidents result from alignment of latent and active failures
  through successive defensive layers. The model remains the default explanatory
  device for most organisational accidents and underpins barrier-based investigation.
- **Latent vs Active Failures** — Reason's distinction is foundational to ICAM:
  active errors are committed at the sharp end; latent conditions originate in
  organisational decisions, design, procedures, training. Latent conditions are
  where the system improvement opportunities lie.
- **GEMS (Generic Error Modelling System)** — skill-based slips and lapses,
  rule-based mistakes, knowledge-based mistakes. Useful in classifying human
  performance failures.
- **Just Culture** (with Marx, Dekker) — distinguishes blameless human error from
  at-risk behaviour from reckless conduct. A practical algorithm for response
  proportionate to the conduct.

### Sidney Dekker — Just Culture, New View, Drift, Restorative Practice
- *The Field Guide to Understanding 'Human Error'* (2014) — the case against
  attributing accidents to human error in itself; "human error" is the symptom
  of trouble deeper in the system. Foundational reading for modern investigators.
- *Drift into Failure* (2011) — slow, incremental departure from safe operating
  envelopes; failure is normal in complex systems undergoing competitive pressure.
  Connects to systemic goal management.
- *The Field Guide to Understanding Human Error* and *Just Culture* — distinguishes
  retributive from restorative responses to incidents. Restorative just culture
  asks "who is hurt, what do they need, whose obligation is it to meet that need?"
- *Safety Differently* (with Provan) — a practitioner-oriented synthesis;
  positions safety as people's ability to make things go well rather than the
  absence of failure.

### Erik Hollnagel — Safety I vs Safety II, FRAM, ETTO, Resilience
- **Safety I vs Safety II** (*From Safety-I to Safety-II*, 2014) — Safety I:
  safety is the absence of accidents; learn from what went wrong. Safety II: safety
  is the presence of successful outcomes; learn from what goes right, which is the
  vast majority of work. Both are needed; over-reliance on Safety I is the
  default failure mode.
- **FRAM (Functional Resonance Analysis Method)** — a method for analysing
  complex socio-technical systems; how functions couple under variation produces
  emergent outcomes.
- **ETTO Principle** (Efficiency-Thoroughness Trade-Off) — workers and
  organisations constantly trade thoroughness for efficiency; the resulting
  variability is what makes both successes and failures possible.
- **Four Resilience Capacities** — anticipate, monitor, respond, learn.
  Cited in Section 9 above.

### Todd Conklin — Human and Organisational Performance (HOP)
- *Pre-Accident Investigations* (2012) — HOP foundation text. Workers are not
  the problem; workers are the solution. Error is normal. Blame fixes nothing.
- **HOP 5 Principles**: (1) error is normal, (2) blame fixes nothing, (3) learning
  and improving is vital, (4) context drives behaviour, (5) how leaders respond
  to failure matters.
- HOP is operational philosophy translated from Safety II / High Reliability
  Organisation theory; the most directly actionable framing for frontline
  supervision and line management adoption.

### Amy Edmondson — Psychological Safety, Teaming, Failing Well
- *The Fearless Organization* (2018) — psychological safety as the belief that
  one will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions,
  concerns or mistakes. Foundational to high-reporting cultures.
- Original research at Harvard demonstrated that higher-performing teams reported
  *more* errors, not fewer — because reporting was safe. Underpins the case
  against punitive responses to error.
- *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023) — distinguishes basic, complex, and intelligent
  failures; argues that intelligent failures are the engine of learning.

### David Provan — Safety Professional Role, Safety Differently
- *Safety Differently* (with Dekker, 2018) — practitioner synthesis of New View
  thinking applied to organisational safety practice.
- PhD thesis (Griffith University) on the role of the safety professional —
  argues against the safety professional as compliance enforcer, in favour of the
  safety professional as adviser, facilitator, learner.
- **Brain Strain** and **The Safety of Work** podcasts (with Drew Rae) — applied
  safety science accessible to practitioners; useful continuing development source.

### Jens Rasmussen — Skills-Rules-Knowledge, Drift, AcciMap
- **Skills-Rules-Knowledge framework** — three levels of cognitive performance;
  underpins Reason's GEMS and modern understanding of error type.
- **Drift towards failure** — Rasmussen (1997) characterised systems as operating
  within a safe envelope bounded by economic, workload, and acceptable performance
  boundaries; under competitive pressure, operation drifts toward the safety
  boundary. Foundational to Dekker's later work.
- **AcciMap** — hierarchical accident causation analysis method; less commonly
  used than ICAM but useful for systemic analyses involving regulators,
  associations, and government.

### Andrew Hopkins — Australian Safety Researcher
- *Failure to Learn* (Texas City, 2008), *Disastrous Decisions* (Deepwater
  Horizon, 2012), *Lessons from Esso's Gas Plant Explosion at Longford* (2000) —
  the strongest available case study material on organisational accidents in
  process industries, written by an Australian sociologist and accessible to
  practitioners.
- Major recurring themes: mindfulness vs collective mindfulness, decentralised
  decision-making, the failure of indicator-based safety management, the role of
  organisational culture in catastrophic accidents.
- Essential reading for AU practitioners; the analytical lens applies directly
  to Australian process industry contexts.

### Karl Weick — High Reliability Organisations, Sensemaking
- *Managing the Unexpected* (with Sutcliffe, 2007) — five characteristics of
  HROs: preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify interpretations,
  sensitivity to operations, commitment to resilience, deference to expertise.
- Sensemaking — how organisations construct meaning from ambiguous information;
  applies to early signal detection (HiPo, near miss) and incident response.

### Using these references in practice
When writing for a board, ELT, or senior client audience, naming the source
elevates an argument from "the safety industry says" to "the foundational research
shows." Use sparingly — one or two named citations per document is typically
enough to establish evidentiary grounding without becoming academic. For internal
training, programs, and facilitator materials, the same names function as a
shorthand for established legitimacy.

## 13. WHS in M&A and Due Diligence

### Why WHS due diligence matters in transactions

WHS exposure travels with the asset. An acquirer inherits:

- Open workers compensation claims and the long-tail of claim run-off liability
  (psychological injury claims and chronic disease claims are the highest-value
  latent exposures)
- Open notifiable incidents and active regulator investigations
- Live improvement, prohibition, or non-disturbance notices
- Ongoing prosecutions or enforceable undertakings
- Industrial manslaughter exposure for events that occurred under prior
  ownership — most jurisdictions have no limitation period for industrial
  manslaughter
- Latent disease exposure (asbestos, silica, chemical) — claims may emerge
  decades post-exposure
- Contingent liabilities from contractor and supplier arrangements

WHS due diligence findings inform deal pricing, warranties and indemnities,
completion conditions, and post-acquisition integration priorities.

### Pre-acquisition WHS assessment scope

| Domain | Source documents to request |
|---|---|
| Notifiable incidents | Incident management system extract (3–5 years); regulator notification register |
| Prosecutions and notices | Court records (publicly available); regulator correspondence; enforceable undertakings register |
| Workers compensation | Premium history and claims experience (3–5 years); high-cost claims register; open psychological injury claims (cross-reference `compensation-rtw.md`) |
| Active regulatory engagement | Inspector visit records; current open notices; regulator correspondence (last 12 months) |
| WHS management system | ISO 45001 certification or audit reports; latest internal audit findings; corrective action register |
| Critical risk management | Critical risk register; CCV records; HiPo investigations |
| Latent disease exposure | Historical exposure to asbestos, silica, chemicals; health surveillance records |
| Officer due diligence | Officer briefing minutes; due diligence records (cross-reference `legislation.md` §6) |
| Contractor and supplier WHS | Contractor management system records; prequalification register |
| Training and competency | Training matrix; HRWL register; current statutory training records (cross-reference `legislation.md` §11) |
| Environmental exposure | EPA notices; contaminated land assessments; environmental licences (cross-reference `environment.md`) |

### Red flags

| Red flag | Implication |
|---|---|
| Open Category 1 or 2 prosecution | Material financial liability; reputational; potential officer exposure |
| Active industrial manslaughter investigation | Highest-tier exposure; deal structure may need carve-out |
| Pattern of HiPos not investigated to closure | Systemic WHS failure; expensive integration |
| Workers comp premium >2× industry rate | Premium impact persists post-acquisition |
| No ISO 45001 / equivalent certification or recent audit | WHS management system maturity unknown; integration cost likely higher |
| High proportion of open vs closed corrective actions | System discipline weakness, not just isolated events |
| Recent senior WHS leader departure | Often indicates underlying issues acquirer hasn't yet surfaced |
| Significant asbestos / silica / chemical exposure history without active health surveillance | Latent disease exposure; potentially uncosted liability |
| Significant volunteer / contractor workforce without integrated WHS arrangements | Hidden duty exposure |
| Significant contaminated land or environmental licence breach history | EHS contingent liability beyond WHS |

### Deal structuring implications

- **Asset deal vs share deal** — asset deals may leave some WHS liability with
  the vendor (e.g., pre-acquisition workers comp claims) depending on
  transaction terms; share deals transfer the entity including all liabilities
- **Warranties and indemnities** — WHS-specific warranties typically cover
  compliance with legislation, absence of undisclosed incidents/notices, and
  accuracy of disclosed documents; indemnities may cover specified known
  matters (e.g., open prosecution)
- **Completion conditions** — material WHS findings may be addressed
  pre-completion (e.g., remediation of an open prohibition notice) or as
  completion adjustments
- **Officer due diligence post-acquisition** — incoming officers inherit the
  duty from the date they become officers; pre-acquisition non-compliance is
  part of the evidentiary picture if a subsequent event occurs

### Integration planning

Post-acquisition WHS integration typically focuses on:

1. **Immediate (Day 1)** — ensure all critical controls remain active during
   the transition; communicate any changes in notification chains; integrate
   incident reporting
2. **30–90 days** — align the acquired entity's WHS management system to the
   acquirer's standards; identify gaps; prioritise high-risk gaps for closure
3. **6–12 months** — integrate critical risk taxonomy and CCV programme; align
   training and competency frameworks; consolidate workers compensation
   arrangements
4. **12–18 months** — integration into the acquirer's governance and assurance
   cadence; close ISO 45001 certification gaps; align officer due diligence
   regime

### Common errors in WHS M&A

- Treating WHS due diligence as a compliance checklist rather than a risk
  assessment
- Relying solely on the data room — site visits, interviews, and inspector
  engagement reveal more than documents
- Underestimating latent disease and psychological injury claim trajectories
- Assuming current WHS culture transfers cleanly under new ownership (it
  rarely does; cultural integration is a 2–3 year programme)
- Inadequate transition planning for critical control accountability during the
  ownership change — accountability gaps in the first 30 days are a documented
  pattern in post-acquisition incidents

### Sector-specific M&A overlays

The general due diligence framework above applies to all acquisitions.
Certain sectors carry heightened WHS exposure that requires sector-specific
attention during due diligence and integration.

#### Mining sector overlay

Additional documents and considerations:
- **Tailings dam liability** — post-Brumadinho (Vale, Brazil, 2019), tailings
  dam integrity is a board-level concern. Request: dam safety reviews,
  geotechnical reports, third-party assessments per the Global Industry
  Standard on Tailings Management (GISTM, 2020)
- **Legacy mine sites** — open shafts, methane accumulation, water ingress,
  abandoned plant; rehabilitation bonds and historic liabilities
- **Statutory roles** — Site Senior Executive (SSE), Underground Mine Manager,
  Ventilation Officer; these positions transfer with the entity; competency
  and currency verification
- **Worker exposure registers** — silica (cross-ref `hazards.md` §2),
  diesel particulate matter, RCS, RCD; cumulative exposure data for
  presumptive claims (cross-ref `compensation-rtw.md` §12)
- **Indigenous land use agreements** — cross-ref `diversity-inclusion.md`
  §1; native title obligations transfer
- **Mining-specific regulator engagement** — Resources Safety NSW, RSHQ
  (QLD), DMIRS (WA); cross-ref `sector-regimes.md` §2

#### Healthcare sector overlay

Additional considerations:
- **Latent claim trajectory** — needlestick injuries, infectious disease
  exposure (COVID, hepatitis, TB); presumptive provisions for healthcare
  workers in some jurisdictions
- **Patient handling MSI** — healthcare has the highest workers comp MSI
  claim cost per FTE in most state schemes; assess the no-lift policy
  status, hoist availability, training compliance
- **Aggression and violence claims** — ED, mental health, aged care
  particularly; trend analysis and program response
- **Regulatory licensing** — radiation, gene technology (OGTR), drugs and
  poisons; licence currency and transfer
- **PC2/PC3 facilities** — registration with relevant regulator;
  certification currency; cross-ref `sector-regimes.md` §5
- **Sentinel events** — coronial findings; open investigations; root cause
  analysis history
- **Aged care intersection with Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission**
  for residential aged care facilities

#### Construction sector overlay

Additional considerations:
- **Open Principal Contractor liability** — projects under acquired entity's
  PC appointment; WHS Management Plan status; HRCW SWMS register currency
- **Subcontractor management arrangements** — prequalification platform
  (Rapid Global, Cm3, Avetta — cross-ref `whs-procurement.md` §2);
  open contracts; performance data
- **Project pipeline review** — projects in tender; recently awarded; mid-
  delivery; close-out — each phase has different WHS risk
- **HRCW register** — the 18 HRCW categories (cross-ref `hazards.md` §8);
  ongoing SWMS compliance; recent regulator inspections
- **Latent disease exposure** — asbestos (pre-2004 buildings), silica
  (post-2024 reduced WES), engineered stone (legacy work post-ban —
  cross-ref `hazards.md` §1); workforce exposure history
- **Construction industry-specific compliance** — General Construction
  Induction (white card) currency, HRWL register, scaffold certification
  (cross-ref `legislation.md` §11)
- **Building certifier and product disclosure** — post-Grenfell context;
  combustible cladding remediation; product compliance for installed
  materials (cross-ref `case-studies.md` Grenfell)

### When this section is most relevant

Pre-acquisition due diligence; post-acquisition integration planning; board
papers on WHS exposure in a proposed transaction; divestment preparation (the
seller has reciprocal interest in clean documentation).
