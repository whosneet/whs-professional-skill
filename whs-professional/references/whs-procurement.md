# WHS in Procurement & Supply Chain

Operational reference for WHS practitioners working at the procurement interface
— supplier selection, prequalification, tender response, contract execution,
ongoing performance management, and supply chain due diligence. Procurement is
where most contractor-related WHS exposure is created, often before a single
worker mobilises. The decisions made here determine what controls are possible
downstream — not just whether they get used.

## 1. WHS in the Procurement Process

### The procurement decision is a WHS decision

Procurement is the most consequential WHS lever the organisation operates that
is not visible in the safety function's day-to-day work. The supplier chosen
determines the hazards introduced to site, the competency of the workforce
performing the work, the quality of the management system applied to the work,
and the residual risk that the principal carries. A poor supplier selection
cannot be fixed downstream — the safety function will spend its time managing
the consequence of a decision it did not make.

WHS belongs as a defined quality criterion in the supplier evaluation matrix,
not as a tick-box gate that confirms minimum compliance. The difference is
material — a tick-box approach treats every prequalified supplier as
interchangeable; a quality-criterion approach distinguishes between suppliers
on the basis of WHS capability, performance, and culture, and gives weighted
preference to the better ones.

### You cannot offload PCBU duty by contract

Model WHS Act s 5 defines the PCBU broadly — a person conducts a business or
undertaking whether or not it is for profit, whether or not it is run alongside
others, and irrespective of the legal structure. The principal who engages a
contractor remains a PCBU in respect of the work and continues to owe duties
under s 19 (primary duty), s 20 (workplace), and the consultation, cooperation,
coordination obligations under s 46 (the "3Cs"). The contractor is also a PCBU
and owes the same duties for its own workers.

Model WHS Act **s 272** voids any contract term that purports to limit,
exclude, or modify a duty under the Act. Indemnity clauses, hold-harmless
provisions, and "the contractor is responsible for all safety" clauses do not
discharge the principal's WHS duty. They allocate commercial risk between the
parties; they do not allocate statutory duty.

The practical implication: the principal cannot rely on contractual transfer of
responsibility as a defence. The duty to consult, cooperate, and coordinate
with other PCBUs holds the principal to a positive obligation to engage with
contractor WHS — not to delegate it. The buyer-side selection and management of
contractors is where this duty is operationally discharged.

> Cross-reference `legislation.md` §4 for overlapping duties and the 3Cs duty
> in detail.

### Procurement stages and WHS touchpoints

| Stage | Description | WHS touchpoint |
|---|---|---|
| **Sourcing** | Identify potential suppliers; market scan; long-list | WHS reputation check; prior incident history; regulator engagement; sector-specific risk profile |
| **Prequalification** | Verify supplier capability and compliance against defined criteria; short-list | Insurance, ISO 45001 or equivalent, prior incidents, regulator notices, financial stability (see §2) |
| **RFP / Tender** | Detailed proposal against scope, price, methodology, quality, WHS | WHS capability statement, methodology, key personnel, performance history, references (see §3) |
| **Evaluation and selection** | Scoring against weighted criteria; recommendation; approval | WHS weighting in evaluation matrix; clarification of WHS responses; site visits to nominated supervision personnel |
| **Contract** | Negotiate terms; finalise scope, schedule, commercials, WHS schedule | WHS schedule appended to contract; HSE Annexure; KPIs; reporting obligations; termination rights on WHS grounds |
| **Mobilisation** | Onboard contractor; induct workforce; verify documents; integrate into management systems | Pre-mobilisation WHS verification; site induction; SWMS review; permit-to-work integration; HSR introductions |
| **Execution and ongoing management** | Routine performance monitoring; intervention as required; periodic review | Scorecards (leading + lagging), inspections, audits, incident reporting, performance forums (see §5) |
| **Variation** | Scope changes, schedule changes, additional sites | WHS implications of variation; re-verification; updated SWMS; revised risk register |
| **Off-boarding** | Demobilisation, final account, lessons captured, records retained | Final WHS performance review; record retention (incidents, training, exposure); latent disease considerations; knowledge handover |

The WHS function's involvement varies by stage — heavier at sourcing,
prequalification, evaluation, mobilisation, and termination; lighter (but not
absent) during routine execution. The biggest failure mode is WHS being
introduced too late — usually at mobilisation, when the supplier has already
been selected and the commercial deal is done. By that point WHS is reactive,
not strategic.

### The buyer-side perspective on contractor WHS

Most WHS reference material is written from the supplier perspective —
prequalifying as a contractor, responding to tenders, demonstrating capability.
The buyer-side view (the principal as PCBU engaging the contractor) is less
documented and operationally more consequential. The questions a buyer-side
WHS function needs to answer:

- Is the supplier's stated WHS capability congruent with their performance?
- Is the supplier's management system actually in use, or is it documented and
  shelved?
- Does the supplier's culture align with what the principal expects to see in
  field execution?
- What are the supplier's WHS strengths and weaknesses, and where does the
  principal need to compensate through its own oversight?
- What happens when the supplier subcontracts — is the chain visible and
  governed?

These are not questions answered by a prequalification certificate. They are
answered by site verification, references checked properly, document review
beyond the surface layer, and engagement with the supplier's WHS leadership.

## 2. Buyer-Side WHS Evaluation

### Prequalification platforms in the AU/NZ market

Several third-party prequalification platforms dominate the AU/NZ contractor
WHS verification market. Each verifies documents against defined criteria and
issues a status indicator (compliant, conditional, non-compliant). They differ
in scope, depth, sector coverage, and cost.

| Platform | Scope | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Rapid Global** | Comprehensive contractor / supplier compliance; widely used in construction, resources, utilities | Verifies WHS, insurance, environmental, modern slavery, training records; modular |
| **Cm3** | Originally OHS-focused; now expanded to broader compliance | Strong in property, retail, FM sectors; assesses WHS systems against ISO 45001-aligned criteria |
| **Avetta** | Global platform; large in resources and energy | Connects principals and contractors; verifies WHS, insurance, financial stability |
| **ISNetworld** | Global platform originating in US; established in AU resources sector | RAVS (Review and Verification Services) verifies safety management programs |
| **Pegasus** | Workforce management + contractor management; common in resources, utilities, infrastructure | Combines individual worker competency (LMS) with company-level prequalification |
| **MyComply / Site Pass / Linksafe** | Workforce-centric (worker inductions, ticket verification) | Used at site level; not a full company prequalification platform |

The platform is the tool — the principal still owns the decision. A "Rapid
compliant" status is not a determination that the contractor is safe to engage;
it is a determination that the documents on file meet the platform's criteria.
The principal's WHS function must still evaluate fit-for-purpose against the
specific scope of work.

### What prequalification platforms verify — and what they do not

| Verified | Not verified |
|---|---|
| WHS Management System exists (document submitted) | Whether the system is implemented and used |
| Public liability and workers compensation insurance currency | Adequacy of insurance limits for the work scope |
| ISO 45001 certification status (if held) | Field practice congruent with certification |
| Self-reported incident statistics (TRIFR, LTIFR) | Accuracy of statistics or under-reporting |
| Regulator enforcement history (self-disclosed) | Pending investigations, near-miss prosecutions |
| Sample SWMS or procedures (where uploaded) | Whether SWMS are followed on site |
| Worker training records and HRWL currency | Worker competency in practice |
| Subcontractor disclosure (if requested) | Subcontractor chain visibility down to tier 3+ |

Prequalification is a necessary but insufficient component of buyer-side WHS
evaluation. It verifies the existence of system documents; it does not verify
the system in use. The gap between documented system and actual practice is
where most contractor-related incidents occur.

### WHS prequalification criteria

Beyond the platform-verified items, the principal's WHS prequalification
criteria should include:

| Criterion | What to evaluate | How to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Insurance | Public liability ($20M+ typical for construction; higher for HRCW); workers compensation in each jurisdiction of work; professional indemnity where design is included; contract works insurance | Certificate of currency; named insured includes principal where appropriate |
| Management system | ISO 45001:2018 certification (third-party) OR demonstrated equivalent management system | Certificate from accredited certification body (JAS-ANZ accredited) — not self-declaration |
| Prior incident history | Fatalities, serious injuries, HiPos, lost time injuries over 3–5 year window; trend direction | Disclosure statement; statutory data (NICNAS, ABS, regulator publications where public); references |
| Regulator engagement | Improvement notices, prohibition notices, prosecutions, EUs, ongoing investigations | Disclosure statement; regulator public register searches |
| Financial stability | Ability to fund WHS investment, training, plant maintenance, insurance premiums | Financial statements (where commercially appropriate); credit check; trading history |
| Workforce competency | Trades, HRWL, ticket currency, supervisor competency | Training matrix; sample of HRWL certificates; LMS extract |
| Subcontractor management | Tier 2+ visibility; flow-down of WHS obligations; subcontractor prequalification | Subcontractor list; subcontractor management procedure; sample subcontractor agreement |
| WHS leadership and culture | Visible commitment from senior leaders; reporting structure for WHS to senior leadership | Organisational chart; WHS reporting line; site visits where practicable |

### Evaluating prequalification at scale

Large portfolios (hundreds of active contractors) create a scaling problem
that defeats individual evaluation. The buyer-side WHS function cannot
manually assess every supplier; it must apply a risk-tiered approach.

**Tier 1 — high-risk / high-value contractors** (typically <20% of supplier
count, >60% of contracted spend, the majority of high-risk work):
- Full prequalification + independent verification (site visit, document deep-dive)
- Annual second-party audit of management system
- Quarterly performance review
- Dedicated WHS oversight from principal

**Tier 2 — moderate-risk contractors** (consultants, mid-size specialty
trades, routine services with elevated hazards):
- Full prequalification via platform
- Sample-based document review
- Half-yearly performance review against scorecard
- WHS function on-call basis

**Tier 3 — low-risk contractors** (office services, professional services,
low-risk maintenance):
- Prequalification via platform; reduced scope
- Annual scorecard review
- Exception-based escalation

Tier assignment is a function of inherent risk (work type) × scale (volume,
duration, workforce size) × consequence exposure. A small specialty
contractor doing one day of confined space work may sit in Tier 1 for that
engagement; the same contractor doing routine inspection sits in Tier 2.

### High-risk procurement categories needing additional buyer-side evaluation

Certain categories warrant additional WHS evaluation beyond standard
prequalification — typically because the work is high-consequence, regulated
separately, or has a track record of catastrophic outcomes.

| Category | Why additional evaluation is needed | What to add |
|---|---|---|
| **Construction (especially HRCW)** | High-fatality sector; principal contractor obligations (model WHS Reg 309); SWMS regime (Reg 299); WHSMP requirement (Reg 309) | WHSMP review; SWMS sample audit; site visit to a current project; verification of HRWL register |
| **Mining services** | Separate regulatory regimes (NSW WHS (Mines and Petroleum Sites) Act, QLD CMSH/MQSH Acts, WA Mines Safety regulations); statutory positions (mine manager, ventilation officer) | Statutory position verification; mine safety management plan review; competency mapping |
| **Hazardous chemicals** | Stored / handled / transported quantities trigger MHF, dangerous goods, environmental, and workplace exposure regulations simultaneously | Dangerous goods licences; carrier accreditation; MSDS/SDS sample review; emergency response capability |
| **Demolition** | High-energy work, hazardous materials (asbestos, lead, PCBs), structural collapse risk; licensing required in most jurisdictions | Demolition licence verification; hazardous materials survey capability; sequence and method statement |
| **Asbestos removal** | Class A (friable) and Class B (non-friable) licensing; air monitoring; clearance certification | Licence class verification; sample air monitoring records; clearance certifier accreditation |
| **Working at heights / rope access** | Falls remain leading cause of construction fatalities; rope access (IRATA) has separate competency framework | IRATA / SPRAT certification; rescue plan capability; sample anchor verification records |
| **Confined space** | High-consequence work; rescue plan must be credible (not just documented) | Rescue plan walkthrough; atmospheric monitoring equipment; trained standby personnel |
| **Electrical (live work)** | Restricted to specific competencies under model WHS Reg 158; arc flash risk; network operator authorisation often required | Electrical licence verification; arc flash assessment capability; live work justification |
| **Major plant operations (crane, EWP, mobile plant)** | Combination of operator competency, plant currency, lift planning competency; consequence of failure is fatal | HRWL register; plant compliance plate records; critical lift planning capability |
| **Labour hire** | Host PCBU and labour hire PCBU both owe duties; supervision and induction quality is often the failure point | Labour hire licence (VIC, QLD, SA, ACT require licensing); worker induction process; supervision model |

### Buyer-side site verification before mobilisation

For Tier 1 contractors, prequalification verifies the system on paper.
Site verification before mobilisation verifies whether the system is real.
The visit should cover:

- **Workforce engagement** — speak with workers (not just management); ask
  about hazard reporting, stop-work authority, training, supervisor support
- **Current site walk** — observe practice in execution, not in induction or
  training; look for SWMS being used, PPE in non-observed conditions, hazard
  reports being raised
- **Document spot-check** — pull a recent incident from the register and trace
  it through to corrective action close-out; pull a recent SWMS and observe
  the task in field; check HRWL currency for the workers present
- **Critical control verification** — pick one critical risk relevant to the
  scope and verify the control in operation (not just documented)
- **WHS leadership** — meet the WHS leader, the project manager, and the
  supervisor; assess capability and authority

A pre-mobilisation visit is not an audit (it is not independent, not against
formal criteria, not reported as audit). It is a verification activity — its
purpose is to confirm that what was represented in prequalification is
operationally true. Findings inform the contracting decision and the
mobilisation plan, not a formal report.

> Cross-reference `inspections-audits-permits.md` for the inspection vs audit
> vs verification distinction.

## 3. WHS in Tender Response (Supplier Side)

### The inverse perspective

The same lens applied to buyer-side evaluation creates the supplier-side
requirement: the tender response must demonstrate genuine WHS capability, not
window-dressing. Buyers who know how to evaluate WHS rigorously can see
through generic responses quickly — and increasingly do, particularly in
construction, resources, and government tendering.

The principle: a tender response should reflect what the organisation
actually does, not what it would like to claim. Overpromising in tender that
cannot be delivered in execution is the most common failure mode and the
fastest route to a contractor being terminated or red-listed.

### Typical tender WHS sections

A WHS tender response typically covers:

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| **Capability statement** | Organisation overview; WHS leadership structure; scope of certifications; sectors and projects of relevance |
| **WHS Management System** | ISO 45001 status; system structure (policy, procedures, registers); how the system applies to the specific scope |
| **Methodology** | How the work will be done safely; key hazards; controls; SWMS framework; specific procedures applicable |
| **Key personnel** | CVs of nominated project manager, supervisor, WHS lead; competencies, experience, retention plan |
| **Performance history** | TRIFR, LTIFR, fatalities, prosecutions over defined period; trend; comparable project experience |
| **References and case studies** | Project examples with permission; client contacts; specific WHS outcomes |
| **Subcontractor management** | How subcontractors will be selected, prequalified, managed; flow-down of obligations |
| **Innovation / continual improvement** | What the supplier is doing beyond compliance; HOP, Safety II, named programs |

### Disclosure of safety performance data

This is the most strategically nuanced section of a tender response. Safety
performance data is both a compliance disclosure (mandatory in many tenders)
and a competitive positioning element (selectively presented).

**What must be disclosed** (where the tender requires it):
- TRIFR, LTIFR, AIFR — typically rolling 12 months and prior periods
- Fatalities — date, location, summary, corrective actions
- Prosecutions and enforceable undertakings — disclosure is typical, with
  outcomes and remediation
- Notifiable incidents over defined period
- Workers compensation premium and trend (some industries; less common)

**What to contextualise** (how the data is framed):
- Trend matters more than point-in-time — improvement in TRIFR over 3 years is
  more compelling than a low point-in-time figure with no history
- Comparable scope is critical — TRIFR for a labour-intensive construction
  project is structurally different from a maintenance contract or a head
  office; tenders sometimes evaluate apples-to-oranges
- Definition matters — TRIFR calculation differs across organisations
  (AS 1885.1 base, hours included, recordability criteria); state the
  definition used
- HiPo disclosure is mixed — some tenders ask, some don't; volunteering it
  signals maturity (you are looking for them) but creates comparability
  challenges with suppliers who do not report them

**What not to do**:
- Do not undercount — if the buyer later discovers an underreported fatality
  or serious injury, the relationship and the contract are at risk
- Do not over-cite headline figures without context — "zero LTIs in 12 months"
  for an organisation that doesn't typically have LTIs is not meaningful
- Do not omit prosecutions — these are public; omission is treated as
  dishonesty

### The difference between mandatory disclosure and competitive positioning

| Disclosure | Competitive positioning |
|---|---|
| Tender asks for TRIFR over 3 years | Supplier provides 5 years with trend analysis |
| Tender asks if there have been prosecutions | Supplier discloses, contextualises, and describes remediation |
| Tender asks for ISO 45001 status | Supplier explains certification body, scope, audit findings closed |
| Tender asks for SWMS samples | Supplier provides samples + describes how they are used in practice |
| Tender asks about subcontractor management | Supplier describes flow-down + provides subcontractor scorecard sample |

The competitive positioning is to demonstrate operational depth that goes
beyond the literal question. Buyers who know what to look for distinguish
between suppliers on this dimension.

### ISO 45001 certification — ticket of admission vs differentiator

ISO 45001:2018 certification is now table-stakes in most AU/NZ procurement
for moderate-to-high-risk work. The presence of certification is necessary;
it is not sufficient. The differentiator is what the certification reveals
about the system:

- **Scope** — does the certification cover the specific work, sites,
  jurisdictions? A certified system for office operations does not certify
  field construction operations
- **Certification body** — JAS-ANZ accredited bodies (BSI, SAI Global, BV,
  DNV, TÜV) carry weight; non-accredited certifications do not
- **Audit findings** — the surveillance audit report (with non-conformities,
  observations, OFIs) tells the buyer what the system actually looks like;
  selective sharing of findings (especially closed Major NCs and the
  remediation) demonstrates transparency
- **Surveillance vs recertification timing** — the system has been audited
  recently, with findings closed, and is on a credible recertification path

The mature tender response treats ISO 45001 as the foundation and then
describes what sits on top of it — critical control verification, HOP
adoption, Safety II thinking, named programs, leadership engagement.

> Cross-reference `frameworks.md` §3 for the ISO 45001 clause map.

### References and case studies (with permission)

The most credible part of a tender response is verifiable third-party
endorsement. Buyers will increasingly call references rather than rely on
written submissions. The supplier's WHS function should:

- Maintain a register of project references with permission to nominate
- Coach reference contacts on what is likely to be asked
- Provide reference contacts with a one-page summary of the work delivered
- Treat each reference call as a relationship — the contact is doing the
  supplier a favour

Case studies in the written submission should be specific (named project,
scope, dates, outcomes), supported by data where appropriate (incident
metrics, hazard reports, audit findings), and connected to the scope of the
current tender (relevance, not just impressiveness).

## 4. Modern Slavery Due Diligence Overlap

### Modern Slavery Act 2018 (Cth)

The Modern Slavery Act 2018 (Cth) requires entities with consolidated revenue
of AUD $100M+ to publish an annual Modern Slavery Statement describing the
risks of modern slavery in their operations and supply chains, and the
actions taken to assess and address those risks.

"Modern slavery" includes trafficking in persons, slavery, servitude, forced
labour, debt bondage, forced marriage, deceptive recruiting for labour or
services, and the worst forms of child labour. The Act creates a reporting
obligation, not a prohibition — but increasing regulator and stakeholder
scrutiny is moving toward effective due diligence, not just disclosure.

The NSW Modern Slavery Act 2018 (state) operates alongside; the Commonwealth
Act is the dominant framework. The Australian Modern Slavery Statement
Register is publicly searchable.

**Recent direction**: the McMillan Review (2023) of the Cth Act recommended
strengthening from a reporting obligation toward a positive due diligence
duty (similar to AU's Respect@Work positive duty and the EU Corporate
Sustainability Due Diligence Directive). Legislative response is in progress
as at 2025; practitioners should monitor.

### Intersection with WHS contractor management

The Modern Slavery framework operates on supply chains; the WHS framework
operates on contractor management. They overlap substantially:

- Both require visibility down through subcontractor tiers
- Both apply a risk-based prioritisation approach
- Both depend on credible verification, not just self-attestation
- Both intersect with worker rights, working conditions, and workforce
  composition

The WHS contractor management framework is often the operational vehicle for
modern slavery due diligence — particularly the on-site verification, the
worker engagement, and the subcontractor scrutiny. WHS practitioners are
frequently asked to contribute to modern slavery risk assessment because
they are the ones with workforce visibility.

### Risk-based prioritisation

Both modern slavery and WHS contractor management apply risk-based
prioritisation. The factors are similar:

| Risk factor | Modern slavery relevance | WHS relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Geography** | Source country with high prevalence (cross-reference Walk Free Global Slavery Index); subcontractor location | Regulatory regime variation; enforcement capacity; cultural norms |
| **Sector** | Cleaning, agriculture, construction, manufacturing, electronics, garment, hospitality — recognised high-risk sectors | Construction, resources, transport, manufacturing — high-fatality sectors |
| **Workforce type** | Migrant labour, temporary visa workers, contractor-of-contractor arrangements, isolated work | Labour hire, casual workforce, contractor chains, language barriers |
| **Procurement characteristics** | Long supply chains, complex subcontracting, lowest-cost bidding, opaque sourcing | Same factors; lowest-cost bidding tends to compress WHS investment |
| **Workforce visibility** | Difficulty in observing worker conditions; remote sites; subcontractor-controlled access | Same — if you can't see the workers, you can't verify their working conditions |

### How the WHS function typically contributes

WHS practitioners are typically asked to contribute to modern slavery due
diligence in the following ways:

- **Incident data review** — WHS incidents involving migrant workers, language
  barriers, induction failures, or unusual workforce arrangements can be
  modern slavery indicators
- **Site verification** — pre-mobilisation visits and routine inspections
  generate workforce observations relevant to modern slavery (housing
  conditions, identity documents, wage payment, freedom of movement)
- **Subcontractor visibility** — WHS prequalification and contractor management
  typically have the deepest visibility into subcontractor chains in the
  organisation
- **Worker engagement** — WHS function relationships with frontline workers
  are often the most credible channel for raising concerns
- **Training and awareness** — WHS induction is often the only formal contact
  point with contractor workers and can carry modern slavery awareness content

The WHS function does not own modern slavery compliance — that typically
sits with procurement, legal, or ESG functions. But the WHS function is
operationally essential to the due diligence process.

### The integrated supplier review

Mature organisations integrate financial, WHS, modern slavery, and ESG
review into a single supplier evaluation process — rather than running them
as parallel and disconnected workstreams. This is more efficient and surfaces
correlation between domains (a financially distressed supplier is more
likely to compress WHS investment and may be more vulnerable to modern
slavery risk).

| Domain | Lead function | WHS contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Financial / commercial | Procurement / Finance | Operational context on supplier health from WHS observation |
| WHS / safety | WHS function | Lead role |
| Modern slavery | Procurement / Legal / ESG | Workforce observation, subcontractor visibility |
| Environmental | Environment function | Cross-coverage where EHS integrated |
| Quality | Quality function | Cross-coverage where integrated management systems |
| Ethics / compliance | Legal / Compliance | Conflict of interest, bribery, regulatory exposure |
| Cyber / data | IT security | Contractor access to systems and data |

A single integrated supplier scorecard with weighted contribution from each
function is the design pattern. WHS typically contributes 20–40% of the
total score depending on the work category.

## 5. Ongoing Contractor WHS Performance Management

### Beyond the initial contract

Procurement attention typically concentrates at sourcing, prequalification,
and contract execution — and then attention falls away during the routine
delivery phase. The WHS function must operate against this default. The
period after contract execution is when most WHS exposure crystallises;
ongoing performance management is the mechanism by which the buyer-side
duty to consult, cooperate, and coordinate is operationally discharged.

### Performance management cadence

Cadence is a function of tier (see §2), risk profile, and the contract term.

| Tier | Routine cadence | Triggered review | Annual |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 (high-risk / high-value) | Monthly scorecard; quarterly performance review with supplier WHS leadership | Any HiPo, fatality, serious injury, EU, prosecution | Annual contractor WHS audit + relationship review |
| Tier 2 (moderate-risk) | Quarterly scorecard | Triggered as above | Annual scorecard review |
| Tier 3 (low-risk) | Annual scorecard | Triggered as above | Annual scorecard review |

The cadence runs in parallel with commercial performance review, not as a
separate workstream — the commercial conversation and the WHS conversation
are connected, and the supplier needs to hear both as part of the same
relationship.

### Contractor scorecards — leading + lagging indicators

A contractor scorecard is the standard mechanism for ongoing performance
visibility. Critically — the scorecard must include leading indicators, not
just lagging. A scorecard with only lagging metrics (TRIFR, LTIFR) tells the
buyer what happened months ago; a scorecard with leading metrics tells the
buyer what is happening now.

**Lagging indicators**:
- TRIFR, LTIFR, AIFR — rolling 12 months and current period
- Fatalities, serious injuries, HiPos
- Notifiable incidents
- Workers compensation claims, premium impact
- Prosecutions, enforceable undertakings, regulator notices
- Corrective action close-out rate and age

**Leading indicators**:
- Hazard report rate per worker (engagement proxy)
- Critical Control Verification completion vs plan
- SWMS quality (audit findings)
- Inspection close-out rate
- Training currency (HRWL, white card, role-specific)
- Toolbox talk delivery and attendance
- Safety leadership engagement (felt leadership walks, executive visits)
- Worker engagement survey results
- Subcontractor management indicators (prequalification currency, audit findings)

The scorecard structure should distinguish these clearly, weight them
deliberately, and present trend (not point-in-time only). A scorecard
showing 6-month trend in both leading and lagging indicators reveals more
than any single-month snapshot.

### Intervention triggers

The scorecard generates routine visibility. Intervention triggers are
defined thresholds that move the relationship from routine management to
active intervention. Triggers should be defined in the contract, not
introduced ad hoc.

| Trigger | Response |
|---|---|
| Fatality on any of the supplier's work (any location) | Immediate notification; full incident review; principal participation in investigation; stand-down assessment for principal's work |
| Notifiable incident on principal's work | Joint investigation; pause work on the affected site; full root cause + systemic review |
| HiPo trend deterioration (rate per worker, or systemic theme) | Targeted audit; supplier WHS leadership engagement; corrective action plan |
| Failed audit finding (Major NC unaddressed) | Formal performance management; conditional contract status; re-audit |
| Regulator notice (improvement, prohibition, EU) | Disclosure to principal; joint review of corrective actions |
| Worker complaint received via independent channel (regulator, union, hotline) | Confidential review; on-site verification; supplier engagement |
| Critical control verification failure | Stop work; root cause; verification before resumption |
| Sustained underperformance against scorecard (3+ periods) | Performance improvement plan; contractual remediation; reduced scope; ultimately termination |

### The contractor management forum

For organisations with multiple major contractors operating across sites,
the contractor management forum is the standard governance mechanism. It is
a joint WHS forum bringing together:

- Principal's WHS leadership
- Major contractor WHS leaders
- Operational stakeholders (project managers, site managers)
- Optionally: HSRs, regulator (for very major operations)

The forum's purpose:

- Share lessons learnt across contractors
- Coordinate on common hazards (e.g., shared site, interface work)
- Discuss systemic themes emerging from scorecards
- Drive consistent standards (PPE, training, permit-to-work, critical risk)
- Provide collective intelligence on regulator focus, industry incidents,
  emerging issues

The forum is most effective when it operates as a peer mechanism (not a
principal-down briefing); contractors share their own intelligence and
challenges. Confidentiality conventions apply — what is shared in the forum
stays in the forum unless agreed otherwise.

Cadence is typically quarterly. The forum is not a replacement for one-to-one
contractor performance management — it is additional.

### Off-boarding considerations

Contractor off-boarding receives the least WHS attention and creates several
specific risks that the WHS function must manage.

**Knowledge handover**:
- Site-specific risks identified by the outgoing contractor
- Open hazards, near-miss observations, unresolved issues
- Lessons from incidents during the contract
- Relationships and tacit knowledge with the principal's workforce
- Subcontractor-specific risks

If the work transitions to a new contractor or in-house team, this knowledge
must transfer. A formal off-boarding meeting with the WHS function in
attendance is the standard mechanism.

**Retention of records**:
- Incident records (minimum 7 years; longer for HRCW, asbestos exposure,
  workers compensation claims)
- Training records and HRWL currency
- Worker exposure records (silica, asbestos, lead, noise) — long-term
  retention obligations (typically 30+ years for occupational exposure data)
- SWMS, risk assessments, audit reports
- Worker compensation claims and RTW records

Retention obligations sit with both parties. The contract should specify
who retains what; the WHS function should verify retention before final
sign-off.

**Latent disease exposure liability**:
- Asbestos exposure, silica exposure, noise-induced hearing loss, hazardous
  chemical exposure may not manifest as injury or disease for decades
- Liability for exposure during the contract period typically remains with
  the contractor who employed the worker — but the principal may be jointly
  liable as concurrent PCBU
- Worker exposure records are the evidence base for any subsequent claim;
  loss of records favours the claimant
- The contract off-boarding is the last opportunity to verify exposure
  records have been captured and retained

> Cross-reference `compensation-rtw.md` for latent disease claims, dust
> diseases tribunals, and the long-tail liability profile.

### Contractor termination on WHS grounds

Termination of a contractor on WHS grounds is rare but consequential. The
considerations:

**Legal**:
- Contractual basis for termination — the WHS schedule and termination
  clauses must support the action
- Performance management process — usually a formal PIP, conditional period,
  and right to remediate must have been observed unless the conduct is
  egregious (gross negligence, repeated serious incidents)
- Notice provisions and consequential costs
- Risk of dispute, claim, or counterclaim

**Reputational**:
- The contractor may be on a regulator-published register (Rapid, Cm3,
  Avetta status visible to other principals)
- Industry intelligence networks share termination information informally
- Worker movement between contractors may carry the narrative forward
- Defamation risk if the basis for termination is communicated externally
  without care

**Practical**:
- Demobilisation logistics — exiting work scope safely, transferring open
  issues
- Risk of degraded performance during the notice period (the "lame duck"
  problem)
- Replacement timing and the consequences of a gap in coverage

**Documentation**:
- The decision to terminate must be evidenced — performance data, formal
  notices, supplier responses, escalation history
- Legal review before termination is communicated
- The internal record stands up to subsequent regulator or court scrutiny

Termination on WHS grounds is a decision that the WHS function recommends
but does not own — the commercial owner of the contract makes the decision
with legal advice. The WHS function's role is to present the evidence and
support the rationale.

> Cross-reference `inspections-audits-permits.md` §3 for the audit
> methodology that often generates the evidentiary base for termination on
> WHS grounds.

## 6. Output Checklist

Before finalising any procurement / contractor WHS output, confirm:

### Buyer-side evaluation
- [ ] Risk-tier assignment matched to inherent risk × scale × consequence
- [ ] Prequalification platform status is necessary but not sufficient — system in use verified, not just documents
- [ ] Insurance limits proportionate to scope
- [ ] ISO 45001 certification scope covers the actual work
- [ ] High-risk categories (HRCW, mining, hazardous chemicals, demolition, asbestos, height, confined space, electrical, major plant, labour hire) have category-specific evaluation
- [ ] Pre-mobilisation site verification scheduled for Tier 1
- [ ] s 272 contractual transfer language reviewed — duty cannot be offloaded

### Tender response (supplier side)
- [ ] Tender response reflects actual organisational capability, not aspirational
- [ ] Safety performance data with trend, not just point-in-time
- [ ] Prosecutions and EUs disclosed honestly with remediation context
- [ ] ISO 45001 certification scope clearly stated; surveillance audit findings discussed where appropriate
- [ ] References approved with permission and briefed on likely questions
- [ ] Case studies specific, dated, supported by data

### Modern slavery overlap
- [ ] Risk-based prioritisation applied (geography, sector, workforce type)
- [ ] WHS contribution to modern slavery due diligence documented
- [ ] Subcontractor visibility to credible depth
- [ ] Integrated supplier review where in place

### Ongoing performance management
- [ ] Scorecard includes leading + lagging indicators
- [ ] Cadence proportionate to tier
- [ ] Intervention triggers defined in contract, not introduced ad hoc
- [ ] Contractor management forum operates as peer mechanism
- [ ] Off-boarding includes knowledge handover, record retention verification, exposure record capture
- [ ] Termination on WHS grounds supported by documented performance management process

### Cross-cutting
- [ ] PCBU duty not offloaded by contract language
- [ ] 3Cs duty (consult, cooperate, coordinate) operationally discharged
- [ ] WHS function involvement starts at sourcing, not at mobilisation
